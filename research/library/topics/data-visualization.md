# data-visualization

- **Never encode a data category by color alone.** Pair series colors with direct labels, shapes, patterns or line styles, choose colors that stay distinct in grayscale, and provide the values in a table for readers who cannot see the chart; a color-coded result also needs its figures and a text alternative. Confidence: high. Sources: B18 p.154-155; B86 p.436-438; B06 p.160-161; B91 p.33, 59; B80 p.111, 117
- **Pick the display by how many numbers there are.** Three numbers or fewer belong in a sentence, four to about twenty in a table, and more than twenty in a graph. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.291; B64 p.167
- **Choose the chart by the question being asked.** Lines show change over time, vertical bars compare amounts, histograms and density plots show spread, box plots show outliers, scatter plots show the relation of two variables and percentiles show range; use area or size for quantity only with care. Confidence: high. Sources: B64 p.167; B61 p.448-449; B86 p.99; B40 p.66-67
- **Never truncate a bar axis or draw 3D bars.** Start bar scales at zero or mark the break visibly; truncated scales and 3D columns mislead readers. Confidence: high. Sources: B02 p.416, 422-423; B46 p.297-298; B24 p.211, 369-372
- **Strip chart junk.** Drop 3D effects, decorative backgrounds and heavy gridlines; keep only the marks a reader needs, with light gridlines if any. Confidence: high. Sources: B24 p.119, 211, 369-372; B02 p.422-423; B86 p.99
- **Give every chart a title that states its point, labeled axes with units, and a legend.** A pretty chart without context or with arbitrary colors implies relationships that do not exist; summarize the takeaway in one sentence near the chart. Confidence: high. Sources: B18 p.154-155; B24 p.210-211; B76 p.116
- **Encode ordered data with lightness, not hue.** Value is read before hue, so sequential data takes one hue stepping light to dark; hue alone separates categories only when hues are far apart and also differ in lightness. Confidence: high. Sources: B76 p.116; B90 p.88-90; B46 p.733-734
- **Keep a sequential scale to six or seven steps.** Readers rank gray or lightness steps quickly up to about six or seven, then confuse them; bin the data rather than adding steps. Confidence: medium. Sources: B76 p.116-118; B68 p.48; B86 p.487
- **Limit series per chart.** Keep line graphs to four or five lines, bar charts to about ten to twelve bars and pies to five slices; past that, use small multiples or group the remainder. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.302, 306-307, 310-311; B91 p.33
- **Separate categorical colors as far as the palette allows.** Neighboring hues such as red, orange and red-orange are hard to tell apart; maximize hue and lightness distance and add a legend sorted by value. Confidence: medium. Sources: B76 p.116, 118
- **Make data colors contrast with the chart background.** On light grounds use saturated but not glaring data colors; on dark grounds lighten data colors and keep them well separated. Confidence: medium. Sources: B76 p.118; B91 p.263
- **Choose data colors for legibility first, brand second.** Brand palettes rarely suit maps or data; start from a legible, natural palette and fold brand colors in only where they do not hurt reading. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.88, 120
- **Know which job each color does: label, measure, represent or decorate.** Serious data needs careful color and avoiding harm comes first; never let decoration masquerade as encoding. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.120
- **Use preattentive variables for what must pop out.** Color, size, orientation and alignment are found in constant time; use one of them for the emphasis and add a second (color plus shape) to separate groups. Confidence: medium. Sources: B86 p.436-438
- **Follow overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand.** Open a data view on the whole scope so people orient themselves, let them narrow, then open single items. Confidence: high. Sources: B21 p.89, 292-293; B02 p.416, 422-423
- **Use nice scale intervals.** Step axes in 1, 2, 5 or 10 units, keep tick labels to two significant digits where possible, and start at zero or flag the break. Confidence: low. Sources: B46 p.297-298
- **Keep bar gaps narrower than the bars.** A gap of at most half the bar width keeps bars reading as one comparable series. Confidence: low. Sources: B46 p.306-307
- **Order pie slices and keep them large.** If a pie is used at all, use at most five slices, each at least 5 percent (18 degrees), start the largest at twelve o'clock and label each with its value. Confidence: low. Sources: B46 p.310-311; B86 p.99
- **Right-align numbers, left-align text, and keep columns strict.** If vertical rules are dropped, alignment carries the columns, and gridlines stay light. Confidence: medium. Sources: B24 p.119; B23 p.576, 583
- **Set figures in tabular lining numerals.** Tabular digits share one width so columns align; proportional figures suit running text, and oldstyle figures clash with capitals in tables. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.74-75
- **Stripe or chunk long tables.** Alternate row shading tested best for reading across rows; without it, add space after every fifth row once a table passes about seven rows, and split tables instead of cramming. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.245-246; B23 p.576, 583
- **Show one unit at a time and name it in the header.** Put the unit in the column header with a toggle for alternatives, and use real table markup. Confidence: low. Sources: B23 p.576, 583
- **Show quantities in a meaningful frame.** A share or a comparison (22 percent free) means more than a raw exact count. Confidence: low. Sources: B26 p.289
- **Never hide the comparison baseline.** Showing a result without its control, prior period or reference crosses into misleading persuasion. Confidence: low. Sources: B40 p.68
- **Do not fake precision.** An emotion curve or a smooth line implies measurement that may be intuition; label, layer or band uncertain data instead of drawing it as exact. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.180-182; B30 p.283-287
- **Show uncertainty explicitly.** Report benchmark figures with 95 percent intervals, outcomes as stacked bars of success, failure and abandonment, and times as histograms summarized by the geometric mean. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.283-287
- **Draw forecasts as a visually distinct continuation.** Plot actuals solid, the forecast dashed after a vertical now marker, with a confidence band that widens with the horizon; for aggregate daily totals use bars, forecast bars lighter than actual ones. Confidence: medium. Sources: B12 p.268-273, 281-283
- **Reject a fitted curve that predicts the impossible.** Several models can fit the same points; check forecasts against physical limits and ask whether overshoot or undershoot costs more. Confidence: low. Sources: B12 p.273-281
- **Layer just enough data to reveal causes.** A few overlays on one base map or chart can show cause; more facets bury it. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.343-345, 350
- **Add a distribution preview to range filters.** A histogram or sparkline of item counts behind a range slider shows where items are and prevents empty ranges. Confidence: low. Sources: B04 p.166-168
- **Keep the meaning of threshold colors fixed across charts.** If low is red and high is green in one chart, keep that order everywhere, and pair the colors with labels or icons (see the red-green rule in color). Confidence: low. Sources: B24 p.238, 241
- **Limit highlight colors to a small conventional set.** Highlights work when there are few of them and they mark without hiding the underlying content. Confidence: low. Sources: B37 p.132
- **Label what a people-metric does and does not measure.** Visual encodings of scores about people (small dim marks for low scorers) are read as verdicts; state the metric's limits beside it. Confidence: low. Sources: B56 p.110-111
- **Expect unfamiliar encodings to be misread.** Even charts of 20 to 100 items are misread when the encoding is new to the audience; tailor the form to their visual literacy. Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.416, 418-420
- **Keep deliverable maps and diagrams short, readable and split when large.** Check contrast and type size, avoid jargon, and break an oversized map into readable parts. Confidence: low. Sources: B67 p.67-68; B64 p.187
