# prototyping

Input rows: 131. Merged rows: 47.

## Purpose and scope

- **Before building any prototype, decide which single question it exists to answer.** One prototype checks one assumption, such as whether people find a feature, grasp a label or finish a flow; assumptions that have nothing to do with each other get their own prototypes, and the feedback serves learning rather than validation. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.17, 56, 106; B72 p.27, 119-120; B34 p.101, 104; B71 p.30, 136
- **Start with the assumption that would sink the product if it were wrong.** Order assumptions by whether a false one means the product fails, balance the value of testing each against the effort it takes, and begin paper testing with the flows that carry the most risk. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.87, 89; B28 p.337-339
- **Make only what is needed to settle the next open question, and raise fidelity as evidence accumulates.** Many MVPs require no code at all: a handful of slides put in front of target users, or a storefront run by hand, can prove demand before anything is built. While the core value remains uncertain, stop polishing components. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.101-104; B06 p.181-184; B30 p.159-164; B72 p.115-116
- **Keep the plumbing out of tests of value.** Drop navigation, sign-in and password reset, assume the user is already signed in, show the core value with one obvious call to action, and record what people do instead of what they say they would do. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.102; B78 p.105-107
- **Use feature fakes rarely and retire them quickly.** A button that leads to a coming-soon message gauges demand; pull it as soon as the threshold is reached, and make it up to users where needed. Confidence: low. Sources: B34 p.105-108
- **Prototype what customers touch before the systems behind it.** Put the screen, the brochure or the pitch in front of people first; most new ideas fail because teams overrate how well customers will grasp them and how much customers will care. Confidence: low. Sources: B81 p.32-33
- **Start with the entry points and the core 20 percent.** About 80 percent of interaction uses about 20 percent of the functionality, so prototype home, sign-up and the main task first, then dashboards and profiles. Confidence: medium. Sources: B87 p.55; B96 p.118
- **When an error would be expensive, prototype, and run usability tests on any multi-step flow before release.** Build an interactive prototype for flows with several steps, products that cannot be patched fast, or anything where a mistake could hurt people; if a reworked flow fails in production, you cannot tell whether the idea or its execution was at fault. Skip prototyping only when engineering could ship the real version just as quickly. Confidence: low. Sources: B27 p.59, 83-84, 109-110, 157
- **Prototype early, since change gets steeply more expensive as fidelity rises.** The work needed to alter a design climbs more than linearly as it approaches production code and real content; interaction problems discovered after the build cost far more than ones caught in a clickable draft. Confidence: medium. Sources: B74 p.20-21; B87 p.11-12, 20; B11 p.52-53
- **Explore two or three clearly different concepts before refining any one.** Draft 2 to 3 concepts at low fidelity, with each running 3 to 8 screens deep, then test and merge them across roughly five days; including a deliberately provocative option draws out needs users cannot put into words. Iterating on one concept alone risks polishing a weak idea. Confidence: medium. Sources: B58 p.482-484, 603-604; B57 p.109; B25 p.50

## Fidelity

- **Choose the fidelity the question calls for, not the most you can produce.** A prototype built beyond need attracts the wrong comments and slows development; developers need less fidelity than stakeholders do; tell reviewers precisely what the prototype covers and what feedback you are after. Confidence: medium. Sources: B87 p.54-55; B34 p.112-115, 132; B96 p.114, 117-118, 123; B42 p.210-221; B85 p.62, 74
- **Prototype behavior first, keeping visuals rough.** Polished visuals pull comments toward color and type, look finished so testers become polite, and leave the team unwilling to rework structure; low fidelity uncovers roughly as many problems and keeps critique on the flow. Keep early prototypes plain on purpose: when iterating requires a specialist, fidelity is too high. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.431, 441-442; B27 p.159-160, 182-183; B57 p.107-108; B02 p.477, 480; B26 p.133, 150, 156-157; B72 p.115, 120; B46 p.806
- **Make customer-facing test prototypes realistic enough to draw reactions.** Aim for a believable facade built in about a day; flimsy paper or bare boxes can break the illusion and invite design feedback instead of natural behavior. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.172-177; B27 p.161-165; B87 p.24-25
- **Set fidelity on five separate dimensions.** Breadth, depth, interactivity, visual refinement and the data model each vary on their own, so a prototype can look rough yet go deep in one flow; how hard a tool is to use is a separate matter from fidelity. Resolution (how much detail) and fidelity (how close to the final look, sound and behavior) are also distinct. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.74-81; B74 p.64-65; B75 p.213-214
- **Use breadth to test navigation and depth to test tasks.** A horizontal prototype shows every top-level option one layer deep; a vertical one builds two or three paths fully. Build wide and shallow first, then deepen only the tested areas. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.76-79; B28 p.68-69; B74 p.48-49
- **Name each deliverable by what it lets people do.** A wireframe is low-fidelity structure, a mockup is static visual design, and a prototype is interactive at any level of visual fidelity; work typically moves from sketches and wireframes, through clickable flows, to builds that have been tested. Confidence: medium. Sources: B87 p.10-11; B75 p.210-211; B34 p.129-132
- **Plan how much must work.** An early exploratory prototype needs about 30 to 40 percent of functions working; an assessment-test prototype about 60 to 80 percent. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.77, 79
- **Ask targeted questions when you show a high-fidelity prototype.** Polished work looks finished, so reviewers critique the surface and teams consider fewer alternatives unless you steer them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.477, 480; B87 p.54-55

## Content, links and states

- **Fill prototypes with realistic content and data, never lorem ipsum.** Numbers must add up and names must be plausible; let participants enter and see their own data, and budget real time to find, clean and import sample data. Use realistic but not real records where data is sensitive. Confidence: medium. Sources: B12 p.420-421; B74 p.27-28, 42, 56; B81 p.183-184; B51 p.337, 340-341
- **Settle how links outside the prototype's scope respond.** Options are inert links, labeled placeholders, one shared page saying the area is not in this prototype, or a placeholder per destination; any response beats a silent dead end, and a not-yet-implemented screen is a chance to ask what the user expected to see next. Confidence: medium. Sources: B74 p.48-49; B28 p.68-69; B81 p.156-163
- **Include decoy controls.** A prototype containing only the controls the test tasks need makes those tasks too easy; add plausible extra controls that answer "not yet implemented". Confidence: low. Sources: B20 p.450-451
- **Keep the prototype consistent with itself.** Give one person ownership of matching names, dates, fonts and colors across it, settle shared elements from the brand guide at the start, and rehearse before test day; a typo or mismatch reminds participants that none of it is real. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.193-196; B96 p.117-118
- **Do not let a prototype stand in for documentation.** It hides history and states nobody triggers; keep a decision log between versions and pair it with storyboards or an interaction matrix of the interesting moments. Confidence: medium. Sources: B74 p.34-35, 37; B62 p.69
- **Design flows and scenarios, not isolated pages.** A prototype must work across the journey; it also exposes transition problems, such as how much to show between a home page and results, that static comps never force. Confidence: low. Sources: B87 p.17-18, 46

## Storyboards and narrative

- **Storyboard the test flow before building it.** Use about 5 to 15 frames, about one minute each, starting one or two steps before the product (a search, a shelf, an article) and extending past the obvious end; keep the tested flow to about 15 minutes inside a 60-minute session. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.156-166, 247; B25 p.47
- **Give each prototype a brief narrative.** Say which slice of the full experience it shows; for take-home exercises, send the clickable prototype together with a short rationale covering task, why, who, context, idea and metrics. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.113, 121; B25 p.56
- **Present vision prototypes as a short video with narration.** Spend one to two minutes on one or two use cases, and finish on the screen at which the user receives the benefit. Confidence: low. Sources: B12 p.420-421
- **Act out scenarios to take the user's perspective.** Role-play, props and stop-go improvisation help the team feel the situation; plan how to record it, since performances leave no artifact. Acting four scenarios for two personas takes about an hour. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.115-118

## Methods and media

- **Use paper for cheap, fast, collaborative early exploration.** A paper prototype takes about an hour and should never take more than a couple of hours; it lowers participant anxiety, and people freely relabel controls or sketch the page they expected on a blank sheet, which reveals mental models a polished test hides. Confidence: medium. Sources: B87 p.22-23; B38 p.100-101; B06 p.185-187, 206-207; B34 p.113; B30 p.162
- **Know where paper stops working.** Paper cannot show states, animation or scrolling well, changes how people read, and makes users imagine the context; move to linked digital wireframes for complex flows, and use paper for the final test only when the product itself is paper. Confidence: medium. Sources: B27 p.161-165; B87 p.24-25; B38 p.100-101; B81 p.183-184
- **Have a dedicated human computer run paper tests.** One person moderates and a second does nothing but swap pieces, rehearsed until their responses are machine-like; paper widgets must stay simple enough to work in real time, and the prototype gets revised after each session. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.337-339; B87 p.26-27; B74 p.90
- **Use a Wizard of Oz to check the core value before you build it.** A person out of sight acts as the system, typing or speaking its replies, so you discover what people expect free of the limits of today's technology; staff it so replies come quickly, and never promise anything that cannot be built. Confidence: high. Sources: B33 p.123-124; B87 p.27-30; B30 p.159-164; B06 p.181-184; B34 p.109-110; B20 p.434
- **Prototype personalized AI with participants' own data or a human stand-in, and simulate wrong answers.** Dummy data makes personalized features feel fake; deliberately wrong outputs show how people react to errors. Confidence: medium. Sources: B51 p.337, 340-341; B12 p.360-361
- **Test business ideas with storyboards before code.** Storyboards, paper and Wizard of Oz cost almost nothing and measure reactions to a concept; in one storyboard test, 3 of 50 people reporting a problem was enough signal to pivot. Confidence: low. Sources: B30 p.159-164
- **Use presentation software for simple, linear mid-fidelity click-throughs.** Slides are installed everywhere, support links and transitions and suit screens; choose the aspect ratio first and set slides to advance only on links. Collaboration, libraries and branching interactivity run out fast. Confidence: medium. Sources: B74 p.146-147, 154; B87 p.32-34; B81 p.180-192
- **Fake the experience using whatever tool is quickest for the medium.** Slides for screens, staff following a script for services, a real space modified for places, a brochure for hardware; people can stand in for bots. Confidence: low. Sources: B81 p.180-192
- **Prototype in semantic HTML when the question is content structure, but stay within your skill.** Writing a linear semantic document forces choices about how elements relate and can seed production code; before choosing, see how many flows you manage in 30 minutes with a tool and with code. Two HTML branches compared two scroll models in hours rather than weeks. Confidence: low. Sources: B87 p.35-37; B74 p.191
- **Pick a tool according to audience, sharing needs and team skills.** Early solo ideas fit paper or sketch-to-click tools, and remote tests need hosted links; insist on commenting, link sharing, revision history and imports that keep layers. Confidence: low. Sources: B74 p.51-53; B87 p.40-41
- **Build modular prototypes so components swap between sessions.** A shared component library let one team replace a cart across 60 screens in about 15 minutes. Confidence: low. Sources: B62 p.118-119
- **Prototype motion as motion, not as static screens.** Sketches answer whether motion helps, motion comps show how elements move, and interactive builds show whether it feels right and stays nonblocking; when only wireframes are available, annotate keyframes with trigger, rule and end. Confidence: medium. Sources: B14 p.155-156; B42 p.210-221
- **Draft sample dialogs and read them out loud before you build a conversational product.** Write, like a screenplay, the five most frequent use cases together with the error paths; from both sides, step through context, trigger and intent, then orientation, action, guidance, error and closure; finally hold a table read to catch lines that are repetitive or stilted. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.44, 198; B33 p.121-122
- **Prototype spatial experiences in 3D, on the target device, from early on.** Flat tools and paper cannot convey scale, lighting or room size; stage the scenes physically or turn to VR prototyping tools, and rather than writing a 3D pipeline by hand, build on an engine that already exists. Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.177-181, 247; B15 p.42-43
- **Treat any AI-generated output as a prototype and never as final.** Fact-check, edit and refine it; it turns harmful once shared as finished work. Generated sketches can still let teammates who do not draw take part in early ideation. Confidence: low. Sources: B75 p.77, 215-216, 434, 540
- **Make workshop ideas testable within hours.** Scenarios, storyboards, greyscale wireframes or clickable prototypes can be tested in hallways or online during the workshop itself; a full sprint prototype takes about one working day. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.209-211; B81 p.18, 170-172; B96 p.114

## Presenting design work

- **Have stakeholders try the design instead of reading descriptions of it.** Written requirements and annotated wireframes are easily misread; a clickable prototype makes flows and animation explain themselves, and portfolio pieces work better as clickable flows than as static images. Confidence: low. Sources: B87 p.12-13, 16; B05 p.132, 216
- **Show visual work at true scale and in context.** Judge posters at full size and viewing distance, embed web comps in a browser page at the target width, and show logos alone and mocked on real objects. Confidence: low. Sources: B71 p.136; B95 p.92-94, 128
- **Make comps for client sign-off fully resolved, and first builds fully clickable.** Sign-off comps should let the client picture the finished product; every page of a first build should link, and the build should be checked against the approved comps. This applies to approval, not to early testing. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.102-103, 129
