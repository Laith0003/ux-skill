## Framing the problem

- **Spend the early effort on defining the problem.** Diverge and converge on the problem before doing the same for solutions; budget up to about two-thirds of available time for problem definition on open projects, and accept that test results may redefine the problem. Iterating on an existing design improves that design but does not produce an innovative one. Confidence: medium. Sources: B29 p.29, 41-45; B30 p.50-52; B40 p.11, 13-15, 18; B72 p.309
- **Treat the brief and every requested feature as a hypothesis about a need.** Question the request (a rebrand to lift sales may really be a targeting problem), ask why a user or executive wants a specific solution, and look for cheaper fixes to the underlying need; features built by decree tend to go unused. Confidence: medium. Sources: B71 p.22, 62; B40 p.13-15; B27 p.111-114; B57 p.48-49; B03 p.46
- **Write one problem statement that names the user, the need and the insight, without a solution.** Do not chain two problems with "and" or "or", and reject disguised requirements such as "launch an app by Q2"; keep the statement visible and park off-topic ideas. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.49-51; B72 p.25; B96 p.55, 66
- **Reframe stuck problems by moving up and down the why chain.** Ask "why" to broaden and "what stops us" to narrow from a How Might We statement (groups of up to 4, 15 to 20 minutes), or run a Five Whys exercise, optionally regenerating alternate chains with a language model. Confidence: low. Sources: B96 p.67-68; B75 p.99-101
- **Pin down vague brief words and the who, what, when, where, why and how.** Agree what adjectives like "cool" or "stylish" mean, and let the six questions reveal who it is for, the channel, the schedule, the setting, the motive and the money available. Confidence: low. Sources: B71 p.22-24; B25 p.24, 30-34
- **Write a real brief and use it as the yardstick.** Writing a brief raised concept quality by about 20 percent on average; briefs that worked were 500 to 1,500 words long, give at least 25 percent of content to expression (look and feel), at least 10 percent to strategy and 5 percent to function, and treat over 55 percent on process as a warning sign. Derive a one-page mini brief (problem, personas, scenarios, goals and principles, capped at 5 objectives, 5 audience needs and 6 character words) and read it aloud at the start of meetings. Confidence: medium. Sources: B72 p.33, 40-43; B05 p.48, 168-169; B09 p.82-83
- **Write a one-line manifesto or concept statement and cut features against it.** A short declaration of what the product is decides what to leave out; a system concept statement of 100 to 150 words, or a 20-second pitch, tests whether the idea is defined at all. Confidence: medium. Sources: B57 p.57-61; B20 p.131-132; B48 p.125
- **Confirm scope, deliverables, fidelity and assumptions before designing.** Settle sizes, features, inclusions, exclusions, revision rounds and timeline up front, and state missing facts as explicit assumptions rather than stalling; most project blowups trace to details missed at the start, and churn in the proposal predicts churn in the project. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.134-136, 144, 149; B25 p.21-22
- **Aim at the end state the user wants, not at a step on the way.** Someone signing in wants to be inside the product, not to type a password; designing toward the end state cuts unnecessary steps. Confidence: low. Sources: B14 p.72
- **Write job stories instead of feature requests.** Stating the situation, the motivation and the expected outcome keeps ideas tied to the job the user is doing rather than to features. Confidence: low. Sources: B96 p.82-83

## Goals, requirements and principles

- **Set goals as measurable outcomes, never as features, and add explicit anti-goals.** "Raise authenticated visits by 15 percent" can be critiqued against; "add remember me" cannot. Agree KPIs at kickoff, phrase business goals as observable behaviors, list what is out of scope, rank the top 3 goals, and never lower a reasonable usability target to fit a weak design. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.81, 183; B96 p.61; B35 p.29, 38; B54 p.160-161; B94 p.60-61; B74 p.47-48
- **Name failure states as well as targets, and keep measuring after launch.** For AI and networked products set 3 to 5 outcomes plus what must not happen, because behavior drifts and the product is never finished. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.360, 431-435; B45 p.6
- **Write principles narrow enough to reject most options.** A principle that almost every option satisfies does no work; use 3 to 5 experience adjectives and present 3 to 5 visual language studies, one or two of them pushed to an extreme on purpose. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.36, 82, 183; B26 p.161, 163
- **Capture requirements as user needs, then trace every functional requirement to one.** Write needs in user language (objects, actions, contexts) without technical solutions, show the list back to users, number functional requirements and cite the need each meets, which also exposes the cost of late changes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.136-138, 146; B57 p.50-53
- **Invest in requirements and early testing because late fixes cost far more.** A fix that costs 1 unit in analysis costs about 10 in design, 100 in prototype and 1,000 after release; thorough requirements analysis reportedly cut change and maintenance cost by as much as 80 percent; the UI is roughly 37 to 50 percent of engineering effort; about 5 percent of cost spent on concepts fixes about 70 percent of final cost. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.880; B49 p.127, 239; B89 p.127; B72 p.33; B23 p.46, 80; B29 p.10, 12; B50 p.173
- **Balance user desirability, business viability and technical feasibility explicitly.** Build where business goals and user needs meet, agree the priority when they clash (for example live help versus support cost) and design the trade-off; keep a feature only when it is buildable in budget, people will use it, and it advances a goal. Confidence: medium. Sources: B29 p.315-319; B11 p.44, 46-47; B44 p.21-23; B38 p.42-43; B25 p.31-34
- **Usable is not enough: aim for useful and desirable too.** Fix basic usability first, then add motivation; products mature from functional through reliable, usable, convenient and pleasurable to meaningful, and a usable feature nobody needs is waste. Assess seven qualities: usefulness, usability, findability, credibility, desirability, accessibility and value. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.27-30; B40 p.21-26, 33-34; B35 p.21-22, 352
- **Protect the basics while adding features.** Under the Kano model people notice basic needs only when they fail, satisfaction with performance needs grows with investment, and delighters become ordinary expectations over time. Confidence: low. Sources: B54 p.216-219
- **Serve both end goals and experience goals; treat business and technical goals as constraints.** Meeting end goals while ignoring experience feels unpleasant; technical aims exist to support user and business goals and should never take priority over them. Confidence: low. Sources: B01 p.13-14
- **State the evidence behind each decision.** Order evidence from weakest to strongest (personal experience and theory, then patterns and secondary research, then primary research), combine several sources, require primary research for critical features, and ground choices in known principles rather than personal taste. Confidence: low. Sources: B11 p.126-129; B29 p.48-50
- **Start from what people need, not from the technology on hand.** Decide whether AI, VR or AR is warranted only after defining the user, problem, environment and tasks, be willing to conclude a problem needs none of them, and choose the experience before choosing UI elements. Confidence: high. Sources: B21 p.274-277; B65 p.156-157, 235; B48 p.124, 127; B66 p.130-132; B54 p.28-30; B78 p.18
- **List everyone a service touches before designing it.** Services break when they overlook a person beyond the obvious user, so list everyone affected and their concerns and design flows such as payment so nobody is excluded. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.142-143, 146; B55 p.60-61

## Assumptions, hypotheses and risk

- **Declare assumptions as a team and test the riskiest first.** Call requirements assumptions; turn them into hypotheses with outcome, user, benefit and one feature each plus the signal that will prove them wrong; start with a table of about 7 to 10 rows, plot by risk against value, record how and against what criteria each will be tested, and prototype the riskiest first. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.46-47, 54-55, 67-69; B96 p.105-106, 121; B81 p.61-63
- **Start with a pre-mortem.** Imagine the project already failed, list why, and turn hidden assumptions into questions to answer; score risks 1 to 5 on probability and impact (1 to 25), and set a long-term goal 6 months to 5 years out. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.61-63; B96 p.48-49; B17 p.16, 23-24
- **Count invalidation as a cheap win.** Learning in days that nobody wants a product beats building it; a test ends either as a cheap failure or as a success with flaws, and both save months, so follow with a shorter round and then larger-scale data. Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.30, 129-130, 133; B81 p.228-229; B13 p.186, 213-215
- **Pick the process to fit the problem.** Iterative exploration earns its cost when needs are fuzzy or the goal is a leap; a gated linear process still suits well-defined incremental improvements. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.22
- **Treat process stages as modes you loop through.** Define, research, ideate, prototype, select, implement and learn (or the five design-thinking modes) can overlap and loop back when later stages expose gaps. Confidence: low. Sources: B71 p.20; B40 p.11, 18
- **Protect focus during intensive sessions.** Interruptions cost about 23 minutes to recover from, so ban devices and side work during sprints. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.44; B96 p.56-58

## Scope and features

- **Map every feature to a stated user need or outcome and drop orphans.** Filter features through user, organizational and situational needs, merge redundant ones, and have users walk through the list; one product cut 11 of 20 planned features for its first release, and a release once spent 30 percent of effort on a feature users ranked 11th. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.65-66; B06 p.141, 169-175; B57 p.51-53; B67 p.70-71; B58 p.128
- **Resist feature creep; strengthen what is already strong.** Matching competitor features bloats products and there is rarely budget to remove features later; lock high-priority goals, push the rest to a later version, ship an essential first version, and use research evidence to refuse late additions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.257-260; B06 p.151-153; B38 p.64, 85; B85 p.122-123; B57 p.60-61
- **Scope hidden costs honestly.** User-generated content implies moderation, categorization and admin tooling; say so early. Confidence: low. Sources: B38 p.56
- **Prioritize by value against cost.** Rate importance and fix cost, sort by the ratio and cut at an affordability line (cheap label fixes rise to the top); or plot value against effort or feasibility and do high-value easy items first, high-value hard second, easy low-value third. Count maintenance cost, not only build cost. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.611-622; B64 p.208; B67 p.76-77; B25 p.44-45; B35 p.141-142; B32 p.113
- **Split recommendations into now and later.** Distinguish fixes the current schedule can absorb from the deeper changes the product really needs, draft without political filtering first, and expect only about half of recommendations to be adopted, mostly for lack of time. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.318-319; B89 p.120
- **Plan the roadmap about 18 months to 2 years out.** Ship core features first, collect 3 to 6 months of usage data and test results before any big change, and hold a review at least every quarter. Confidence: low. Sources: B35 p.108-110

## Ideation

- **Produce many distinct options before converging.** Review at least twice as many thumbnails as comps you will present, vary early comps across layout and navigation rather than tweaking one, list 3 or 4 ideas across type, platform and modality, sketch 3 to 5 concepts per idea, and expect to discard about a dozen ideas; compare radically different alternatives because similar variants teach little. Confidence: medium. Sources: B95 p.68, 116-117, 127-128; B25 p.42-43; B16 p.461; B66 p.133; B28 p.76, 302-303; B26 p.161
- **Break fixation with explicit variation prompts.** Heuristic prompts reliably produce more and more creative concepts; use SCAMPER to generate several fixes per problem, or tactics such as simplifying the brief, changing medium or doing the opposite of the market leader. Confidence: high. Sources: B72 p.88, 97; B30 p.258-262; B71 p.142-143
- **Borrow from other domains, never copy blindly.** Short demos of products from unrelated industries (roughly 3 minutes per demo, capturing 10 to 20 ideas) or a few analog product images per participant beat same-industry review. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.100-105; B72 p.85
- **Use a staged sketch to beat the blank page.** Notes 20 minutes, rough ideas 20, Crazy Eights 8 minutes (8 panels, 60 seconds each), then a detailed solution sketch of 30 to 90 minutes; returns diminish beyond about 10 to 12 solution sketches. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.115-121, 125, 244; B74 p.195
- **Constrain studio sketches with a context of use.** Give each sketch a specific user, setting and goal, plus a pattern to use and a feeling to evoke; 4 to 5 constraints tell a story without overload, with about 30 minutes of solo sketching. Confidence: low. Sources: B30 p.263-269
- **Let ideas incubate overnight before deciding.** People were about a third more likely to connect distant ideas after sleep, so place a night between divergence and decision; keep each solution brainstorm itself short (under about 15 minutes). Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.81; B27 p.101
- **Bring users into ideation, not only validation.** Participatory sessions reveal which features users value and what is missing, while designers keep the final say; projects that involve users only after build can fix only cosmetic issues. Confidence: low. Sources: B87 p.45; B06 p.26-30
- **Timebox generative AI exploration and mine its misfires.** A time limit stops endless regeneration, and odd outputs can point to a better direction even when never used directly. Confidence: low. Sources: B75 p.195, 197, 203-205, 481-482

## Sequencing the design work

- **Settle content before layout.** Work concept, then script, then sketch; plan content throughout with strategy and writing ahead of visual templates; finalize copy before production or use placeholder text in the real font and size; validate comps with real worst-case content such as the longest titles. Confidence: medium. Sources: B33 p.119-120; B55 p.192-194; B05 p.247; B35 p.268-269
- **Put writers and visual designers on the same team.** When writers sit on a separate approval track, the result is copy poured into boxes drawn without it; systems mirror the communication structure of the teams that build them. Confidence: low. Sources: B33 p.7-8, 113-116
- **Start from one real feature and the whole flow, not the shell or isolated components.** Navigation decisions need information from a few built features; components designed in isolation add up to an incoherent product, so explore across real flows first, then refine pieces. Confidence: medium. Sources: B47 p.8-9; B22 p.140-141, 145-146; B62 p.78-79
- **Design for whole activities and test the parts together.** People accept complexity that feels essential to the activity; help, documentation and interface can each pass alone yet fail together, so include at least one test where people use all the pieces in sequence. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.231-233, 245-246; B28 p.48, 74, 239-240; B33 p.7-8
- **Storyboard the whole sequence before building single screens.** Lay out every spread or screen side by side, vary scale, cropping and quiet pages for pacing, and use 4 to 6 panel storyboards (about 10 minutes) for flows. Confidence: medium. Sources: B92 p.19; B10 p.152-154; B12 p.69, 82
- **Mock up conversations as numbered transcripts and track prompts in one sheet.** Name each speaker, number every line and set the user's turns in bold; sort brainstormed utterance pairs by topic and scope (about 2 hours, 15 to 30 minutes solo); keep label, full text and intent of every prompt in a master spreadsheet. Confidence: medium. Sources: B60 p.220-227; B63 p.172-173
- **Test the earliest artifact that can answer the question.** Paper and wireframes are far cheaper to fix than shipped code, but 3D and spatial interactions that feel strong on paper often disappoint, so build a working slice early on the target hardware. Confidence: medium. Sources: B23 p.46, 80; B83 p.684, 716-717; B48 p.174, 247
- **Make the least artifact that communicates, and throw it away once answered.** Speed beats polish for intermediate work; do not stretch a prototype to test a second question. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.170-171; B85 p.57, 110
- **Overlap sketching, front-end and engineering in short phases.** Share problems with engineers early because effort estimates (a day versus months) should shape the pitch; for AI products iterate prototype, a quick model spike and the data in one loop; run UI and industrial design in parallel sprints of a few weeks. Confidence: medium. Sources: B87 p.37-38; B51 p.394-397; B12 p.357-361; B72 p.377-378, 384
- **Return to wireframes for structural changes; annotate only where needed.** Roll minor changes into mockups but rework IA changes in a fresh wireframe pass; annotate complex or unique template pages, show priority by position and heading size, and mark wireframes as not visual design. Confidence: medium. Sources: B38 p.31; B93 p.429-436
- **Pull stakeholders into quick sketching when requirements are thin.** Messy joint sketching beats a formal design round built on incomplete information. Confidence: low. Sources: B38 p.22

## Accessibility and inclusion in the process

- **State accessibility targets in the spec and annotate designs before handoff.** Specify heading levels, landmark regions, reading order, contrast pairs, non-color cues, focus styling and keyboard interaction; a late accessibility gate breeds resentment and rework, so bring the expertise in from the start and design for the hardest contexts first. Confidence: medium. Sources: B80 p.35-36; B32 p.62-63; B18 p.59, 67
- **Check inclusion at every stage gate and question the spec itself.** Exclusion accumulates: people lost at requirements are not rescued by good detail design, and most evaluation checks conformance to a spec rather than whether the spec excludes people. Measure both absolute exclusion or conformance level and the gap to competitors, and set a yearly reduction target. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.203; B32 p.58-59, 61
- **Build standards into the tools people edit with.** Limit the CMS to semantic styles, keep vetted alt text attached to each media item, flag nonstandard terms as authors type, and make accessibility part of ongoing governance so routine edits do not wear it down. Confidence: medium. Sources: B55 p.156; B18 p.219-224
- **Document every exclusion decision and justify it.** Ship an inaccessible option only when nothing else works or the cost would be plainly out of proportion, and keep the record so later teams can revisit it. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.52-53, 64-65, 76, 82
- **Fix accessibility problems in a fixed order of preference.** First try removing the feature, then modifying it, then adding a parallel channel, then offering another way to do the task, and only as a last resort an assistive add-on; check again afterwards for new problems. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.113
- **Verify perception, then cognition, then motor action.** Confirm users can perceive state, predict behavior and act comfortably, testing at each level before moving on. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.106-109
- **Staff for the target cultures and adapt heuristics to them.** Include representatives of key markets on the team, plan about 50 percent more meeting time for international projects, and soften rules such as hiding unneeded information for high-context audiences. Confidence: low. Sources: B49 p.54, 81-83, 121-123

## Evaluation and quality control

- **Find problems first; rate and fix them later.** Interrupting evaluators to rate severity or brainstorm fixes breaks their flow, so collect severity afterwards and discuss solutions in a separate meeting. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.65-66, 182; B09 p.43, 131-132
- **Inspect by task step, not field by field.** Walk hypothetical users through scenarios; line-by-line reviews miss what goes wrong mid-task. Confidence: low. Sources: B89 p.189-190, 192
- **Check each design against the action cycle questions.** Users must be able to tell what they can do, how, what happened, what it means and whether the goal was reached; each unanswerable question is a gap. Confidence: low. Sources: B79 p.82-84
- **Watch for intermittent failures.** A feature that fails now and then erodes a product without anyone flagging it, whereas a complete failure at least gets noticed. Confidence: low. Sources: B03 p.38
- **Test small and often.** Run regular small usability rounds every 2 to 6 weeks within a program plan of 2 to 3 years. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.319, 354, 359
- **Proof every deliverable in passes from big picture to detail.** First fit, purpose and dimensions; then eye flow, weight, palette and font count; then every word, alignment, crop, widow and kerning; squint, step back (about 6 feet) and flip the work upside down to expose imbalance. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.78-80, 202-204; B68 p.138-140, 152, 170-172
- **Freeze the hue set before polishing.** Late in a piece adjust value and intensity of existing colors; a new unrelated hue forces everything to rebalance. Confidence: low. Sources: B68 p.150, 172
- **Make variants real reworkings, not variety for its own sake.** "For variety" excuses whims; a variant should rework a scheme thoroughly. Confidence: low. Sources: B90 p.100
- **Show palettes applied, with few options.** Show the colors in use on real deliverables and explain why each pairing works; many alternatives only confuse. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.11
- **Collect one to five quality benchmarks before designing.** Comparable reference work sets the polish bar to measure against, not something to copy. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.116, 202
- **Plan finishing effects and production arithmetic early.** Work out content per page, total pages and sheet size, and budget animation, sound or print finishes at the start so they support the message. Confidence: low. Sources: B92 p.63; B71 p.160

## Shipping and change

- **Protect tested designs through production.** After iteration, labels, positions and navigation are paid-for UX; brief builders on the intent, keep a project style guide, stay involved, compare the build to the approved designs, and do not alter approved work in production without a clear usability reason. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.298-299, 383-385, 455-456, 894; B38 p.32, 59; B95 p.95-96; B71 p.34, 149
- **Roll out redesigns and system changes gradually.** Habit-bound users of daily tools resist sudden overhauls (one abrupt redesign lost about 2 percent of daily users); phase changes, run betas with expectations set, start with colors and type before buttons and navigation, and reserve sprint capacity for system work. Confidence: medium. Sources: B22 p.124-125, 138-140, 151-152; B40 p.33
- **Check new work against the component library, and standardize only what will be reused.** Reconcile new elements with the library before sign-off, check variants, reuse, accessibility and content variation in code review, and pilot before generalizing. Confidence: low. Sources: B62 p.326-331
- **Give every initiative a named owner and an upkeep budget.** Do not start without one owner on the business side and one on the technical side, budget persona updates every 1 to 2 years, and give newcomers an introduction pack. Confidence: low. Sources: B39 p.13, 120; B50 p.21-22, 96-97, 166, 173
- **Check the message hierarchy with the client in two questions.** Did we capture what you meant, and is anything absent or in the wrong order? Confidence: low. Sources: B39 p.46
- **Open several points of negotiation in highly connected systems.** Where outcomes are uncertain, give stakeholders overlapping entry points instead of one fixed solution. Confidence: low. Sources: B45 p.10-11
- **Audit AI systems in stages and follow up.** Scope, map, collect artifacts, test and reflect, then check recommendations at 3, 6 or 12 months; measure AI workflow time end to end including prompting and review before claiming efficiency, and map the workflow before choosing what to automate. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.202, 266; B75 p.522-524, 529-530
- **Choose immersive hardware deliberately.** Use AR when people need to keep working in their surroundings, VR when the experience needs their whole attention; decide whether immersion or reach matters more, and design for the target device (a seated button-only viewer cannot support walking flows). Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.129-140, 180

## Planning and project operations

- **Estimate effort from task-level hours and revisit monthly.** A usability test can run about 215 hours across planning, recruiting, protocol, sessions, analysis and reporting; vendor timelines ran well past internal estimates because of client review checkpoints; plan 45-hour weeks with time for management, outsource no more than about half of wireframing, and keep project proposals to 2 pages. Typical mapping projects run from 1 to 2 days to over 6 weeks. Confidence: medium. Sources: B58 p.150-159, 167, 568; B64 p.122-123
- **Do not spread one UX person across many projects.** A ratio such as one engineer to 11 projects makes the role ineffective; large projects need dedicated IA, usability, content and visual design capacity. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.891; B58 p.74-75
- **Break work into small tasks with one owner and a date.** Bigger projects need smaller tasks; keep reusable step lists per project type and a board with one column per phase. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.110-113, 121, 125-131
- **Hold an internal review at least a day before delivery and deliver early in the day.** This leaves time for final changes and for client tweaks across time zones. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.26, 117, 133, 205-207
- **Do the arithmetic before accepting late global changes.** Multiply items by time per item against time left before agreeing. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.105-106, 246
- **Do not give clients homework.** Prepare draft copy from what already exists, shortlist choices and suggest defaults, so the client just approves. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.222-224
- **Close every project with a concrete retrospective.** Score the result from 1 to 10, write down specific wins, mistakes and changes, and convert them into process rules. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.139-142
- **Name files with a sortable date stamp.** A convention such as year-month-day-time, company, project keeps versions in order. Confidence: low. Sources: B44 p.292
- **Follow a staged persona process from data to revision.** Collect data, form and agree a hypothesis, fix the number, describe, list situations, gain acceptance, disseminate, write scenarios and revise. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.21-22, 166; B01 p.15-16
