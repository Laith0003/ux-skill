## Writing for screens

- **Write for scanners: half the words, key words first.** About four in five people scan and only about one in six reads word for word, so cut print copy roughly in half, use the inverted pyramid, meaningful headings, paragraphs capped at five sentences, bullets, and restrained bold on the first mention of key terms, and lead every heading, line, list item and link with its meaningful word; one rewrite in this style cut task time by 80 percent, and lists alone gave nearly half of another site's gain. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.168, 259, 267-269, 613, 615; B77 p.51-52; B18 p.155, 158; B11 p.104-105; B23 p.269, 536
- **Target a reading age of about 12 for general audiences.** Average adult reading sits around grade 8 to 9 and about one fifth of adults read at grade 5 or below; WCAG 3.1.5 (AAA) asks for a simpler summary or version when text needs more than lower secondary reading ability, and children's sections need far simpler language. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.120, 594; B23 p.269, 536; B18 p.150, 164, 256; B57 p.67
- **Treat readability formulas as an early warning, not proof.** They count syllables and sentence length and are only accurate to about 1.5 grades; confirm clarity by testing real content with real readers. Confidence: medium. Sources: B18 p.160-163; B46 p.593
- **Put a plain key-points summary above complex content and reveal detail on demand.** Rushed and low-literacy readers rely on it while experts simply skip it; long specs, disclaimers and legal text open only when needed. Confidence: medium. Sources: B18 p.159-160; B35 p.350, 365
- **Write in plain, active, concrete language.** State any condition ahead of the step it governs, keep steps in sequence, name the actor and verb early, speak to the reader directly, and split dense text with informative headings and genuine lists; typography cannot rescue dull or passive prose. Confidence: medium. Sources: B18 p.151-152; B88 p.152-153
- **Give instructions after the choice, not before it.** Explaining every recovery path before people pick one front-loads information they do not yet need. Confidence: low. Sources: B33 p.73-74
- **Keep headings and body in a consistent rhythm and give long reads resting points.** Similar heading lengths and parallel subhead grammar let readers skim one level, and predictable breaks help multi-sitting reading. Confidence: low. Sources: B92 p.118, 120; B39 p.74-76

## Home pages and first impressions

- **Make the home page say what the site is within seconds.** First impressions form in about 50 milliseconds and the first three seconds decide whether people expect to find what they need; answer what this is, what is offered, what I can do and why here, then check it by asking first-time visitors what the product does, for whom and how to get it. Confidence: high. Sources: B77 p.100-101; B55 p.27; B27 p.55-57
- **Make pre-purchase content read as trusted advice.** At the research moment people read reviews and look for the people behind a service; marketing fluff fails there. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.55
- **Establish organizational credibility early for high-context audiences.** In some cultures people first want to know the organization behind the product, its background, standing and leaders, before doing business, while low-context users want the task first. Confidence: low. Sources: B08 p.110-111, 126-127, 134

## Real content in design

- **Design with real, unruly content, never lorem ipsum past low fidelity.** Placeholder text hides an unclear value proposition, misleads test participants and layouts, and leaks into production; test very long and very short titles, ten-word headlines, long compound words and long names, and read every line aloud. Confidence: high. Sources: B18 p.160-163; B33 p.125; B85 p.81-82, 180; B22 p.143, 228, 251; B96 p.116-123; B74 p.55-56, 167; B87 p.43-44; B95 p.97-98
- **When a placeholder is unavoidable, label what belongs there.** Prefix dummy text with a note on the intended content, mark missing images with a neutral gray box labeled as for placement only, and assume anything placed may ship. Confidence: low. Sources: B38 p.51; B95 p.97-98
- **Treat interface words as design material that is iterated, not signed off once.** Copy gated behind editorial approval stalls fixes that a single sentence would solve; edit supplied copy and correct supplied photos rather than pasting them as delivered. Confidence: low. Sources: B33 p.6-7; B95 p.87

## Strategy and scope

- **Publish less, and only what serves a goal or a user need.** Map every piece to a business objective or user need; dropping just-in-case content removes about half of wished-for projects, and every feature that needs feeding (blog, video series, chat widget) must be weighed as a maintenance commitment. Confidence: high. Sources: B55 p.25-28; B39 p.13-14; B71 p.64
- **Plan content before layout and run it as an ongoing operation.** Settle what gets produced, who is responsible and how success is tracked ahead of layout work, plan substance, structure, workflow and governance together, and staff the repeating cycle of create, maintain, evaluate and govern rather than a one-off launch task. Confidence: high. Sources: B39 p.11, 24; B55 p.40-41, 48-49, 123, 161-176; B35 p.193-195
- **Prioritize content with a scored rubric.** Score each candidate 1 to 5 on requirement, reach, relevance, richness and revenue, keep what clears a resource line, and spend original writing on high-value topics while curating the rest. Confidence: medium. Sources: B55 p.136; B39 p.95
- **Name one content owner with the authority to refuse requests.** An editor-in-chief role sets standards, budgets and policy. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.30-31, 152
- **Keep strategy separate from tactics.** A blog or a social account is a tactic to test against the strategy, not the strategy itself. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.47-48
- **Define audiences precisely and rank them without ties.** Split vague groups such as customers into prospective and existing, choose the top three to five before auditing, and rank per channel to expose gaps. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.74, 92-93, 123-125
- **Build a message hierarchy, then derive topics and a purpose for each piece.** One primary message, a few secondary ones and supporting points guide writing (they are not copy); every topic serves at least one audience, and each piece is labeled persuade, inform, validate, instruct or entertain instead of following blanket rules. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.125-130
- **Prescribe each content item in a matrix.** For every item, capture its key messages, length or format, keywords, the people who own, write and review it, its source and its deadline. Confidence: low. Sources: B39 p.97
- **Set tone as an explicit experience goal.** Agree on three traits the voice should have and three it must avoid, then test each line against both lists; a playful bank or a stiff children's site breaks expectations. Confidence: low. Sources: B33 p.91-93; B57 p.66-67

## Audits

- **Audit before planning, quantitatively first.** Inventory every item with ID, title, URL, format, source, owner, location, metadata, traffic, last update and language, then judge quality; every early hour of auditing saved about four later in one project. Confidence: high. Sources: B55 p.67-71; B39 p.61-62; B35 p.149-161
- **Rate five or six qualitative factors on a consistent 1 to 5 scale.** Pick factors such as findability, readability, understandability, actionability, accuracy, audience fit and voice, write rating rules with examples, pilot them with one person and spot-check across auditors. Confidence: high. Sources: B55 p.73-74; B39 p.70-71; B06 p.159-161
- **Size the audit to the decision.** Use a full inventory for a migration, a broad shallow pass to find facets, or a core sample of the highest-volume section when time is short; one person can audit about 1,500 items, while 10,000 needs a team. Confidence: medium. Sources: B39 p.63, 72; B55 p.73
- **Let crawlers count and people judge.** Automated tools give quick, neutral volume data and about 80 percent of a vocabulary, but quality, accuracy and usefulness need human review. Confidence: medium. Sources: B55 p.67-68, 72; B93 p.185
- **Run rolling audits on large sites.** Audit one area per cycle, weekly to quarterly, with about 10 to 15 percent of a role set aside, so later projects begin with a current view of the content. Confidence: medium. Sources: B55 p.81; B39 p.84
- **Cut redundant, outdated and trivial content, but verify automated pruning rules.** Duplicated landing pages, stale new posts and past events go first; rules such as under 10 views a week or unchanged for three months need checking, because analytics mislead and legally required pages are rarely visited. Confidence: low. Sources: B39 p.68-70
- **Audit at the element level and flag missing titles and descriptions.** Record title, subhead and image per item, check structural consistency, and make sure every page has a unique title and meta description. Confidence: low. Sources: B39 p.74-76; B35 p.154-155, 166
- **Label audit entries by theme to reveal gaps and skew.** Columns for location, period, people and medium show what is missing and what to commission. Confidence: low. Sources: B39 p.76

## Maintenance and governance

- **Plan maintenance for everything you publish.** Track last update and next review per item, set review triggers, legal retention and the search impact of deletion, and plan sunsetting and moderation before launching social or user-generated features; collections change significantly within about 12 months. Confidence: high. Sources: B55 p.166-168; B39 p.91, 135-136; B93 p.381; B84 p.39, 52
- **Run a single publishing calendar for all channels.** Record which team releases which content, on which channel and at what frequency, covering the site, social, video, in-app messages and ads, with governance meeting monthly and metrics reviewed quarterly. Confidence: high. Sources: B39 p.82-83, 141-142; B35 p.193-195; B55 p.28
- **Model content so it can live outside a single page.** Keep content independent of its presentation and define the smallest chunk that must be indexed or reused on its own, so one edit propagates everywhere and content adapts to new devices. Confidence: medium. Sources: B55 p.141-142; B93 p.436-438
- **Curate third-party and community content.** Syndicated feeds and bought content libraries still require an editor's review, ratings from a vocal few are not curation, and new community spaces should be seeded with role-model posts before launch. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.133-135; B39 p.52
- **Keep support content focused on what people ask now.** List the most frequent questions of the moment (about five) at the top of support pages. Confidence: low. Sources: B77 p.182

## Format, medium and culture

- **Fit length to attention spans, which vary by culture.** Sustained attention runs about 10 minutes; typical presentation tolerance ranges from about 15 minutes to an hour across countries; keep localized documents and strategy reports under about 50 pages. Confidence: high. Sources: B44 p.109; B49 p.46, 347; B93 p.397
- **Pair visuals with narration rather than with text when teaching.** Spoken text plus graphics produced far higher learning gains than text alone; keep narration within about 7 seconds of its visual, and in VR or AR use voiceover instead of long reading. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.716-718; B48 p.195
- **Write conversational content in single-sentence turns with repair variants.** Keep each agent turn to one sentence or less (two or three for extended explanations), split FAQ paragraphs into separately askable answers, and author a repeat, paraphrase, example and definition for each. Confidence: medium. Sources: B60 p.90-95, 125
- **Name things in the audience's frame and pair evocative names with plain categories.** Poetic color or product names delight browsers but frustrate people searching for a basic term, and names must be honest about what they contain. Confidence: low. Sources: B37 p.63; B53 p.142, 144, 339, 379, 433
- **Describe AI features by what they actually do.** Starter prompts should show the tool working on the user's real content, and robot or magic imagery misleads people about the product. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.104; B43 p.221-222
- **Show work in its real context.** Work shown too small, cropped or without the problem it solved frustrates reviewers; prune outdated pieces. Confidence: low. Sources: B84 p.39, 52, 59
