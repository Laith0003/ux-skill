## Ethical limits

- **Never pre-tick opt-ins or add-ons.** Anything that benefits the business at the user's cost must be opt-in, phrased plainly and left unchecked; defaults are powerful (opt-out consent ran 86 to nearly 100 percent against 4 to 28 percent for opt-in), so set them in the user's interest. Confidence: high. Sources: B02 p.51-53; B54 p.136-137; B77 p.199-200; B23 p.521; B11 p.100-101
- **Refuse dark patterns outright.** Fake download buttons, hidden installs, ads placed to catch misclicks, hyperbolic link bait and outside advertising on an empty search results page trade brief gains for lost trust. Confidence: high. Sources: B77 p.199-200; B44 p.212; B04 p.132-135; B02 p.51-53
- **Make leaving as easy as joining, and word it without shame.** No multi-step unsubscribe mazes; an opt-out link should say what it does, because confirmshaming copy erodes trust and hides the action from screen reader users; honor opt-outs and cap contact frequency. Confidence: high. Sources: B02 p.51-53; B07 p.203-206; B56 p.70
- **Show the full price up front.** In a field test with millions of users, hiding fees until the last step raised spend about 21 percent and purchase 14 percent, which is exactly why drip pricing must be refused; keep pricing rules transparent and comparable rather than charging more to people unlikely to shop around. Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.61; B56 p.139
- **Never target by distress or pose as an authority.** Funnels that segment people by pain point, or pages styled to look like a government service, are predatory. Confidence: medium. Sources: B56 p.64-70, 170-171
- **Give a fair return for every request.** Forced email walls, unneeded fields, takeover ads and results held hostage until sign-up take without giving and lower satisfaction and word of mouth; evaluate a design by the lasting relationship rather than a single conversion figure. Confidence: low. Sources: B54 p.147; B44 p.189
- **Keep implied cues honest.** Type, color and imagery can suggest hand-made, premium or official without saying so; using that to mislead is deception. Confidence: low. Sources: B19 p.48-49

## Choice and defaults

- **Cut choice overload with defaults, filters and grouping.** Many people do nothing, so the default decides; in a classic study 24 jam flavors drew more shoppers but only a tenth as many buyers as 6; limit major options, group the rest, and make the likeliest action the largest button bound to Return. Confidence: high. Sources: B11 p.100-101; B14 p.88-90; B54 p.136-137

## Permissions and notifications

- **Ask for permission when the feature is requested, with the concrete benefit.** A location request tied to a particular venue won many more approvals than a vague one; explain the reason, what happens to the data and where to revoke it. Confidence: high. Sources: B52 p.190-191; B07 p.67; B33 p.75-76
- **Notify only with consent and only when the person can act.** Good reasons to notify are a message from another person, progress on a goal the user set, or a change that needs a response; never advertising, never false urgency, delivered to the active device only. Confidence: medium. Sources: B33 p.75-76; B52 p.238, 241-242
- **Make notifications specific, linked and timed to local context.** Name who and what, link straight to the content, and schedule for the recipient's time and situation; device-side scheduling produced about 15 times more notifications acted on. Confidence: medium. Sources: B52 p.238, 241-242; B33 p.75
- **Cap large badge counts.** Show 9+, 99+ or 999+ rather than exact large numbers. Confidence: low. Sources: B52 p.243
- **Throttle in-app feedback requests.** Ask at most every few uses (one product used every 5) and stop after a submission or a decline. Confidence: low. Sources: B52 p.76

## Feeds, metrics and social proof

- **Optimize recommendations for value delivered, not clicks.** Click and dwell objectives promote clickbait and amplify outrage; target completion or real use, consider ranking that rewards approval across groups, and let users state what they want (no politics this month). Confidence: medium. Sources: B61 p.114, 121; B43 p.197-198, 255
- **Show counts deliberately, because any visible number becomes a game.** In a 14,000-person experiment, visible download tallies made the gap between hit and flop songs much wider, and visible scores create goals people chase for good or ill; surface only numbers that serve users and turn data into specific tips. Confidence: medium. Sources: B43 p.80-84; B54 p.196-203
- **Use social proof from real faces, openly.** Showing friends who acted lifted participation (a deactivation page with friends' faces cut deactivations 7 percent; friend photos raised voting clicks); it is a strong lever and must never be covert. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.66-67, 230; B56 p.151-152
- **Use scarcity only when it is credible.** A few left persuades, but all-of-stock-left signals nobody wants it and a limited offer backfires if the deal later improves; caps on contributions can also raise their quality. Confidence: low. Sources: B44 p.220-221; B54 p.187-190
- **Size discounts to be noticed but believed.** About 5 percent goes unnoticed, 20 to 25 percent draws attention, 50 to 70 percent invites skepticism. Confidence: low. Sources: B44 p.95, 217-218

## Motivation

- **Give people a visible head start.** Starting a progress track partly filled (for example at 20 percent rather than zero) and prefilling an example message nearly doubled completion in one study. Confidence: medium. Sources: B44 p.208-209; B54 p.116-117
- **Ask people to name when and how they will act.** Getting a specific time for the action and giving a simple trigger lifted follow-through from 3 to 28 percent in a classic study; test it in context. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.110-113
- **Give feedback often to change behavior.** Continuous or daily feedback beats monthly, feedback alone cut energy use about 10 percent, and a public street display cut use 15 percent in three weeks. Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.215-217; B20 p.361-362
- **Tease a specific gap the person cares about.** Curiosity grows as more of a set is revealed (8 of 10 known beats 2 of 10); keep the tease proportional and never charge for what is free elsewhere. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.98-102
- **Offer unexpected, personal, no-strings gifts and delighters.** Personalized gifts nearly doubled donations (18 to 35 percent); a gift everyone gives becomes an expectation, and quid-pro-quo gifts feel pushy. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.83-90
- **Fix the trigger before the motivation.** Behavior needs motivation, ability and a trigger together; place triggers in front of motivated people, then make the action easier, and only last try to raise motivation. Confidence: low. Sources: B54 p.225-227
- **Find the game already in the task instead of bolting on points.** Badges on a dull task fade; uncover the real challenge, give clear goals and feedback, and raise difficulty with skill to keep people in flow. Confidence: low. Sources: B54 p.172-184

## Emotion and aesthetics

- **Design for visceral, behavioral and reflective response.** People form an affective judgment in milliseconds and rate attractive interfaces as more usable, and the memory of the ending drives recommendation, so frustration near the end spoils the whole. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.64-67; B06 p.75-76
- **Treat usability as the driver of recommendation.** System usability scores explain about 39 percent of likelihood to recommend; promoters average about 81 and detractors about 52, so aim above 80. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.246-247
- **Use easy-to-read type where you want action.** The same steps set in a plain face were judged quicker to do and buyers decided sooner (17 versus 41 percent deferring); save hard-to-read type for moments meant to feel crafted. Confidence: medium. Sources: B19 p.64-66
- **Expect type and color to prime the experience.** Identical food was rated sweeter, sourer or tastier depending only on letterforms, and abstract color sets mood before content is read; brighter values signal intensity and pale ones calm. Confidence: medium. Sources: B19 p.118, 121; B68 p.65, 177, 184, 192-206
- **Test trust and security cues per culture.** Heavy lock and guard imagery was rated far more negatively by German than by Indian users, and large trust badges can raise anxiety; overall polish and clarity carry trust. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.257; B23 p.529
- **Speak like a local.** Local terms, references and the right level of formality are trusted more; slang or the wrong register reads as disrespect. Confidence: low. Sources: B08 p.91, 138, 287

## Validation

- **Measure demand with an honest feature stub.** A button for an unbuilt feature (or upgrade) counts intent; tell people it is not ready yet when they click. Confidence: low. Sources: B27 p.86, 88, 103-104, 119
- **Build engagement on a solid core.** Participation, narrative and surprise add value (people prize what they helped make), but experience without substance is hollow. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.243-249
- **State specifics instead of platitudes.** Generic claims of excellence bore, and overselling pushes prospects to leave. Confidence: low. Sources: B84 p.65, 111
