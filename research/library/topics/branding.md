# branding

- **Keep the identity consistent across every touchpoint.** Logo, color, type, voice and behavior should say the same thing everywhere; inconsistent shades or claims the organization cannot back up erode recognition and trust. Confidence: high. Sources: B76 p.41, 108, 132; B84 p.39-40; B71 p.130; B13 p.237-240
- **Match the visual tone to the real audience and the content.** A style that signals the wrong age, seriousness or sector misleads people about who the product is for; test tone with the intended audience, not only designers. Confidence: high. Sources: B27 p.41, 182; B19 p.63, 84-85; B31 p.63, 73; B70 p.26-28
- **Choose type whose personality fits the message.** A playful face on a serious message becomes the story; formal faces add reverence that may not suit casual content; perceptions of typeface personality are widely shared, so check them with non-designers. Confidence: high. Sources: B19 p.63, 84-85; B31 p.63, 73; B47 p.20-25
- **Set personality through font, color, radius and tone together.** Serif reads classic, rounded sans playful, large radius friendly, zero radius formal; pick one corner treatment and never mix square and rounded corners in one interface. Confidence: medium. Sources: B47 p.20-25; B09 p.80
- **Judge style choices against stated emotional goals, not taste.** Ask whether a palette, face or motion supports how the audience should feel; ranked goals make decisions defensible. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.80; B39 p.29, 36-37
- **Write a message architecture of three to five ranked goals.** A prioritized list of what to convey gives designers, writers and search a shared yardstick; keep it separate from inspirational values or vision statements, which do not rank or guide tone. Confidence: medium. Sources: B39 p.29, 36-37
- **State a proposition beyond what the product does.** Competitors often share the same product and values, so name the specific idea the audience should take away and align product and values with it. Confidence: low. Sources: B71 p.24
- **Compress the promise into six words or fewer.** A short, differentiating line is easier to remember and forces a choice of the one quality to be known for. Confidence: low. Sources: B05 p.32, 46, 54
- **Build the mark from the organization's own story, not universal symbols.** Widely shared symbols carry generic meaning and fail to distinguish; personify traits only where they fit the character and audience. Confidence: low. Sources: B71 p.84, 96
- **Map rival colors and pick one competitors cannot claim.** Many sectors cluster on the same hue (blue in finance and travel), so choose a distinctive signature color, while keeping sector conventions where trust depends on them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B76 p.38, 41, 88; B05 p.60-61; B19 p.60-61, 94
- **Follow sector conventions where trust is at stake.** Established professions read trust through restrained, traditional treatments; deviate only with an alternative that still feels sympathetic to the field. Confidence: low. Sources: B19 p.60-61, 94
- **Control the proportion of brand colors, not only the colors.** The ratio between hues changes how a pair reads; fix a dominant color and an accent share. Confidence: medium. Sources: B76 p.36-38; B90 p.69-70
- **Grow a brand palette by transforming its colors, not adding new ones.** Derive lighter, darker, brighter and duller versions of the core hues and their complement; a faint outside accent is tolerable only if too weak to disturb the whole. Confidence: low. Sources: B68 p.113
- **Test color associations with the audience.** Most hues carry both positive and negative readings and meanings shift by market; no fixed rule holds. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.191, 197-199; B37 p.140, 182; B71 p.114
- **Avoid tying a long-lived brand to a fashionable color.** Trend colors date quickly, and a signature color loses distinctiveness when everyone adopts it. Confidence: low. Sources: B37 p.163-165, 172-173, 231; B53 p.107
- **Treat a change to identity color or lettering as a loyalty risk.** People bond with brand color and type and read changes as statements; test identity changes with users before launch. Confidence: medium. Sources: B19 p.19, 93; B53 p.185
- **Publish exact brand values for every medium.** Give screen values separate from print conversions, plus logo color rules for light and dark grounds; finish (gloss or matte) shifts how a color reads. Confidence: medium. Sources: B76 p.126, 132, 136; B59 p.4-5
- **Extend print brand books into digital foundations before building the system.** Add accessibility, color semantics, grids, responsive rules, localization, motion and performance that print guidelines omit. Confidence: medium. Sources: B22 p.47-48
- **Allow the marketing site and the product to diverge around a shared core.** A bold promotional site and a calm app may vary spacing, sizing and typefaces while keeping the same brand color, logo and capitalization rules. Confidence: low. Sources: B22 p.47
- **Hide the logo to check that the system carries the brand.** When a screen without its logo no longer reads as the brand, the visual system is failing at its job. Confidence: low. Sources: B35 p.129
- **Show the logo on every page, legible at its smallest size.** Use approved variants, keep it near the top, set a minimum size (one guide uses 125 px in its largest dimension), and redraw the favicon at 16 px rather than shrinking the full mark. Confidence: low. Sources: B59 p.4; B47 p.212-213
- **Map each brand adjective to a motion quality.** An energetic brand gets overshoot and follow-through, a playful one gets spring, a decisive one gets ease-in-out, a calm one keeps travel short, and a stable one fades or blurs instead of moving. Confidence: medium. Sources: B42 p.180-184
- **Audit existing motion before writing motion guidelines.** Record every animation and state, group by purpose, and check easing and timing against the brand personality. Confidence: low. Sources: B42 p.176-179
- **Make a voice interface sound like the brand.** An assistant speaks for the company, so a professional service should not sound chatty. Confidence: low. Sources: B73 p.50-51
- **Keep the frame quieter than the work it presents.** Fashionable faces, special finishes and loud palettes around a portfolio or product dilute what is shown; a restrained system and one simple device (such as one color per line of business) tie a family together. Confidence: low. Sources: B84 p.133-137
- **Use ultralight weights sparingly and only large.** Very thin type can signal luxury but needs large sizes and strong contrast to show its strokes. Confidence: low. Sources: B31 p.358
- **Avoid overused display faces that read as cliche.** Heavily used display treatments and gimmick ligatures now read as parody. Confidence: low. Sources: B31 p.150, 202
- **Remember that appearance drives credibility within half a second.** People judge credibility in about 300 to 500 ms and rank visual design as a top trust factor, so polish the first screen. Confidence: medium. Sources: B44 p.143; B86 p.275
- **Sell inclusive products on fitness for purpose.** Market comfort and ease, not inclusivity as a label; buyers respond to the benefit. Confidence: low. Sources: B69 p.56-58
- **Base a personal brand on a few core values checked against outside feedback, under your own name.** Narrow to about three values, compare self-view with what others say, and keep one name across profiles so the trail survives job changes. Confidence: low. Sources: B05 p.24, 34-40, 66-67, 85; B13 p.237-240
