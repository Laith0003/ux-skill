# color

- **Never let color be the only carrier of meaning.** Pair every color signal with a label, icon, shape, pattern or position; red against green with nothing else fails the largest group of color-deficient readers (WCAG 1.4.1). About 8 percent of men and under 1 percent of women have a color-vision deficiency, deuteranomaly being the most common form. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.726-727, 732-733; B26 p.438, 449; B20 p.832-834; B86 p.279, 284; B18 p.173; B91 p.33, 36-37, 59; B80 p.106-107, 111, 117; B58 p.111, 137, 299; B08 p.201; B02 p.74
- **Meet the WCAG contrast floors and measure them, never eyeball them.** Body text needs 4.5:1 and large text (24px regular or about 19px bold) 3:1 at AA; AAA asks 7:1 and 4.5:1; controls, graphics and custom hover, active and focus states need 3:1 (1.4.11). Disabled controls, logos and incidental text are exempt. Confidence: high. Sources: B91 p.59, 243, 263; B18 p.135-136, 140, 254; B80 p.55, 105, 118, 123-129; B02 p.74, 137
- **Judge every color in its real surround, never as a lone swatch.** The same sample shifts in apparent lightness, hue and size on different grounds, and the shift grows with the size and difference of the ground; test brand and status colors on every background they will sit on. Confidence: high. Sources: B90 p.15, 27, 31, 35, 46; B70 p.18, 91; B89 p.367; B68 p.31, 79; B37 p.18-19
- **Measure lightness instead of trusting the eye across hues.** In repeated classroom tests most judgments of which of two hues was darker were wrong, and hue does not predict lightness (a yellow and a purple can share a gray level); compute L in a perceptual space before claiming two colors contrast. Confidence: high. Sources: B90 p.38-39; B68 p.23; B46 p.735
- **Separate adjacent colors by lightness, not by hue alone.** Neighbors of equal lightness lose their shared edge (vanishing boundary), and contrasting hues of equal lightness make text and edges vibrate or shimmer; give every boundary that must read a clear lightness step. Confidence: high. Sources: B90 p.88-90; B76 p.182, 186, 188, 191; B70 p.41-42; B20 p.832-834
- **Design in grayscale first, then add color as a redundant code.** Settle hierarchy, grouping and message in black and white so the design survives deficient vision, grayscale printing and forced-color modes; color then highlights instead of decorating. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.726, 732-733; B20 p.832-834; B08 p.201; B76 p.14; B18 p.135-136
- **Review every design in grayscale and in a color-vision simulator before shipping.** Simulators expose unreadable text, invisible calls to action and indistinguishable links; then confirm ratios with a contrast tool. Confidence: high. Sources: B80 p.114, 120-122; B86 p.284; B18 p.135-136; B91 p.34-40
- **Keep the number of colors on a screen small.** Text screens should carry roughly four simultaneous colors at most, with two or three preferred, and a chart no more than six; people can name only four or five colors in isolation but can tell apart six or seven side by side. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.193-194, 729, 733-734, 746-747; B26 p.432, 453; B59 p.8; B91 p.33
- **Spend saturated color sparingly.** One or two saturated accents over muted or neutral tones direct attention; adding more strong colors dilutes the message, tires the eyes and makes a calm, long-use or high-stress tool feel loud. Confidence: high. Sources: B86 p.283, 302; B76 p.14; B10 p.123, 171; B26 p.432; B53 p.283
- **Balance a palette through quantity, not only through hue choice.** Almost any colors combine if their areas are right; area and recurrence set dominance, and the same palette in different proportions reads as a different mood. Light hues need small areas, dark hues large ones to balance (classic ratios give yellow to violet about 1:3). Confidence: high. Sources: B90 p.69-70; B70 p.22, 64; B76 p.157; B68 p.113
- **Size an accent against the lightness of its context.** A light accent among light tints needs a large area to hold its place, while among dark tones a small area is enough; set accent area from the surrounding lightness, not from a fixed share. Confidence: medium. Sources: B70 p.64; B90 p.69
- **Build tonal ramps with perceptually equal steps.** Equal physical increments give shrinking perceived steps (Weber-Fechner), so generate ramps in a perceptual space and check them by squinting for an even flow; uneven jumps read as errors, and adjacent gray steps that must be told apart need about a 20 to 30 percent lightness gap. Confidence: high. Sources: B90 p.80-81; B68 p.81-82, 97; B46 p.741; B76 p.189; B22 p.169-171
- **Predefine a fixed ramp per color and name steps numerically.** Give greys, primaries and accents each a stepped scale chosen in advance (about 7 to 10 steps, base in the middle, room to insert steps), use the same numeric names in design and code, and never produce ad hoc shades with lighten or darken calls. Confidence: high. Sources: B47 p.29, 138-151; B22 p.165-172; B59 p.8-10; B68 p.48
- **Keep the steps a user must tell apart to about seven.** Roughly seven lightness or intensity levels are what people hold in visual memory, so a ramp's working roles should not depend on distinguishing more. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.23, 48; B22 p.170-172; B46 p.733-734
- **Specify colors by value, never by name.** People picture different colors for one word and names drift over decades; tokens carry exact values and encode hue, lightness and chroma, with descriptive names only as labels. Confidence: high. Sources: B90 p.29; B68 p.45-49; B37 p.31-32, 255-256; B22 p.165-166
- **Describe and adjust lightness and chroma as independent axes.** Pale is not bright and dark is not dull: a dark navy can be highly saturated and a pale straw very dull, so a scale must specify both, and contrast changes in one usually drag the other. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.92; B90 p.35, 60
- **Mute a hue along its own hue path, not by mixing in black or gray.** Black deadens a color while a touch of its complement lowers intensity and keeps it alive; deriving a muted step from one hue plus one modifier avoids muddy results. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.75, 94, 99-100, 146; B70 p.50-51
- **Raise chroma or shift hue slightly at the light and dark ends of a ramp.** At constant saturation the extreme steps look washed out; adjust saturation as lightness leaves the middle, and if rotating hue to brighten or darken, stay within about 20 to 30 degrees. Confidence: low. Sources: B47 p.152-157
- **Bridge two grounds with a genuine intermediate color.** To relate to both fields, the linking color must fall between them in hue and lightness; when none fits, change the grounds and leave the bridge alone. Confidence: low. Sources: B90 p.44-45, 56-57
- **Expect neutral grays to take on the complement of their surround.** A gray on yellow reads violet-tinged, on blue orange-tinged; keep UI grays neutral by giving them a lightness different from adjacent chromatic colors, and commit to one gray temperature. Confidence: high. Sources: B70 p.20, 42, 52; B68 p.32; B90 p.46-47
- **Damp unwanted simultaneous contrast with a lightness or hue shift.** When a thin dark element on a saturated field reads as the complement, a small hue shift in the dark or a clear lightness difference cancels it; prototype the real pairings before committing. Confidence: medium. Sources: B70 p.55; B90 p.15-16, 48-49
- **Watch small areas and thin strokes: they lose hue.** Full color sensitivity needs areas larger than about a 3 inch square at normal distance; small dots and lines mix optically with neighbors, so thin colored lines, small icons and small colored text need more lightness contrast, and colored text benefits from 1 to 4 points more size. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.732, 741; B90 p.59; B70 p.66
- **Watch for the Bezold effect in repeated patterns.** Changing one color in a repeated pattern or a dense series can shift the whole combination, so judge striped tables, tiled charts and icon sets as a whole. Confidence: low. Sources: B90 p.36-37, 59
- **Treat harmony schemes as starting points, not answers.** Complementary, split, triadic, tetradic and analogous schemes give structure, but real quantity, light and reading order change them; pick the chord that fits the message, not personal taste. Confidence: high. Sources: B90 p.67-68; B70 p.22, 72-74, 85; B68 p.104, 208; B76 p.182; B78 p.77-80
- **Keep analogous runs and gradients inside a narrow hue window.** Three to five neighbors on a 12-hue wheel stay in sequence and crossing to a hue that contains the opposite breaks the run; two-hue gradients look best within about 30 degrees. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.42-44; B70 p.49; B47 p.229
- **Expect complementary and saturated pairs to vibrate.** Opposites at equal intensity side by side clash, which suits deliberate tension and energy only; analogous palettes feel calm, complements active, pure primary triads harsh. Confidence: medium. Sources: B76 p.182, 188; B37 p.35, 97-98; B53 p.38, 403; B70 p.50-51
- **Never set pure saturated red against pure blue, or yellow against purple.** These pairs cause chromostereopsis (the parts sit at different apparent depths) and strain; also avoid pure blue for text and thin lines. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.832-834; B46 p.737-738, 752-754
- **Set dark text on a pale, muted background.** Near-black type over off-white or pale gray is most legible; keep strong saturated color out from behind text, keep page backgrounds very light (about #eee at the darkest) and body text at about #333 or darker. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.737-738; B59 p.8; B80 p.124
- **Never put gray text on a colored background.** Lower contrast with a same-hue color of adjusted lightness and chroma; gray or white at reduced opacity looks washed out or disabled. Confidence: low. Sources: B47 p.42-44
- **Give reversed text extra weight and a little tracking.** Pale type on a dark ground fills in and thins out, so use a heavier weight, slightly larger size and small positive letter spacing. Confidence: high. Sources: B88 p.139; B19 p.29; B18 p.135-136
- **Prefer near-black and near-white to the absolute extremes for large areas.** Pure black removes depth and texture cues and the absolute extremes punch holes in a composition; tinted darks and off-whites read richer, and a slightly tinted neutral ground ties a palette together. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.139, 164, 169-171; B37 p.259-260; B53 p.89, 143, 146
- **Tint shadows and atmospheric layers instead of using plain black or gray.** Shadows carry reflected and complementary hues, and distance makes things paler and cooler; tinted shadows and lightened, cooled back layers read more natural. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.29, 134, 168; B70 p.81-82; B37 p.177, 265
- **Read color depth as relative and set it with lightness against the ground.** Warm and saturated colors usually advance and cool or dark ones recede, but on black the light hues come forward and on white the dark ones do; when depth order matters, control it with lightness, not with a warm or cool label. Confidence: medium. Sources: B70 p.45-46, 77-78; B92 p.79, 174; B76 p.186, 189; B90 p.85-87; B37 p.31
- **Check color meaning against the audience's culture.** White and purple signal mourning in some regions, red means luck in one market and danger in another, and even warning and normal-state colors are read differently; validate a palette's symbolism with the people who will use it. Confidence: high. Sources: B08 p.72-73, 328-329; B49 p.163, 271; B92 p.168; B71 p.114; B72 p.343; B26 p.453
- **State color symbolism only with the exact color and its neighbor.** An emotional reading depends on the precise chroma, tone and surround, so a claim like red means danger only holds for a specific red in context. Confidence: medium. Sources: B70 p.85; B37 p.121-122
- **Distrust single sensational color-psychology studies.** Calming or aggression-reducing color claims from small samples have not replicated; test emotional color claims with the real audience. Confidence: low. Sources: B37 p.31, 121-122
- **Expect higher chroma to raise arousal.** Saturated colors raise arousal and liking, red and violet are activating and green is low, which helps match color energy to the product. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.160
- **Reserve red for errors, stop and critical actions.** Red reads as danger and stop, so do not spend it on decoration; the green-for-go and red-for-stop button convention holds in most Western interfaces. Confidence: medium. Sources: B37 p.138-140; B35 p.264; B20 p.832-834
- **Put hazard and must-notice items in high-salience colors.** Orange or yellow with black reads as warning, and orange stands out against blue-gray surroundings; reserve these for alerts and things people must find or avoid. Confidence: low. Sources: B53 p.183, 213, 221; B37 p.96
- **Never use blinking color for emphasis.** Blinking red is distracting and a seizure risk; keep any flashing under the WCAG three-flashes threshold (2.3.1). Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.832-834
- **Give text links one consistent color that nothing else uses.** Never color non-clickable headings in the link color, and make links distinguishable without color by an underline or another cue, since a colored link can vanish into body text in grayscale. Confidence: high. Sources: B77 p.48-49; B80 p.116, 121
- **Never separate sections with background color alone.** High-contrast and forced-color modes strip backgrounds and pastel fills vanish for some readers; add borders, rules or space. Confidence: medium. Sources: B18 p.132, 135
- **Check contrast at the worst point of gradients, photos and striped rows.** Test text against both the lightest and darkest part of a gradient, put a near-opaque panel behind text on photos, and test both colors of alternating table rows. Confidence: medium. Sources: B80 p.113-117, 121; B83 p.582-583
- **Give each color group a defined role and keep codes consistent.** Primary colors for actions, neutrals for copy, accents sparingly for status; readers learn a color code fast, so inconsistent use reads as chaos, and limiting action colors keeps clickability clear. Confidence: high. Sources: B22 p.173-174, 213; B92 p.174-175; B76 p.41, 124; B77 p.48-49
- **Keep color codes few and distinct.** A broad palette loses its power as a code; four or five widely spaced hues are the most people identify reliably. Confidence: high. Sources: B76 p.41, 124; B46 p.733-734; B91 p.33
- **Expect colors that look alike to be read as linked.** Two similar colors among three group together and leave the odd one emphasized; reuse an accent deliberately to tie a call to action to its related section, and avoid accidental similarity. Confidence: medium. Sources: B92 p.170; B78 p.41-42
- **Keep a brand palette to one or two primaries plus a few supports.** Most identities stay at three colors or fewer, with two to four supporting colors matched in value; balance accent hues of equal value against one darker anchor. Confidence: medium. Sources: B76 p.142, 146-148; B05 p.60-61; B59 p.8
- **Set dominant colors before accents and record what you used.** Choose the dominant hues first, then add accents and vary their proportion, keeping a record so the palette stays consistent. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.157
- **Transpose a palette by keeping its lightness intervals.** A color group keeps its character when moved lighter or darker as long as the lightness steps between its members stay the same, which is the right test for a dark-mode or high-contrast variant. Confidence: low. Sources: B90 p.60
- **Give a palette range for different emotional content.** A system needs somber dark grounds as well as light ones so serious content never clashes with cheerful color. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.90
- **Constrain generated or dynamic color with a defined palette and rules.** When software picks colors, express the palette and its rules as parameters so output still looks designed. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.84
- **Make a new color system distinct from its environment and siblings.** Choose hues absent from surrounding signage or interfaces so the system stands out, and keep related products' palettes distinguishable. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.60, 64, 118
- **Never trust one screen, one light or one medium as the true color.** Screens differ by calibration, print runs darker than screen, sunlight washes out light-on-dark text and physical swatches never match pixels; define separate screen and print values and test in each target medium and lighting before sign-off. Confidence: high. Sources: B76 p.20, 52, 75, 124; B37 p.32, 123-124; B64 p.187; B59 p.4-5; B35 p.242; B53 p.333, 415
- **Audit color by sampled values on a neutral field.** Expectation overrides perception (color constancy), so reviewers misjudge colors they think they know; isolate a swatch on a neutral mid-light ground or read the sampled value. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.27-29, 135-137; B53 p.173; B90 p.40-41
- **Use additive, not pigment, logic for screen color.** Light mixtures lighten toward white and pigment mixtures darken toward gray, so screen blends and overlays behave differently from print. Confidence: low. Sources: B90 p.53-54; B70 p.66
- **Expect large saturated fields to leave after-images.** Staring at a strong color for 10 to 30 seconds leaves its complement on the next view, so avoid full-screen saturated fields before content the user must read. Confidence: low. Sources: B90 p.15-16, 48; B68 p.106-109; B76 p.188
- **Keep symbolic UI flat and reserve modeled shading for imagery that needs realism.** Large areas of perfectly even color can feel one-dimensional and slight variation adds depth, but keep that variation quiet so it never fights the content. Confidence: low. Sources: B37 p.92, 186-187; B53 p.207; B59 p.8
- **Surround a signature color with generous white to make it read premium.** A single strong color on plenty of white stays clean, while colored grounds around it get difficult; restraint can signal more value than ornament. Confidence: low. Sources: B76 p.130-132; B53 p.283
- **Prefer colors that survive time and transmission.** A first impression is not enough; check that brand colors hold up across compression, themes and repeated reproduction. Confidence: low. Sources: B37 p.83, 100, 215; B53 p.38-39, 143, 152-153
- **Use web-native brand values instead of converted print swatches.** CMYK or spot-color conversions give poor screen colors, so define screen values separately. Confidence: low. Sources: B59 p.4-5
- **Give AR labels a backing plate and high contrast.** Labels over real scenes vanish against bright or busy backgrounds without a solid or translucent plate, larger size and saturated color. Confidence: medium. Sources: B83 p.582-583, 719
- **Make physical controls contrast with their housing.** Contrasting buttons are found faster, and very saturated packaging can read as low quality. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.329
