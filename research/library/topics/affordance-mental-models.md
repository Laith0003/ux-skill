## Conceptual models

- **Give people a correct conceptual model through what the product shows.** Users learn a system only from its visible structure, labels, feedback and help, so present a model that matches how they think about the task, never the code, database or one-control-per-component layout; never make people learn an idea that lives only in the builders' view of the system. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.42-46; B26 p.49-50; B20 p.335-339; B32 p.124, 128; B06 p.30-31
- **Bridge both gulfs on every step: what can I do, and what just happened.** Use signifiers, constraints and mapping as feedforward and clear feedback after each action; walk each task step asking whether people will try the right thing, notice the control, connect it to their goal and see progress, since a no on any of these predicts a learnability problem. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.52-53, 83; B06 p.30-31; B89 p.131, 137
- **Correct wrong mental models with explicit status.** People assume more input means faster results (turning a thermostat to maximum, pressing a crossing button repeatedly); state what the system is actually doing, for example that heating is on until the target is reached. Confidence: high. Sources: B02 p.151-152; B20 p.335-339; B57 p.87
- **Expect mental-model mismatch to exclude more people than physical limits do.** In one study 60 percent of set-top box difficulties were cognitive (against 40 percent for older analogue sets), caused by unfamiliar paradigms; only observing users reveals them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B32 p.169-172
- **Collect the target culture's mental models instead of shipping your own.** Products embody their makers' culture by default; tasks themselves can differ by country (locating a destination by landmark or crossroads rather than postal address), and images change meaning with context and culture. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.68, 96-97, 247; B71 p.78, 80
- **Rules that cannot be written down cannot be learned.** Map each interaction's rules in a diagram, and depart from an established convention only when the alternative is clearly and immediately superior. Confidence: low. Sources: B14 p.70-71, 76

## Memory: recognition over recall

- **Put knowledge in the world so people recognize rather than recall.** Show names instead of codes, list the available options, keep help on screen while people act on it, and expose voice and gesture commands with feedforward because those sets are invisible by default; people recognize roughly 100,000 words but can recall only 2,000 to 3,000. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.108-109, 117-118, 216; B02 p.138, 146; B89 p.368-369; B83 p.182; B44 p.122; B46 p.107-108
- **Plan for 3 to 5 items in working memory, and apply that limit to recall, not to visible lists.** Current estimates put working memory at about 3 to 4 chunks lasting 5 to 30 seconds; numbers of 9 or 10 digits already strain it, so chunk them; the older 7 plus or minus 2 figure concerns unaided recall and does not cap a menu the user can see. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.102-103, 110; B46 p.107; B02 p.141-142; B11 p.100

## Signifiers and affordances

- **Signify where and how to act, not only that action is possible.** On a touch screen the whole surface affords touch, so each target and gesture needs a perceivable cue through shape, color, position, grouping or a familiar pattern; every function needs its own signifier, or most users never find it. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.11, 26-27; B66 p.27, 29; B11 p.112; B54 p.36; B40 p.33
- **Make every page self-evident and every clickable thing obviously clickable.** Someone arriving for the first time should understand a page's purpose and operation almost instantly; flat styling strips cues, so text fields keep visible borders and actionable elements stay distinct from labels. Confidence: high. Sources: B77 p.22, 26, 29, 48, 162-165; B24 p.316-317; B11 p.112
- **Remove false affordances.** Anything styled as a button has to behave as one, a non-interactive element must not look clickable, and visual devices such as a rule at the fold must not suggest the page has ended. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.690-692; B14 p.48, 50; B11 p.112
- **Make hidden gestures and capabilities visible.** Swipe-only and zoom-only features go unused without cues; show page dots, a partly visible next card, content running off the edge or a brief motion nudge, never rely on a cursor change alone, and remember that gestures are learned, not innate, and hidden ones mostly cause accidental triggers. Written prompts to swipe are largely ignored. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.33-35, 123; B24 p.66-68, 319, 322; B42 p.126-129; B66 p.30, 122-123; B58 p.200-201
- **Treat notes, arrows and labels that users add as design bugs.** If a simple control needs a written instruction, the shape is failing to explain itself, so redesign the shape. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.29, 34, 140; B20 p.692-695
- **Give hidden functions their own visible control and a one-step way home.** People afraid to explore never found lock, silent or voicemail functions until each had a dedicated control; assume nothing about novices, some of whom do not know scroll bars or double-click, and show states such as power saving that would otherwise look like off. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.79-80, 134-139; B58 p.305-308
- **Check each control for four kinds of affordance.** It must be useful (functional), understandable from its label (cognitive), visible and legible (sensory) and easy to hit (physical); failing one undermines the rest. Confidence: low. Sources: B20 p.679-689

## Mapping, constraints and consistency

- **Make each trigger do the same thing every time and label the same thing the same way.** Controls whose behavior depends on hidden context, or terms that change between screens and devices, stop people forming a reliable model; varying the position of titles across screens nearly doubled thinking time. Confidence: high. Sources: B14 p.45; B32 p.171; B46 p.78
- **Avoid modes, or show them persistently.** One shortcut that saves in a mode and sends in another produces mistakes; when a mode changes behavior (silent, editing, equalizer), keep a visible state indicator and make toggled states read clearly against neighboring keys. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.784-785, 802-803; B14 p.21, 36; B78 p.15-16
- **Lay out controls like the things they control.** Best is the control on the object, next beside it, then in the same spatial arrangement; a single row of controls for a grid of targets guarantees errors, and in 3D, objects must move and rotate in the same direction as the hand. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.36, 121-123; B83 p.623-624
- **Use constraints so the correct action is effortless and the incorrect one cannot happen.** Combine physical, cultural, semantic and logical constraints so a novel item works without instructions, and let people see physical limits before they attempt the action. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.130-132, 137; B57 p.90-91
- **Keep spatial models stable across transitions and touchpoints.** Panels enter and leave from the same side with the same timing, layers scale toward or away from the viewer, and channels stay consistent so people do not have to reorient at each step. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.41; B42 p.86-93
- **Make controls with different outcomes look and feel different.** Identical controls for opposite effects invite fatal slips; shape or style code them. Confidence: low. Sources: B06 p.44; B78 p.15-16

## Conventions

- **Innovate on content, imitate conventions.** Keep logo, home, search, cart and account tools where people expect them (add an explicit Home link on mobile, where the logo is rarely used that way), keep well-known icons and gestures (gear, star, magnifier, pull to refresh) for the meanings people already attach to them, follow platform button order, and introduce novel UI only with a traditional fallback, in small steps. Confidence: high. Sources: B23 p.74, 196, 478; B24 p.363-364; B57 p.86-88; B44 p.50, 99; B78 p.67-69; B14 p.48, 50
- **Make exploring safe with undo and a dependable Back.** People learn by experimenting when mistakes are free; unexpected pop-ups, abrupt sound, discarded input and an erratic Back button all deter it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B86 p.32-33; B66 p.27, 29
- **Expect some users to pick the action before the object.** Commands disabled until something is selected repeatedly confused people; explain what is needed or allow either order. Confidence: low. Sources: B89 p.329, 335
- **Prefer standard controls and direct manipulation on touch devices.** Act on content directly instead of through tiny buttons, but avoid custom toggles whose state is unclear. Confidence: low. Sources: B04 p.328-339
- **Place actions near what they act on and enlarge important small targets.** Shorter distance and larger hit areas cut errors, especially for people with motor impairments. Confidence: low. Sources: B11 p.99, 111-112
- **Expect appearance to set beliefs about ease and quality before use.** Few buttons read as easy, many as powerful, and material finish as quality, and such first judgments survive even after people read the facts. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.325

## Automation, realism and immersive spaces

- **Choose clearly natural or clearly magic interaction, not half-realism.** Interaction that is only partly realistic frequently underperforms both ends of the scale, so deliver only the realism the task needs, keep borrowed physical cues (shadow, overlap, sliding) physically consistent, and avoid near-human faces or voices unless the unease is intended. Confidence: high. Sources: B83 p.641, 706, 708; B54 p.37-38, 68; B75 p.58, 386-390
- **In rich 3D or XR scenes, mark which objects are interactive.** Early failed attempts teach people that nothing responds; use a small reticle that signals interactivity, show controller, ray and cursor together, and add objects of known size where distance is hard to judge. Confidence: medium. Sources: B83 p.153, 546; B48 p.50-51, 150-151
- **Show automated systems' current state, next step and a way to stop.** Let people preview a plan or result, choose to start it and keep steering while it runs. Confidence: low. Sources: B21 p.69, 90, 182
