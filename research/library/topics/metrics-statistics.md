## Usability metrics and targets

- **Give each critical task measurable criteria (baseline, target, minimum) and stop iterating once they are reached.** Choose the handful of tasks that most users perform most frequently, write success criteria specific to each, and benchmark against a competitor or the prior version (if a competitor reaches 70 percent success, aim for 80 with 70 as the minimum). A target table records the user role and goal, then the measure, instrument and metric, then baseline, target and observed result. Look at the spread and not only the mean, never set a target of zero errors, and read a pass in the first round as a hint that the targets were too easy. Sample targets: buying a ticket drops from 3 minutes to 2.5 with under 1 error, and first impression rises from 7.5 to 8 out of 10; 95 percent error-free by attempt three, 80 percent unaided, under 10 minutes, 90 percent satisfied. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.397-424, 592, 624-625; B30 p.222-225; B89 p.230; B58 p.89
- **Use a 70 percent task-success bar in early rounds, iterate toward 95, and demand 100 only where failure causes harm or data loss.** Reaching 100 percent cost about 50 times as much as reaching 95 in one measured program. Under 50 percent success even with assistance signals a very serious problem. Confidence: high. Sources: B28 p.81, 288, 296-297; B30 p.224-225; B89 p.230, 294-295
- **Expect roughly a 30 percent measurable improvement after fixing most problems found.** Repairing 70 to 90 percent of reported problems usually lifted performance by about 30 percent; in iterative testing, tasks got 28 percent faster with 37 percent fewer problems, task time fell from 68 to 51 seconds, and success rose from 73 to 84 percent while time dropped from 113 to 57 seconds. Confidence: high. Sources: B89 p.125; B08 p.265; B46 p.35, 92
- **Treat usability as effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction measured together.** Report task success, time on task or click count, and a standardized satisfaction score (for instance 7 of 10 finished login, 7 seconds on average, satisfaction 77 out of 100); pair every efficiency measure with a quality check so that errors do not pay for speed. Confidence: medium. Sources: B11 p.49-51; B25 p.51-52; B20 p.397-424
- **Report assisted and unassisted success as separate numbers.** Keep completion with help, completion without help, and completion within the time benchmark distinct; never fold prompted successes into clean ones. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.126-127, 249, 287-288; B89 p.230
- **Assess learnability across repeated trials rather than on first use.** One redesigned system ran about 20 percent slower during the first half hour, 20 percent faster in the second, and at about one third of the old time once 90 minutes had passed. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.158-160
- **Set a maximum task time from expert estimates with generous slack.** Average several insiders' estimates and multiply by about 2.5; some tasks still take three times longer than predicted. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.119-120
- **When tracking tasks across versions, fold success, time, clicks and satisfaction into a single comparable score.** Turn each into a ratio of goal to median, give them equal weight (0.25 apiece) for a score between 0 and 1 (for example 0.635 versus a 0.75 target), hold the participant profile steady, have experts establish goal times on that same system, and follow up low-scoring tasks with formative testing. Confidence: low. Sources: B58 p.523, 527-528, 667
- **With very small samples, grade task speed on a coarse scale instead of timing it.** Offer 3 or 5 levels (such as quick, a little slow, very slow) along with a distinct value for failure, and rely on averages only when comparing tasks or designs. Confidence: low. Sources: B03 p.331-332

## Sample size

- **Run formative usability tests in several small rounds, with about five people for each distinct user group.** Four or five people in each audience cell surface roughly 80 percent of problems; for a single round eight is safer, because the eighth participant may expose a grievous error that the first four did not. No qualitative study should have fewer than five people. Confidence: high. Sources: B28 p.110-111, 164; B17 p.39; B89 p.58-59
- **Size a survey from the margin of error you need: roughly 385 responses for a 5 percent margin either way at 95 percent confidence.** Above a population of about 10,000 the number hardly moves (370 to 377 for 10,000 to 20,000, 384 for a million), and a 3 percent margin either way takes about 1,066. It falls when the proportion you expect sits well away from 50 percent (73 when 95 percent is expected). Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.139; B30 p.232, 271; B44 p.336-337; B03 p.368; B06 p.101
- **Estimate problem discovery with found(i) = N x (1 - (1 - L)^i).** L (share of problems one user or evaluator finds) ranged 19 to 51 percent, mean 34, across six studies, so five is a typical answer, not a universal one; low-L products need more people. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.58-59
- **Give every analyzed subgroup its own full sample.** A survey that will be split by segment needs the target sample in each segment; at least 100 per audience, 300 to 500 respondents for basic quantitative work and a couple of thousand for advanced techniques. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.368; B17 p.39-40; B58 p.501-502
- **Halving a margin of error takes four times the sample.** Plus or minus 20 percent at n = 20 becomes about 10 percent at n = 80; a confidence interval is two margins wide. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.36-37
- **Small samples still support valid statistics when you use small-sample methods.** The rule that statistics need 30 or more users is a myth: with the right procedures, even 2 to 10 users give usable intervals and tests, and the t distribution at 10 degrees of freedom is already close to its large-sample behavior (within 3.2 to 6.8 percent of z at 30). What a small sample cannot do is detect small differences. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.27, 43, 263-265; B32 p.139-140
- **Work out comparison sample sizes with a power term instead of a rule of thumb.** For 80 percent power add z = 0.842 (in one example this raised the count from 15 to 22 users). To show a rate of 65 percent or more when 55 is expected takes 36 users; a 70 versus 90 percent comparison takes 50 per group at 90 percent confidence and 80 percent power, 37 at 80/80 and 14 at 80/50; finding a SUS difference of 2 points (SD 4.1) takes 136 users, 68 in each group. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.87, 135-136, 143-147
- **Do not pick a quantitative winner from a prototype test.** Small tests give qualitative insight; choosing between designs on conversion or sign-up needs thousands to tens of thousands of users. Confidence: medium. Sources: B74 p.33; B57 p.110-111; B56 p.13
- **Use lab tests to find problems and large unmoderated studies to size them.** Six to eight lab users reveal problems but not how often they occur, and slowdowns around 0.1 second are invisible in small tests. Remote unmoderated studies need about 200 completes without segmentation, 400 to 800 to segment (target about 300); a remote first-click or benchmark test can reach 100+ participants with under an hour of setup. Confidence: medium. Sources: B58 p.501-502, 508; B89 p.71; B30 p.224-225
- **Expect wider variance from crowdsourced remote experiments.** Crowd panels reproduced lab results at about a sixth of the cost but with more spread, so budget a larger sample. Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.565-566
- **Include no fewer than three people from each sub-group in every qualitative round.** Discovery rounds use 5 to 20 people, with 20 ideal; validation rounds use 5 to 12 per round across several rounds. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.39
- **For controlled experiments, vary one factor and plan 10 to 12 participants in each condition.** An experiment designed to reach significance usually needs a total of 40 or more participants. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.62, 110, 304-305
- **Handle small samples of disabled participants carefully.** Standard tests break down under uneven attendance, fatigue and data that is not normally distributed: use repeated measures, report power and effect size, and present findings qualitatively when the numbers cannot support a test. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.139-140

## Confidence intervals and estimates

- **Attach a confidence interval to every point estimate.** Estimates from small samples always miss by some margin: with n = 5, the observed completion rate is on average about 11 points away from the true rate, and the 95 percent interval typically spans more than 30 points. Dashboard example: 60 percent success with a 95 percent CI of 47 to 72. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.36-37, 42, 53; B30 p.285-286
- **Report completion and conversion rates with the adjusted-Wald interval.** For 95 percent, add two successes and two failures and then compute the standard interval; the plain Wald interval covers far too little when n is under about 100 or the rate sits close to 0 or 100 percent (true coverage can drop to 50 to 60 percent), while the exact binomial interval is too conservative (a nominal 95 acts like 99). Worked case, 7 of 10: adjusted-Wald gives 39 to 90, exact gives 35 to 93, plain Wald gives 42 to 98. For 8 of 9: 54.3 to 99.9. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.38-41, 58
- **Adjust the point estimate of a small-sample completion rate.** Below n = 20 always adjust: use the Laplace estimate for observed rates of 90 to 100 percent, the raw proportion for 50 to 90, and the Wilson estimate for 50 or below. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.42
- **Know the critical values.** z = 1.64 for 90 percent, 1.96 for 95, 2.57 for 99; one-sided tests at alpha 0.05 match a 90 percent two-sided interval and alpha 0.10 matches 80. About 68, 95 and 99.7 percent of normal values fall within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations; a 50 percent result with a standard error of 3 points therefore covers 47 to 53 (1 SD), 44 to 56 (2 SD) and 41 to 59 (3 SD). Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.37, 40, 62, 293-294; B03 p.386-387
- **Compute the standard error of a proportion as the square root of p times q over n.** 400 of 1,000 gives 1.6 percent; 2,000 respondents give 1.1; 5,000 give 0.7. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.385-386; B94 p.36-37
- **Decide whether a goal is met from the interval bound rather than the point estimate.** A completion goal counts as met only once the lower bound is above it; a time goal only once the upper bound falls under it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.59-60, 62
- **Use exact small-sample binomial tests unless there are at least 15 successes and 15 failures.** The mid-p version is less conservative: 8 of 9 against a 70 percent benchmark gives exact p = 0.196 and mid-p = 0.118. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.62-66

## Task time

- **Summarize task time with the geometric mean below about 25 users and the median above.** Times are right-skewed (slowest users take 5 to 10 times as long as the fastest; 192 users gave median 71 s against mean 84 s; values 2, 2, 2, 2, 450 give median 2 and mean 91.6). Log-transform times before intervals and t-tests, then convert the bounds back to seconds. Example: a geometric mean of 1 min 54 s whose confidence interval runs from 1 min 37 s up to 2 min 14 s. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.46-50, 53, 71; B14 p.163; B28 p.289-292; B02 p.357; B30 p.285-286
- **Compare mean, median and mode before trusting any average, and always state N.** A gap between them signals outliers or a mixed population (for example experts plus students); one 16-minute session moved a mean to 4.4 minutes against a median of 3.0. Show range and standard deviation, and check whether an outlier represents a real minority. Report medians for skewed values such as income and age. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.378-380; B28 p.289-292
- **Expect large individual differences in speed.** Users at the 75th percentile were 1.8 times faster than those at the 25th for text editing, 2.4 for web use and 3.0 for programming; the best typists were twice as fast with a tenth of the errors. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.114-115
- **Estimate reading and listening time from standard rates.** Reading runs 250 to 300 words per minute; proofreading about 200 on paper and 180 on screen; listening 150 to 160; dictation about 105, falling to 25 once corrections are included. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.129-130
- **Use human performance models for timing estimates.** Model Human Processor cycles are about 80 ms perceptual (minimum 50), 90 ms cognitive and 70 ms motor for able-bodied users; with moderate to severe motor impairment about 100, 110 and 100 to 310 ms in bands. Visual store holds about 17 letters with a 200 ms half-life; unrehearsed verbal items fade after about 6 seconds. Confidence: medium. Sources: B32 p.142; B83 p.109, 141
- **Use Fitts's law and the Hick-Hyman law to predict pointing and choice time.** Movement time is a + b x log2(2D/W), so it rises as distance increases and falls as the target gets wider. Measured throughput was about 10.6 bits/s for the hand, 10.4 for a mouse, 5.0 for a joystick and 1.6 for a touchpad. Choice time increases with the logarithm of how many options there are. Confidence: medium. Sources: B83 p.81-82, 162
- **Match input devices to spatial tasks.** A six-degree-of-freedom tracker was 36 percent faster than a mouse for 3D rotation with no accuracy loss, and amplified rotation mapping was 13 percent faster than one-to-one. Confidence: medium. Sources: B83 p.376, 458

## Comparing designs and significance

- **Choose the statistical procedure from data type, goal and design.** Decide whether the data is binary or continuous, whether you are estimating or comparing, and whether the same people see both designs; that path selects the test. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.19-23
- **Pick the comparison test that fits the study design.** Use a two-sample t for different users and a paired t for the same users, an N-1 two-proportion test when the rates are independent, and, for matched rates, McNemar exact with mid-p. Rules of thumb: a two-proportion test wants 10 or more successes and 10 or more failures in each group (N-1 works when expected cells exceed 1, Fisher below that); McNemar chi-square needs over 30 discordant pairs; past about n = 30 normality no longer matters; unequal variance only matters if one SD is over twice the other; with two groups the degrees of freedom are (Na - 1) + (Nb - 1). Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.85-104, 113; B02 p.593
- **Keep statistical significance apart from practical significance.** Past about n = 100 even trivial differences turn significant, so look at the interval on the difference and ask if users would notice it; at very large scale a tiny gain may still count (a million people is 0.1 percent of a billion customers). Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.82-83; B61 p.447
- **Lock in tails, alpha and the analysis plan before any data is collected.** Default to two-tailed tests unless comparing against a benchmark; moving to one tail after looking at the data lifts the effective alpha from 0.05 to 0.075. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.259, 265-267
- **Choose alpha from what each type of error costs.** By convention p < 0.05 is used for publishing and p < 0.01 counts as stronger evidence, yet for product decisions an alpha of 0.10 or even 0.20 (80 percent confidence) may fit when a false alarm and a missed genuine improvement carry similar costs. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.137, 268-270; B02 p.593
- **Estimate how many false positives to expect across many tests.** With alpha 0.05, twenty tests carry a 64 percent chance of one or more false positives, and 100 tests produce about 5; weigh the count of significant results against that expectation. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.273-278
- **Report effect size alongside p.** Conventional thresholds are 0.2 small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.130; B32 p.139-140
- **Keep raw ratings for comparisons instead of collapsing them to top-box.** Converting ratings to agree or promoter categories throws away information and sensitivity; in one example the difference stopped being significant. Use top-box only as an additional reporting view. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.69-70, 117
- **Test the difference before calling a winner.** 3 versus 6 conversions out of 600 views looks twice as good but is not statistically significant. Confidence: medium. Sources: B27 p.211; B94 p.95-98

## Standardized questionnaires

- **Score SUS exactly.** Ten items alternate positive and negative on a 5-point scale. For odd (positive) items subtract 1 from the rating; for even (negative) items subtract the rating from 5; score an unanswered item as 3. Sum the ten contributions (0 to 40) and multiply by 2.5 for a 0 to 100 score. The result is not a percentage. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.482-483; B11 p.69; B06 p.98-99
- **Read SUS scores against norms rather than the middle of the scale.** Across hundreds of products the mean is 68, a C on a curved grading scale; work with percentile ranks. Reference means: about 67 for cell phones and about 70 for websites; scores of 90 to 100 earn an A. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.67, 75, 213, 219-221; B11 p.69; B20 p.482-483
- **Compute NPS from the 0 to 10 recommend question with fixed bands.** Promoters answer 9 or 10, passives 7 or 8, detractors 0 to 6; NPS is percent promoters minus percent detractors, ranging from -100 to 100 (never an average of the raw answers). Seventeen software products averaged 21, range -26 to 56. NPS is too distant from any one team's work to serve as its outcome metric. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.33, 71; B64 p.150; B25 p.51-52; B34 p.58-59
- **Combine SUS with observed behavior.** For websites SUS may come out high and it does not always follow task success; UMUX-Lite is a shorter instrument for the same perceived-usability construct. Keep surveys brief and validate the questions before launch. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.482-487; B11 p.69
- **Know the formats of other standard instruments before adopting one.** QUIS has 27 items on 0 to 10; AttrakDiff has 22 items on 7-point scales (summed 22 to 154, with a 28-item variant); USE uses 7-point scales; PrEmo shows 7 pleasant and 7 unpleasant figures; SAM shows 9 positive and 9 negative. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.480-493; B11 p.69
- **Keep one instrument and one scale across phases and over time.** Measure the same aspect (frequency, importance, satisfaction) with the same scale throughout, or use an established instrument such as SUS or NPS, and repeat the measurement on a cadence (at least twice a year) to see trends. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.150; B11 p.69; B55 p.168-171; B35 p.356

## Rating scales and question wording

- **Pick odd-numbered scales that have a neutral midpoint.** Agreement scales use 3, 5 or 7 points (3 or 5 is most common); task-difficulty scales use 3 or 5 levels with a distinct failure value, and avoid 2, 4 or 6. Satisfaction, difficulty, confidence and usefulness fit seven points well; label a 7-point semantic differential 3-2-1-0-1-2-3 so the numbers do not bias answers. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.331, 355; B28 p.235-236; B14 p.164; B58 p.506
- **Expect the scale's numbering to change the answers.** The same question put 13 percent of respondents in the bottom half on a -5 to +5 scale and 34 percent on 0 to 10; small wording changes in a prompt also change how people answer, so regression-test wording changes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.117; B41 p.235-236, 238
- **Group rating-scale points by meaning, not by the arithmetic midpoint.** Star ratings are not linear: 4 and 5 both indicate good, while 1 to 3 indicate dissatisfaction; on a 1 to 10 satisfaction scale count 1 to 4 as bad, 5 to 7 as neutral and 8 to 10 as good. Introducing a neutral class lifted one model's accuracy from 0.56 to 0.79. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.94-95, 148-149
- **When you do report top-box, define it.** Top-box counts only the most favorable answer over all respondents, which counters acquiescence; on 7-point scales top-two-box counts 6 and 7, and a brand-impact scale centers at 4 as no effect. Confidence: low. Sources: B57 p.100; B58 p.506, 511
- **Rank needs with an opportunity score.** Rate about 30 desired-outcome statements for importance and satisfaction, then compute importance + (importance - satisfaction); 9 + (9 - 3) = 15 is a strong opportunity. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.63

## Surveys: length, sampling and response

- **Hold a survey under 20 minutes and around 20 questions.** Budget roughly 5 minutes for the instructions plus 30 seconds for each question: about 30 questions is the limit, and 20 leaves room to spare. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.357; B11 p.69
- **Pilot every survey before launch.** Collect 5 to 10 pilot responses plus 2 or 3 sessions watched in person, then field for 1 to 14 days. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.346, 364; B11 p.69
- **Invite a random sample rather than posting an open survey link.** Open links that ask people to take the survey draw those with extreme opinions; instead invite a random share of visitors (for 1,000 responses out of 100,000 weekly visitors, invite 1 in 100) so the response rate and the audience that saw the invitation are known. Web surveys also leave out the offline population (about 6 to 7 percent) and anyone whose cookies or pop-up blockers stop the survey. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.371-374; B30 p.231-235
- **Treat a low response rate as a bias warning, not just a smaller sample.** Response below about 20 percent is doubtful and around 1 percent is close to worthless; open web surveys often get 1 percent or less, so draw a modest random sample (about 1,500) and work for a high response (about 70 percent) instead of chasing volume. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.372-373; B30 p.234
- **Read survey answers as beliefs, not facts.** People exaggerate, ask for everything, answer without an opinion, agree by default and misreport things like ad exposure; correlation inside survey data is not causation. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.390-391; B57 p.100
- **Prefer a chance at a larger reward over a small guaranteed one.** A 1 in 100 chance at a prize worth 100 times the flat amount draws more responses than the flat amount. Confidence: low. Sources: B03 p.364-365

## Reporting small-sample results

- **Below about ten data points, report counts rather than percentages.** Write 10 of 12 instead of 83 percent, since presenting 2 of 4 as 50 percent misleads. Put the raw numbers next to every percentage, prefer the median when data is skewed, and do not claim most or all without evidence to back it. Confidence: high. Sources: B02 p.354, 357, 392; B28 p.293, 296, 304-305; B94 p.42
- **Never tie consequences to a metric built from a handful of cases.** When individuals were scored on 25 to 30 data points, the scores swung from one year to the next almost at random (a quarter shifted by 40 points, and one moved from 6 to 96); a cohort in the thousands might justify that kind of judgment. Confidence: medium. Sources: B56 p.13, 113-116

## Severity and inspection

- **Judge problem severity on impact and frequency together.** One scheme scores 0 to 4 (cosmetic = 1, minor = 2, major = 3, catastrophe = 4 and must be fixed before release, 0 = no problem) and weighs frequency, how hard the problem is to overcome, persistence and market impact. Another gives severity a 1 to 4 score (irritant, moderate, severe, unusable) and frequency a 1 to 4 score (10 percent or less, 11 to 50, 51 to 89, 90 or more) and sums them; frequency equals the share of affected users multiplied by the chance they encounter it (10 percent x 50 percent = 5 percent). Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.72, 74, 294-295; B28 p.299-301
- **Average severity ratings from three or four independent raters.** One rater's severity is little better than chance (inter-rater correlation 0.24, rating SD 0.75); the chance of landing within half a point of true severity is 55 percent with one rater, 77 with two and 95 with four, and three raters put a problem in the right quartile 94.5 percent of the time. Rating a full list takes about 30 minutes per rater. Raters agreed on core versus taste issues for only about two-thirds of reports, where 80 percent is the floor for acceptable agreement. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.70, 73-80, 300-301
- **Use several evaluators for inspection, and keep user testing in the mix.** A cost model put the optimum at about four evaluators, and inspection costs far fewer hours per finding than testing, but user testing rated problems as more severe than walkthroughs, heuristic review or guidelines did, and an inspection that misses an expensive problem costs more than the test it replaced. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.57-60, 156, 237-244
- **Rank fixes by frequency.** Most failures usually come from a small share of causes (repairing the most frequently reported 20 percent of bugs eliminated 80 percent of crashes), yet feature use is distributed differently: people tend to use about 20 percent of the features, and each uses a different 20 percent, so aggregate usage alone is no reason to cut a feature. Confidence: medium. Sources: B78 p.30; B44 p.203-204; B52 p.53

## Analytics

- **Use analytics for what happened and research for why.** Low traffic to a page may mean it is buried, not unwanted; drop-off points and search logs form hypotheses to test with users, and assumptions drawn from data alone should be flagged. Confidence: medium. Sources: B23 p.215-216, 220; B35 p.126-127, 312; B55 p.103; B34 p.54; B63 p.281-283
- **Do not optimize for session length or time on site.** Metrics built on stickiness can reward users for being stuck; someone who finishes quickly and leaves may represent the best outcome, and task completion can overlook early success. Confidence: medium. Sources: B23 p.231; B63 p.281-283; B27 p.206-210
- **Never compare page views across a redesign; establish a baseline spanning one business cycle or more.** After a redesign the site is in effect a different one. Gather data over a period of weeks to months that covers no less than one complete business cycle, then keep measuring in the same way over time. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.472, 476-477, 481; B55 p.168-171
- **Use bounce-rate bands as a rough read.** Typical is 40 to 60 percent, above 65 is high, below 35 is low; one common definition ends a session after 30 minutes of inactivity. Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.301, 632
- **Each month, review the top queries and the top queries that return zero results.** Query logs capture needs in users' own words, and query frequency follows a Zipf curve: on one site, 14 queries accounted for 10 percent of searches, 98 for 30 percent, 500 for 50 percent and 7,877 for 80 percent, so fixing the head covers a large share. Confidence: medium. Sources: B04 p.48-50
- **Track pogosticking on search results.** To get the pogosticking score, take detail page views, subtract the detail views that came in from external search engines, and divide by the number of searches; users who did not pogostick reached their target 55 percent of the time, versus 11 percent for those who did. Confidence: medium. Sources: B04 p.91
- **Look at retention across windows that carry meaning, for example 7 or 28 days.** Confidence: low. Sources: B63 p.282
- **Shape navigation to session depth.** If most sessions are one or two pages, optimize for short hops; sessions around five pages need different wayfinding support. Confidence: low. Sources: B03 p.478
- **Read complaint volume as a direction, not a count.** Doubled complaints after a change signal a change for the worse, not twice as many unhappy users. Confidence: low. Sources: B03 p.489
- **Score content against a baseline with a weighted scorecard.** Pick quantifiable factors, give each a target value and a weight, combine at least two methods, and repeat the measurement to reveal trends. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.168-171
- **Treat old scroll-depth numbers as out of date.** In an early study, over half of users never scrolled, 42 percent reached the second screen and 1 percent got beyond seven; measure again on your own product. Confidence: low. Sources: B04 p.186
- **Audit content before estimating migration work.** Inventory data narrows the estimate range (80 to 120 hours narrowed to 80 to 100 once 20 percent of content failed migration criteria). Confidence: low. Sources: B39 p.57

## A/B testing and experiments

- **Keep A/B tests from standing in for understanding.** Split tests locate problems and judge fixes yet cannot explain causes; optimizing piece by piece can bloat an interface, and the tests need large samples, so dig in qualitatively when a result swings unexpectedly. Observing users fixed one activation flow and lifted completion 77 percent, a gain a six-week test with a few thousand people later confirmed. Confidence: high. Sources: B03 p.469-470, 481-482; B57 p.110-111; B27 p.82-83
- **Define the success metric and its expected signature before building.** Decide what measurable change means the design worked so results can be interpreted (a 3 percent feature conversion meant nothing without a benchmark); predict the pattern a change should create, such as a spend-over-10 promotion raising the share of orders above 10, and rule out seasonality or ad spend. Agree completion, dropout and satisfaction goals with stakeholders before launch. Confidence: medium. Sources: B27 p.21, 97-98, 213-214; B41 p.217-218; B25 p.51-52
- **Change one thing per A/B test.** Changing several elements at once prevents attributing the result; single-variable changes such as moving a button can go straight to a test without a lab session. Build pages by adding one element at a time from the minimum needed to sell (name, picture, price, buy button) and keep only what significantly improves conversion. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.135-136; B27 p.82-83, 199; B02 p.634-635
- **Run an A/A test and confirm the variants differ only in the feature under test.** Two identical variants should show no difference, which checks the traffic split; an accidental difference, such as a visible price label, sank one test (downloads dropped 64 percent). Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.634-635
- **Match traffic allocation to risk.** A 50/50 split gives the most power for a feature or checkout test; for a risky change, expose the smallest group that still yields reliable results, typically 1 to 10 percent of visitors. Big multivariate tests may run 40 or more combinations and require matching traffic. Confidence: medium. Sources: B27 p.196, 201, 224; B04 p.37-38; B79 p.225; B03 p.481-482
- **Test winners again together against the control.** Changes that each win alone may lose in combination, as when a red button lands on a background that was also made red. Confidence: low. Sources: B27 p.212-213
- **Judge short-term spikes against what follows.** A deep discount lifts revenue during the sale; measure the drop after it ends before calling it a win. Confidence: low. Sources: B27 p.211

## Choosing and guarding metrics

- **Measure outcomes, meaning changes in behavior.** A list of shipped features reveals nothing about value, and revenue or NPS sit too far downstream to attribute; use the AARRR funnel stages (acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue) to choose behaviors a team can actually move. Outcomes are not pass or fail: a 50 percent target that reached 42 still counts as a result. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.54, 57-59; B27 p.19-21
- **Never trust a single metric as proof that users are happy.** Engagement, retention, registration, revenue and support contacts can each be gamed; look for several moving consistently and talk to users. Confidence: medium. Sources: B27 p.206-210; B63 p.281-283; B34 p.54
- **Favor direct measures over proxies.** Once people discover which proxy drives a decision (follower counts, acceptance rates) they inflate it until it means nothing; a proxy that departs from the real target (cost in place of need, arrests in place of crime) builds existing inequality into the system, so describe honestly what is being measured. Confidence: medium. Sources: B56 p.51; B43 p.55-56
- **Segment before concluding from an average.** An aggregate score fell while every subgroup rose because the population mix changed (Simpson's paradox); segmenting a remote study by visit intent showed 34 percent success for product researchers against 67 percent for visitors checking what is new. Confidence: medium. Sources: B56 p.113-115; B58 p.501-502, 511-514
- **Ask what confounder sits behind any correlation.** Correlation in historical or survey data is not causation; look for the hidden variable (poverty, access, seasonality) before acting on a predictive signal. Confidence: medium. Sources: B56 p.135-136; B27 p.213-214; B03 p.390-391
- **Check what a metric leaves out.** A ranking that ignored price gave institutions a reason to raise it; review every KPI and dashboard for the cost or harm it silently omits. Confidence: low. Sources: B56 p.54-55

## Cost justification

- **Convert task success and time on task into money, using conservative inputs.** The revenue effect is potential sales multiplied by the change in success rate; the time saving multiplies seconds saved by the number of users, transactions per day, working days and hourly cost. Typical worked cases: moving from 70 to 75 percent success on a large sales base, or saving 15 seconds for 100,000 staff over 220 days, each worth seven figures annually. Lean low on every input. Confidence: high. Sources: B30 p.227-230; B20 p.882; B57 p.121; B52 p.43-44; B11 p.50; B46 p.34
- **Fix problems early: cost rises about tenfold per stage.** A problem costing 1 unit to fix in design costs about 10 in development and 100 after release; usability-related calls made up over 60 percent of help-desk volume across 24 products. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.35; B20 p.879; B89 p.244
- **Model benefit per locale with modest lifts.** Even a 0.5 to 1 percent gain in look-to-buy, cart completion or support calls repaid international usability work in about six months; domestic users outperformed international ones by about 46 percent on average across studies (shopping success 61 against 47 percent), so test each locale. Confidence: low. Sources: B08 p.16, 18, 245-246, 256, 262
- **Plan localization effort with throughput ratios.** Localization costs about a third of creating new content and 3 to 5 times plain translation; 20 to 30 percent of a site is typically redundant for translation memory; a translator covers 2,000 to 2,500 words a day and a reviewer about 10,000. Figures are dated. Confidence: low. Sources: B08 p.168-179

## AI and model evaluation

- **Never judge a model by accuracy alone when outcomes are imbalanced.** With 5 percent fraud a model scores 95 percent accuracy while catching nothing (0.1 percent gives 99.9). Report precision, recall, F-measure and a per-class confusion matrix: 1,000 cases with 50 fraud and 15 caught gives precision 42, recall 30, F 35 and accuracy 94.5 percent. State the metric definition with the number (top-3 versus top-5 accuracy). A conservative model at 88 percent accuracy missed 11 of 20 real problems. Confidence: high. Sources: B61 p.151-154; B43 p.215-216, 225-226; B51 p.365-367; B12 p.109; B63 p.281-283
- **Decide between precision and recall based on what each error costs.** Prefer precision when false positives do harm (flagging a loan default) and recall when coverage counts (music suggestions); weight classes by business cost (a defect that costs 10 times more gets weight 10; false negatives that cost 5 times more call for optimizing recall); at 70 percent precision, 35 of 50 retrieved items are relevant. Check the balance with users and set the threshold by expected value rather than accuracy. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.366-367; B61 p.36, 155; B12 p.105, 109, 114-115; B21 p.254
- **Factor in base rates before acting on an alert.** If the event is much rarer than one in a million, most flags will be wrong even with a very small false-positive rate. Confidence: medium. Sources: B43 p.183, 237
- **Compare against a simple baseline that people grasp.** On its own an error figure says nothing; set it beside current practice or a trivial rule such as the median, the base rate, the most popular item or human experts. In one large challenge the top entries scarcely beat a regression on four variables, and two features equaled a risk tool with 137 questions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B61 p.383-387; B43 p.45, 70-71, 77
- **Keep a truly held-out test set.** Testing on training data or leaking the answer inflates accuracy dramatically (a 97 percent claim fell to chance once fixed); watch for shortcut features. Confidence: medium. Sources: B43 p.27-28, 218-219
- **Confirm the test population resembles the real users.** A model trained on a single region or group can go badly wrong elsewhere; one tool's high-risk group reoffended at one tenth of the rate seen in its training data. Confidence: medium. Sources: B43 p.53-54
- **Evaluate decisions, not just predictions, and close the feedback loop.** A model accurate under the old process can harm once it replaces that process; if rejected people never feed back (false negatives never measured), the score defines its own reality. Instrument outcomes for people turned away and check whether outputs feed the model's own inputs. Confidence: medium. Sources: B43 p.46-48; B56 p.13-14, 28, 76, 111-112
- **Check whether a human in the loop really adds value.** In one study, people serving as final reviewers of AI recommendations reduced accuracy (69 percent versus 73 for the AI on its own); give humans judgment, ethics and tone, and measure what they contribute. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.415
- **Evaluate conversational AI over multi-turn exchanges.** Language models performed consistently worse in multi-turn conversations and did not recover after a wrong turn. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.175-176
- **Grow the evaluation set until the metric settles.** Compute the metric on progressively larger subsets and stop once its variability levels off (around 2,000, ideally 3,000 or more, in one case); a rough minimum of training data is 10 examples for each feature in each class when classifying, and 50 when doing regression. Confidence: medium. Sources: B61 p.119, 329-331
- **Translate model gains into user outcomes.** A 1 percent accuracy gain in an order-form prefill meant 20 fewer abandoned orders a day; state outcomes such as cutting documentation time 30 percent or 90 percent of users rating output accurate or easy to edit. Confidence: medium. Sources: B61 p.390; B16 p.360, 453; B30 p.227-230
- **Do not promise profile-based personalization before there is data for it.** Products with fewer than about 100 users cannot build meaningful personalization. Confidence: low. Sources: B16 p.47
- **Reward reports of near misses.** These happen much more often than actual failures and yield richer data; route them into incident records. Confidence: low. Sources: B21 p.197

## Conversational and voice metrics

- **Do not use recognition accuracy as the success metric.** A system at 90 percent accuracy automated 85 percent of calls while one at 97 percent automated 40; agree task completion, dropout and satisfaction targets before launch (for example 60 percent of started reservations completed, 75 percent of pilot utterances handled). Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.217-218, 230; B63 p.281-283
- **Track two conversation measures: sequence completion and interactional efficiency.** In the logs, tag base sequences and their expansions; the minimum number of turns is 2 x the completed base sequences, which is set against the actual turn count. High completion paired with low efficiency (0.83 and 0.49) means users get there but work too hard; healthy data looked like 0.88 and 0.92. Confidence: medium. Sources: B60 p.233-237
- **From day one, log every turn, failures included.** Capture the recognition result with its confidence and alternatives, the audio, the matches, timed errors, latency, state names and barge-in; sample complete sessions across a week or longer, and hand-transcribe a sample even if automatic accuracy is said to exceed 90 percent. Confidence: low. Sources: B41 p.226-229
- **Find dropout clusters and read the prompt before each one.** High dropout usually traces to a confusing prompt, an unhandled answer such as "yes", or no sense of progress. Confidence: low. Sources: B41 p.220

## Inclusion and cultural measurement

- **Size inclusion work from prevalence data.** About 1 in 4 adults lives with a disability in one national survey and 20 percent in another (34 percent mild, 45 moderate, 21 severe); 48 percent of disabled people are 65 or older, and capability loss is about 10 times as common at 75 plus as at 16 to 49. Confidence: medium. Sources: B91 p.170; B69 p.191-193
- **Run exclusion analysis on graded demands.** Rate product demands on capability scales (one method uses 10 demand levels across motion, sensory and cognitive groups) and compute overall demand from the three highest. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.192-194
- **Count accessibility legal exposure as part of the business case.** Web accessibility lawsuits in one market rose from 814 to 2,258 in a year (181 percent). Confidence: medium. Sources: B91 p.101, 170
- **Turn cultural interaction style into countable indicators.** Count words or items on each screen for information density, clicks or moves per unit of time for interaction frequency, and dialog steps for sequence, using several indicators for each dimension; group-level rules should rest on groups of 20 or more. The indicators separated two user cultures with better than 80 percent accuracy, although one predicted 4 to 1 interaction ratio actually came out at 1.4 to 1. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.168-171, 198-203
