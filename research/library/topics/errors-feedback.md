## Error messages: where and what

- **Put each error message directly beside the field or control that caused it, next in reading and tab order.** Errors gathered at the top of a page, in a modal, or in a status bar scroll out of view, are missed by screen reader users, and force people to remember the fix after dismissing them; when validation only ran on the server, fewer than half of people finished a form on the first try. Confidence: high. Sources: B18 p.92-93; B52 p.175-177; B27 p.148-150; B86 p.525-526; B46 p.602-604; B14 p.117; B24 p.302-303; B07 p.52
- **Name the item, the real cause and the corrective action in every error message.** A message that misstates the cause (a slash in a file name reported as a folder access failure) sends people in the wrong direction; state what is wrong with concrete nouns and say what valid input looks like. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.808-809, 814-815; B46 p.602-604, 633-635; B07 p.51-52; B51 p.267-268; B11 p.114; B36 p.234-235
- **Never show a bare code, a generic failure, or the word Error alone.** Codes like 404, 413 or internal network errors, and phrases like something went wrong or illegal command, leave people stuck; translate them into what happened, whether it is temporary, and what to do next, with technical detail one click deeper. Confidence: high. Sources: B58 p.306-307; B82 p.128-129, 179; B51 p.267-268; B02 p.209; B24 p.302-303; B44 p.66-67; B52 p.327; B23 p.566-567
- **Blame the system or the situation, never the person.** Messages that blame users lower their confidence and attention, and people who blame themselves stop reporting problems; repeated identical user mistakes are design defects to fix, not training issues. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.77-78, 172; B41 p.69, 112; B02 p.209; B26 p.641-642; B35 p.362; B01 p.15
- **Keep what the user typed when validation fails and point at the faulty part.** Clearing a field or a form forces re-entry; highlight the wrong segment (for example only the year in a date) and let the person fix it from the message without starting over. Confidence: high. Sources: B07 p.52; B79 p.77; B46 p.633-635; B82 p.130-131
- **Give every blocking message a way forward.** A message that only states the obstacle (a limit reached, a device cap, an expired list) must offer the action that resolves it, such as deleting expired items or adding the missing record. Confidence: high. Sources: B33 p.80; B58 p.306-307; B26 p.641-642, 677-678; B79 p.77
- **Validate each field when the user leaves it, and add a top summary with links only for long forms.** Inline on-blur checks catch mistakes while context is fresh; on submit of a long form, a summary that links to each faulty field helps, but never replaces the inline message. Confidence: high. Sources: B52 p.175-176; B23 p.566-567, 570; B86 p.525-526; B54 p.140-142
- **State rules and formats before entry, not after failure.** Show password rules, date formats and examples right next to the field so people do not learn the rules by trial and error. Confidence: high. Sources: B11 p.114; B18 p.100; B07 p.51-52; B40 p.32
- **Mark faulty fields with more than color.** Pair red with an icon, text, outline weight or a short motion, and do not reflow the layout so every field stays where the user left it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B86 p.525-526; B42 p.147-149
- **Announce errors and loading states programmatically.** A spinner or a red border is invisible to someone using a screen reader; use an alert or a live region that exists in the page before the first message is injected. Confidence: medium. Sources: B91 p.68; B80 p.98-100
- **Adapt error tone to culture.** Some cultures accept direct, stern messages and guided wizards, others expect supportive, distanced wording and choice, and some avoid help buttons to save face. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.166, 201, 257; B08 p.85, 326
- **Warn instead of block on optional fields that are hard to validate.** For things such as phone numbers from other countries, offer a soft hint and still let the person move on. Confidence: low. Sources: B07 p.52-53
- **Document every validation rule as a table beside one rendered error state.** Record id, trigger event (blur, submit), condition and exact message so copy and logic stay in sync. Confidence: low. Sources: B62 p.77
- **Apologize in proportion to the harm.** A playful tone may suit a trivial outage, but lost data calls for a plain, adult apology and a fast route to recovery. Confidence: low. Sources: B33 p.79; B02 p.209

## Prevent errors before reporting them

- **Make invalid input impossible with bounded controls and derived data.** Use pickers, steppers, lists and length limits when the valid set is known, and derive values the system already has (date from the clock, region from an address) instead of asking. Confidence: high. Sources: B26 p.640-641, 674-675; B21 p.181; B14 p.93-94; B46 p.633-635; B83 p.171; B40 p.29-30; B36 p.234-235
- **Run sensibility checks on values far outside the normal range.** Query amounts, doses or quantities that are orders of magnitude off the user's usual input, or entered in the wrong unit or currency field, instead of executing them. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.200-201, 207; B21 p.76, 79; B61 p.44
- **Mark suspect input instead of blocking it, and never auto-correct silently.** Underline possible problems in place, keep an audit trail, and when the system corrects something, show the change with an easy undo. Confidence: high. Sources: B26 p.360-362; B49 p.104, 107; B14 p.93-94
- **Tolerate messy input rather than returning a dead end.** Correct misspellings, accept common variants, and suggest alternatives instead of an empty no matches state. Confidence: high. Sources: B33 p.48; B46 p.633-635; B04 p.35-36
- **Surface constraints at the start of a flow, not at the final commit.** Permission, eligibility or capability blocks discovered at the last step waste the whole task; check them first. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.812-813; B15 p.80; B36 p.81
- **Show unavailable actions as disabled and explain what enables them.** Disabled controls should look disabled rather than vanish from main surfaces, and activating one should say which condition unlocks it. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.689-690, 782-783; B83 p.171; B89 p.116-118
- **Keep dependent fields and actions together, and separate destructive commands from common safe ones.** Putting delete or quit beside save invites slips; never put a reversing control where the triggering control was, since a double tap will hit it. Confidence: high. Sources: B02 p.47, 238; B52 p.146; B40 p.32
- **Guard every submit against repeated presses.** Disable the button and show a working state until the response returns, and make the server idempotent so impatient clicks never create duplicate orders. Confidence: high. Sources: B27 p.121-122; B44 p.132-133; B82 p.125
- **Choose safe defaults.** A default that picks a dangerous value turns into an error that looks human but is a design failure; default to the reversible, low-harm option. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.76
- **Avoid modes, and when a mode exists make it unmistakable.** If one control means different things in different states, users will misfire; distinguish states by format, not by memory. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.181-183, 215
- **Make similar procedures differ at their first step.** When a rare sequence starts like a frequent one, experts slip into the frequent path. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.179, 209
- **Never make the system depend on human memory.** Store and recall data for people; memory-heavy flows and forced frequent password changes push users into workarounds. Confidence: medium. Sources: B33 p.73, 81; B79 p.98
- **Design for pointer slips, which reviews tend to miss.** Mis-selection, double-click timing and accidental scroll clicks show up in testing far more than in expert inspection; enlarge hit areas and forgive near misses. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.332, 358; B32 p.127
- **Layer several defenses for high-harm actions.** Serious failures need multiple holes to line up, so combine constraints, checks and alerts, and warn when more than one safeguard is down. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.209-211; B21 p.76, 79
- **Reject impossible values but accept rare valid ones.** A negative age is an entry error to block; an unusual but real value must be accepted, so range checks must be tuned to reality, not to the common case. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.44; B21 p.79

## Confirmation and undo

- **Prefer reliable undo to routine confirmation dialogs.** Confirmations that appear for everyday actions become reflexive clicks and stop protecting at the very moment the risk is real; just do the action, report it without a blocking dialog, and let it be reversed (trash, undo send with a short delay, recovery bin held for weeks). Confidence: high. Sources: B26 p.216, 405, 681-683; B79 p.204-207; B86 p.38-39; B20 p.784, 810; B02 p.28, 47
- **Reserve confirmation dialogs for irreversible or costly actions.** Permanent deletion, payments, closing unsaved work and setting changes earn a confirmation; adding items, signing out and other reversible steps do not. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.784, 810; B24 p.198, 366-368; B11 p.113; B83 p.172; B46 p.633-635; B57 p.91-92; B89 p.117
- **Name the action and the object inside a confirmation.** People attend to the verb, not the target, so show which file, window or record will be affected, and state consequences and any data loss. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.205-207; B26 p.641-642; B33 p.64
- **Keep an undo history of at least 10 to 12 steps, labeled with the action.** Undo in meaningful chunks (a word, not a keystroke) and say what will be undone. Confidence: medium. Sources: B86 p.402, 439-440; B79 p.204-207
- **Offer a review step before consequential submissions.** Let people check and edit what they entered, then confirm what happens next once submitted. Confidence: medium. Sources: B18 p.100; B33 p.41
- **Confirm lightweight actions without interrupting the flow.** Use an inline change, a counter update or a short toast with undo (about five seconds), and keep the full confirmation screen for completed transactions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B24 p.303-311; B78 p.66
- **Let users stop or change a running automated process.** Long jobs need pause, modify and cancel, and every system-triggered automation needs a manual override and a way to switch it off. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.85; B14 p.64

## Feedback, progress and response time

- **Acknowledge every input within about 0.1 second.** When the response lags, people press again, and the second press may duplicate or undo the first. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.37, 72; B46 p.627-629; B32 p.164, 174; B21 p.84-86; B57 p.84-85
- **Match the wait indicator to the duration.** Under 0.1 s needs only the result; up to about 10 s a busy cue; 10 s to 1 minute a progress indicator; beyond a minute an estimate; on the web give something to look at after about 5 s. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.627-629; B20 p.810; B79 p.72
- **Make progress displays prove the system is alive and cancellable.** Show that work is happening, what remains, and a stop or cancel control beside the indicator that takes effect within one to two seconds and confirms it stopped. Confidence: high. Sources: B26 p.663-664; B86 p.436; B20 p.810; B83 p.176; B48 p.161-162
- **Put feedback at the point of attention.** Status lines along screen edges and spinners in distant corners go unseen; show feedback at the control or focus point, and animate it in if it must appear elsewhere. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.749, 806-807, 811-812; B57 p.84-85; B14 p.117; B07 p.52, 61, 136
- **Say what happened, not only that something happened.** Specific feedback beats a generic beep; silent completions cause repeated actions, and improved feedback was the single largest category of fixes in design reviews. Confidence: high. Sources: B89 p.116-118, 142, 149; B79 p.38-39; B83 p.176-177; B14 p.105, 107-108
- **Scale feedback prominence to frequency and weight.** Everyday actions earn a quiet response, infrequent or weighty ones a conspicuous response, and one distinct signal is reserved for the single critical state; too many signals get ignored or turned off. Confidence: high. Sources: B83 p.176; B79 p.202-203; B06 p.32; B14 p.111-112
- **Show results continuously during direct manipulation.** If a dragged item only updates on release, people cannot steer it; preview where a dropped or placed object will land and animate invalid drops back to a valid slot. Confidence: high. Sources: B83 p.148; B42 p.137-140; B48 p.75-76, 168
- **Keep status indicators users rely on, even if they seem unseen.** Removing an activity indicator led a third of users to interrupt work in progress within a week; make hidden state (supply levels, cancellations) visible. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.139-140; B72 p.155, 263
- **Signal latency honestly and keep any promised pause.** Announce expected waits with words, a sound or a loader, and when delays vary, hold a short minimum wait after announcing one so the stated pause is honored. Confidence: high. Sources: B41 p.78-79; B33 p.41; B16 p.254
- **Confirm success through more than one sense where sight may be absent.** Pair visuals with sound or haptics, and for hidden or mounted devices let audio and touch carry the feedback; every press in a touchless or immersive interface needs a sound or vibration. Confidence: high. Sources: B69 p.84, 118-119; B72 p.376, 383; B48 p.46, 149-150; B15 p.114-115
- **Pad time estimates on the high side.** Give a range or the upper likely bound so the real finish arrives sooner than promised. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.73; B26 p.663-664
- **Never auto-dismiss errors or confirmations that need reading.** Messages that vanish after a few seconds are missed and leave people anxious; errors persist until acknowledged, and timed dialogs must not decide on the user's behalf. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.291, 666, 680; B20 p.780, 816, 819
- **Do not count on prominence to get instructions read.** A large bold do-this-first sheet was ignored by most testers; design the flow so the critical step cannot be skipped. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.58-59
- **Keep earcons short, unique and mutable.** Use sound only for events people would want to know about while not looking, keep each under one second, never loop it, and let users mute it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B14 p.121-123; B46 p.631-632
- **Use surface-appropriate loading cues.** Typing dots fit chat, skeletons fit tables and lists, and step labels fit staged transactions (step 1 of 3, securing payment). Confidence: low. Sources: B16 p.254; B82 p.125
- **Show state so users need not calculate it.** Readiness colors and countdown counters remove doubt about whether an action worked. Confidence: low. Sources: B69 p.83

## Recovery, resilience and interruption

- **Design the unhappy paths as deliberately as the happy path.** Map offline, down, missing-option and wrong-input routes from the start; consider a worst-case journey map with legal, security and support to stop risky features. Confidence: high. Sources: B29 p.60; B63 p.46-47; B67 p.24-27
- **Design for interruption and resumption.** Keep goals, progress and state visible, autosave long multi-step work, and avoid auto-logouts that discard what people entered. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.104, 189, 201; B25 p.79; B46 p.633-635
- **Warn before a session times out and keep the user's input.** Offer to extend with at least 20 seconds to respond, or allow turning off or extending the limit to at least ten times the default (WCAG 2.2.1), and restore entered data after re-authentication. Confidence: high. Sources: B18 p.101, 251; B32 p.173; B79 p.201
- **Queue offline actions and retry with backoff before showing a connection error.** Keep pending work on the device, list what is waiting with options to drop an item or push it immediately, retry automatically, and keep the most common task working offline. Confidence: high. Sources: B52 p.316-317, 321-323; B61 p.288, 299-301; B14 p.107-108
- **Classify findings by effect on the user and trace each to its source.** Rate severity as blocks the task, confuses, mismatches expectation, or seen as unnecessary, and name the responsible component before proposing a fix. Confidence: high. Sources: B03 p.333-334; B28 p.298-299; B27 p.122
- **Show stale data with its age instead of an empty screen.** Say what the data is, how old it is, why a refresh failed, and offer retry. Confidence: medium. Sources: B52 p.181
- **Preserve prompt, history and partial output when a generation fails.** Generated output cannot be recreated by resubmitting, so keep context, checkpoints and recent prompts, and name the failing component with concrete options (upload manually, grant access). Confidence: medium. Sources: B82 p.130-131, 179, 194; B21 p.143, 200
- **Report capability and device failures explicitly.** Detect missing hardware or browser support, say what is missing, and fall back gracefully instead of failing silently. Confidence: low. Sources: B36 p.81; B15 p.80
- **Check every link and asset before review.** One broken image, typo or dead link makes people doubt the rest and feel less secure, especially near payment. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.84; B44 p.66, 68

## Automated decisions and AI feedback

- **Give every automated decision notice, an explanation and a working appeal.** Automated benefit, fraud and moderation systems without contestation wrongly penalized tens of thousands of people; say what action was taken, under which policy, and route appeals to a human with authority to override. Confidence: high. Sources: B43 p.51-52, 166-167, 191-192; B65 p.215-216
- **Explain limits in failure and empty states.** When the system cannot help, say why, what it does support, and invite feedback. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.276-278, 290-291; B04 p.35-36, 46; B60 p.183-184, 222
- **Prefer declining over guessing, and drop low-confidence details.** Admitting uncertainty makes later answers more trusted; express doubt in plain strong words rather than percentage scores, which users misread. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.370-374
- **Do not rely on generic disclaimers.** A blanket notice that AI can make mistakes did not change how often advice was followed; put the caution at the specific uncertain output. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.373-374
- **Separate tolerable errors from failures by stakes.** The same miss rate is an annoyance in a playlist and a failure in a vehicle; offer alternatives when stakes are low and act decisively when safety demands it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B51 p.263, 266, 272-273, 286
- **Ask for feedback after the task, with exclusive options, and confirm its effect.** Request ratings after completion with low-friction binary controls, acknowledge each submission, say when and where it takes effect, and change only what the feedback touched. Confidence: medium. Sources: B51 p.222, 231-232, 245-249

## Conversational repair and confirmation

- **Treat repair as the normal path and escalate after repeated misses.** Never loop the same not-understood message; after two to five misses change tactics by offering a menu, buttons, typing or a human. Confidence: high. Sources: B63 p.46-47, 167-168; B41 p.68-69, 139-140; B60 p.144-145; B73 p.98, 109
- **Make each reprompt more specific than the last.** Add the expected format or an example on the second try, not on the first, so help stays useful without becoming tedious. Confidence: high. Sources: B63 p.47, 120; B60 p.144-145; B41 p.68-69
- **Match the strength of confirmation to the price of a mistake, using confidence bands.** Confirm implicitly when confidence is high (around 80 percent and up), explicitly in the middle band, and reprompt below roughly 45 percent; money and settings always get explicit confirmation, tuned with real data. Confidence: high. Sources: B41 p.47-50, 233; B63 p.102-104; B33 p.41
- **Let the action itself confirm when it is visible.** If the light turns on, no speech is needed; add a short okay or done only when the result is delayed or out of sight. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.51, 53-54; B60 p.114
- **Accept that the user may not have the information.** When asking for an account number or code, say where to find it and offer another route if the person does not know it; do not guess causes such as telling people to speak louder. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.140-142, 145, 222
