# typography

- **Hold body text to a measure of about 45 to 75 characters.** Around 60 to 70 characters suits a single reading column; drop to 40 to 50 when text flows in several columns, and treat anything past roughly 80 characters (40 for CJK scripts) as too long for continuous reading. Readers scan slightly faster on long lines but prefer moderate ones, so set the measure for comfort. Confidence: high. Sources: B88 p.126-127; B46 p.607-609; B18 p.141, 254; B86 p.274; B92 p.55; B10 p.52, 83, 89; B47 p.114-116; B19 p.29; B59 p.7; B78 p.81
- **Set running text flush left with a ragged right edge.** Justified or right-aligned body copy slows reading (one study measured 8 to 10 percent slower), opens uneven word gaps and forces hyphenation; justify only where line length and hyphenation control are good enough to avoid rivers. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.607-608; B88 p.118-119; B86 p.272-274; B18 p.138; B10 p.168-169; B19 p.29; B47 p.128-131; B91 p.116; B59 p.7
- **Use mixed case for anything longer than a short label.** Capitals remove the word shapes readers rely on, so all-caps passages read slower and feel like shouting; keep capitals for brief labels and give them extra tracking. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.203-205; B26 p.440; B90 p.30; B64 p.171; B86 p.272-274; B18 p.138; B19 p.22, 29, 41; B91 p.116
- **Keep screen body text at 16px or more and never below about 12px for any reading text.** Body text of 16 to 18px (roughly 15 to 25px is the comfortable band) cuts zooming on phones and serves readers past 40 with presbyopia; captions may drop to 12 or 13px, and 9 to 10pt is only for fine print such as legal lines. Confidence: high. Sources: B86 p.290; B46 p.201-202; B26 p.440; B85 p.131, 175; B80 p.137; B19 p.29; B59 p.6-7; B18 p.161
- **Give body text a line height near 1.5 and tighten it as size grows.** Paragraph leading of about 1.4 to 1.6 (1.5 is the WCAG enhanced target) keeps line tracking easy; headings sit around 1.1 to 1.3 and large display near 1.0. Express it as a unitless or percentage value so it scales when text is resized. Confidence: high. Sources: B18 p.137-138, 141; B46 p.201-202; B47 p.122-125; B59 p.7; B88 p.46-47
- **Use one or two typefaces, two or three weights and a few sizes.** One body face plus one contrasting display face (or the body face for both) covers almost every interface; each extra family or weight adds visual noise and download cost, so give every face a stated job and choose semibold or bold, not both. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.199-203; B26 p.453; B64 p.171; B18 p.138; B47 p.40; B22 p.218; B19 p.29; B59 p.6; B05 p.64
- **Never pair small type with low contrast.** Large text can survive modest contrast and small text can survive if contrast is strong, but small low-contrast text always fails; fix illegible labels with size and contrast, not by rewording around them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B77 p.201; B20 p.683, 749, 834-835; B26 p.440; B37 p.269
- **Let users enlarge text without breaking the layout.** Default to a comfortable size, allow user scaling, and use relative units for size and leading so a resize keeps proportions; WCAG 1.4.4 requires text to scale to 200 percent. Confidence: medium. Sources: B86 p.290; B80 p.137; B18 p.137-138
- **Give light text on a dark ground more weight, size and a little tracking.** Reversed type looks thinner and can fill in, so open tracking slightly (about +0.02em), consider a heavier weight, and keep hairline high-contrast faces out of reversed settings. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.139; B18 p.135-136; B19 p.29; B31 p.92
- **Expect dark text on a light ground to read faster than the reverse.** One measured comparison found dark on light up to about a third faster, so prefer it for long reading and keep dark themes for contexts that justify them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.206
- **Prefer faces with open apertures and a generous x-height for small and screen text.** Large counters and open shapes survive coarse rendering, distance and small sizes; a big x-height does make text look denser, so add leading to match. Avoid condensed, short x-height faces for UI body text. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.46-47, 140; B31 p.28, 131, 234, 304; B18 p.138; B47 p.108-112
- **Avoid thin weights and hairline strokes at small sizes.** Weights under 400 and high-contrast hairlines break apart in small UI text; de-emphasize with a lighter color or smaller size instead, and keep light weights for large headings. Confidence: medium. Sources: B47 p.40-41; B31 p.92, 180; B19 p.29
- **Never stretch, squash, slant or shrink type to fake a style.** Software condensing distorts stroke weights, slanted romans warp curves and shrunken capitals look starved; use a designed condensed width, a true italic and true small caps. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.48, 71, 72; B10 p.19; B19 p.29; B31 p.33-34, 352
- **Track large headlines tighter and capitals looser, and leave lowercase body alone.** Space grows with size, so display type gains from slight negative tracking (around -0.01 to -0.03em); capitals and small caps want about +0.05 to +0.1em; loose tracking on lowercase or italics looks broken. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.136-139; B47 p.132-134; B10 p.171
- **Build a short, documented type scale whose steps are clearly different.** Keep a handful of named styles, each with size, line height, tracking and spacing recorded; drop near-duplicates such as 19px next to 20px, and derive sizes from one ratio or a hand-tuned ladder so mixed sizes still align. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.158, 167; B22 p.174, 218-219; B46 p.199-203; B92 p.60; B47 p.102-107
- **Emphasize with one step from the base style.** Move from regular to bold, or to italic, but never stack bold, italic, caps and underline together; reserve underline for links. Confidence: medium. Sources: B78 p.82-83; B46 p.191-194; B91 p.116, 245; B18 p.138; B59 p.7
- **Center text only for short display lines.** Two or three centered lines suit headlines, posters and formal one-off messages; running copy that is centered loses its line starts. Confidence: medium. Sources: B86 p.272-274; B47 p.129; B10 p.170; B19 p.29; B59 p.7
- **Pair typefaces on one clear contrast plus one shared trait.** Contrast serif with sans or soft with hard, but match proportions or x-height so the two sit well on a shared line; adjust sizes optically, since fonts at the same nominal size can look different sizes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.45-46, 62-63; B10 p.174; B31 p.82, 121
- **Choose a text family with full typesetting features.** Look for several weights (about five or more for UI work), a true italic, small caps, figure styles and coverage of every language you ship before committing; license the full family. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.61; B31 p.137, 173; B47 p.109; B05 p.64
- **Match the typeface's character to the content.** Faces carry personality, and type that fits its message is processed faster and trusted more; choose for role and tone as well as legibility. Confidence: medium. Sources: B19 p.59-60, 89, 147; B71 p.106; B39 p.47-48
- **Do not assume serifs read faster than sans.** Research has not shown a speed or comprehension advantage for serifs; familiarity and setting quality decide perceived readability. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.151; B19 p.24, 59
- **Use the optical size that matches the rendered size.** Caption or text cuts are sturdier for small type and display cuts finer for titles; with variable fonts let the optical size axis follow the pixel size. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.82-85, 88; B31 p.42, 63, 66
- **Use humanist sans with clearly distinct letters for glanceable and distance reading.** Open, differentiated letterforms cut glance time on dashboards, in vehicles and in wayfinding. Confidence: medium. Sources: B19 p.28, 128; B31 p.189, 211, 234
- **Make easily confused letters distinct for dyslexic readers.** Faces that separate b from d, p from q and I from l, with large counters and long ascenders and descenders, help readers with dyslexia; avoid italics, underline and caps for emphasis in their content. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.100; B91 p.116, 245
- **Reduce content before reducing font size.** Cutting size to squeeze text in hurts legibility; edit the text to its essence instead. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.171; B46 p.199-203
- **Keep geometric and uniform neo-grotesque faces for display, not long text.** Strict geometric shapes separate letters instead of binding words, and uniform grotesques lack rhythm for extended reading; corrected geometrics with a two-storey a read better. Confidence: medium. Sources: B31 p.9, 31, 167, 196; B86 p.272-274
- **Verify glyph coverage and add leading for non-Latin and accented scripts.** Missing glyphs render as boxes or question marks, decorative faces swallow diacritics, and scripts with marks above and below need extra line height; in bilingual layouts give both scripts equal visual weight. Confidence: medium. Sources: B08 p.52; B49 p.271-273; B92 p.72
- **Never signal importance through font style alone.** Screen readers do not announce bold, italic, underline or color, so carry emphasis in words or structure as WCAG 1.3.1 expects. Confidence: medium. Sources: B91 p.118
- **Proof type at real size in real running text.** A face only shows its behavior when set in a passage at the intended size and measure; check phones at arm's length and print pieces at actual scale. Confidence: medium. Sources: B31 p.11; B19 p.62; B71 p.66; B35 p.237, 242
- **Mark paragraphs with one signal only.** Use a one-em indent or modest space between paragraphs, not both, and skip the indent after a heading; when using space, about 1.5 times the line spacing meets WCAG 1.4.8. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.128-129; B18 p.141
- **Kern display sizes by eye.** Metric kerning is fine for text, but headline pairs such as To, Ta, Wa and Yo open visible gaps that need optical or manual kerning. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.134-135; B19 p.29
- **Use semantic heading elements and style them for the hierarchy you want.** Pick h1 to h6 for document structure, not for size; never fake a heading with a bold or italic line. Confidence: low. Sources: B59 p.7; B47 p.54-55
- **Size type in px or rem, never compounding em.** Nested em values multiply and drift off the scale. Confidence: low. Sources: B47 p.107
- **Align mixed sizes on a shared baseline.** Centering different sizes vertically on one line looks off; baseline alignment, and a baseline grid across columns, reads as ordered. Confidence: low. Sources: B47 p.118-120; B92 p.45
- **Right-align numbers in table columns.** Aligned place values make figures comparable at a glance; use tabular figures. Confidence: low. Sources: B47 p.128-131
- **Use correct typographic marks.** Hyphens join words, en dashes mark numeric ranges, primes mark feet and inches, and quotes should be curly in production copy. Confidence: low. Sources: B88 p.78-79
- **Avoid familiar defaults chosen only out of habit.** Ubiquitous system faces signal an undesigned product; choose deliberately. Confidence: low. Sources: B31 p.9, 85
- **Control word spacing and rivers.** Spacing too tight blurs words and too loose fragments lines; fix rivers through measure, hyphenation settings, size or leading, and use one space after a period. Confidence: low. Sources: B10 p.89, 169; B95 p.100-101
- **Use script faces only with contextual alternates, and keep heavy scripts large.** Scripts need OpenType alternates and ligatures to avoid repeated identical letters, and low x-height scripts collapse at small sizes. Confidence: low. Sources: B31 p.39, 317, 323, 329
- **Set text inside images at 14pt or larger with good contrast.** Image text cannot be resized or restyled by the reader. Confidence: low. Sources: B91 p.246
- **Size headset text generously and anchor it at a comfortable depth.** In AR and VR, use short large text (about 1 cm letter height at 0.5 m), put it on a contrasting backing panel, and keep HUD planes at least 50 cm away and adjustable. Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.62, 153, 172-173; B36 p.276, 339-341
