# xr-3d

- **Lock the frame rate to the headset's native refresh.** A single missed frame in a head-mounted display shows as flicker, breaks presence and can cause nausea; 60 fps is the absolute floor, current headsets run about 72 to 120 Hz, and active stereo on a shared screen needs about 120 Hz because each eye gets half. Cut features, polygon count or texture size before letting frames drop. Confidence: high. Sources: B36 p.157-159; B48 p.39-40, 61, 151-152, 162-163; B15 p.12, 26, 79, 109; B83 p.209, 213
- **Keep motion-to-photon latency near 20 ms.** Lag makes the world trail head turns, with error growing with turn speed (about 1 degree per 20 ms at a moderate turn), and about 60 ms is the outer limit; read head pose as late in the frame as possible, predict a short way ahead and reproject. Confidence: high. Sources: B36 p.98-101; B15 p.13, 51; B83 p.625-626
- **Let only the user move the camera.** Head tracking maps one to one, and the view is never rotated, zoomed, frozen or moved without a direct user action, because motion that is seen but not felt is the main trigger of simulator sickness; even dramatic events such as a character's death use a fade, not frozen tracking. Confidence: high. Sources: B36 p.263-266; B48 p.61-62, 147; B15 p.41
- **Degrade resolution before frame rate.** If the GPU cannot keep up, reduce the size of the offscreen render target (multiply each axis by the square root of the desired pixel share) rather than dropping frames, keep UI text at full resolution while the scene blurs, and run UI on its own thread so a stalled panel never freezes the world. Confidence: medium. Sources: B36 p.165-170, 348-355
- **Move users at constant, real-world speeds.** Avoid acceleration, head bob and field-of-view kicks; the most comfortable speeds are about 1.4 m/s for walking and about 3 m/s for jogging; limit strafing, backward motion and smooth turning, and offer snap turns in steps of about 15 degrees. Confidence: medium. Sources: B36 p.185, 269-270; B48 p.62, 148, 152
- **Relocate users by teleport or a brief fast move, never slow automated travel.** Let people aim, see a destination marker and facing direction, then confirm or cancel; where spatial orientation matters a very quick transition preserves context better than an instant jump, and slow scripted motion makes people sick. Confidence: medium. Sources: B48 p.148-149; B83 p.513-514, 550; B36 p.231, 250
- **Change scenes with a short fade to black, never a hard cut or a brightness jump.** Keep head tracking live during the fade, never cut to an empty void, and avoid sudden dark-to-bright changes because people cannot shield their eyes in a headset. Confidence: medium. Sources: B36 p.231, 250, 266; B48 p.152
- **Place main content 0.75 to 10 m away.** Closer than about 0.5 m strains the eyes, stereo depth weakens past about 10 m and is gone by about 20 m; for anything far away rely on occlusion and familiar size. Confidence: medium. Sources: B48 p.145; B36 p.137; B83 p.210
- **Guide attention with cues, never by turning the view.** Use spatial sound, light, motion and color changes, dim what is not important, or show a plain prompt to turn; gate story moments on the target actually being in view. Confidence: medium. Sources: B36 p.232-233, 264-265; B48 p.143
- **Give objects depth cues.** Ground objects on known surfaces, let real objects occlude virtual ones, add lighting and shadow, and give anything that must hover a slight movement for parallax; holograms that clip through walls or drift break the illusion. Confidence: medium. Sources: B36 p.235-236; B48 p.84, 86, 164; B83 p.210
- **Constrain manipulation to surfaces and few degrees of freedom.** Selecting and dragging on planes beats free 3D manipulation, accuracy falls with depth, objects keep natural constraints (a chair stays upright), and many tasks need only three degrees of freedom; give feedback on every interaction. Confidence: medium. Sources: B36 p.236-243; B20 p.39-40
- **Plan for arm fatigue.** Mid-air drawing tires the arm within about 15 minutes and holding a phone up for AR within about 5, so keep these sessions short, use them in bursts inside a larger flow, and offer rest. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.39-40; B48 p.116, 159, 167; B15 p.109
- **Use field of view to your advantage, and label in place on wide displays.** Wider fields improve visual search, and as AR field of view nears 100 degrees labels attached to objects outperform labels fixed in the view. Confidence: medium. Sources: B83 p.705-706
- **Start immersive mode only from an explicit user action.** Offer a clear enter-VR button; browsers require a user gesture before going fullscreen or immersive. Confidence: medium. Sources: B15 p.82, 84
- **Never pin VR interface to the user's view.** Popups and HUDs locked to the head cause discomfort; anchor panels in world space instead. Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.62, 152
- **Place key content where head turns stay comfortable.** People turn comfortably about 30 degrees left or right (55 at most), 20 degrees upward (60 at most) and 12 degrees downward (40 at most); minimize vertical turning. Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.145-147
- **Never change the rendered field of view or world scale.** A mismatch with the headset makes still objects swim when the head turns; zoom through an in-world device such as binoculars instead. Confidence: low. Sources: B36 p.264-266
- **Avoid large moving surfaces near the user.** A moving surface that fills the view, including a nearby floor, creates vection; replace stairs with flat floors or ramps and use lifts that open on the side the user already faces. Confidence: low. Sources: B36 p.271-273
- **Make gaze selection forgiving and visible.** Highlight a gazed target at once, show a fill over about 2 seconds of dwell and cancel instantly when the gaze leaves; show the gaze cursor reacting, or appearing only, over interactive objects. Confidence: low. Sources: B36 p.204, 245-246; B15 p.71-74, 121-123
- **Do not rely on keyboard and mouse inside a headset.** The headset hides the hands; design for controllers, hand tracking or gaze, map headset touchpad gestures onto standard pointer events, and target the least capable device you support. Confidence: low. Sources: B15 p.13, 69-70, 105-108
- **Show the physical boundary before people reach it.** Room-scale experiences need a visible boundary or grid near real obstacles and must work in play areas from about 7 to 20 feet. Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.43-44, 100
- **Plan spatial audio from the storyboard.** Ambient sound sets the place and spatial cues locate events out of view; missing or wrong sound makes the scene feel false. Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.194-197
- **Give labels over real scenes a backing plate.** Outdoor AR text without a background vanishes against busy or bright scenes; use high contrast, a solid plate and larger sizes. Confidence: low. Sources: B83 p.582-583, 719
- **Offer a body, but let people turn it off.** A seated avatar that matches the user's pose helps presence, while mismatched hands bother some users. Confidence: low. Sources: B36 p.246-249, 276-277
- **A fixed reference in view can reduce sickness.** A constant anchor such as a virtual nose measurably lowered simulator sickness severity (about 13.5 percent). Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.61
- **Let expert users see and trade performance.** Where workloads vary, expose the frame rate and controls such as render scale. Confidence: low. Sources: B36 p.170, 353-354
- **Expect long first loads from engines compiled to the web.** URL delivery removes the install step, but cross-compiled engines can ship megabytes before content appears; show progress or choose a lighter engine. Confidence: low. Sources: B15 p.75, 94
- **Refresh haptic and force feedback at about 1000 Hz.** Skin senses changes up to that rate, so slower force displays feel rough. Confidence: low. Sources: B83 p.98, 256
