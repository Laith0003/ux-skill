# usability-testing

Input rows: 514. Merged rows: 155.

## Sample size and rounds

- **Run qualitative rounds of about five people, fix what you saw, then run another round.** Several small rounds with design changes between them find more than one large round on a frozen design, because each round tests the previous fixes and a blocking issue no longer hides the smaller ones. Confidence: high. Sources: B79 p.228-229; B23 p.172-173; B27 p.67-69; B46 p.828-830; B02 p.577, 599-600; B06 p.50, 212-213; B94 p.175-180; B11 p.66; B29 p.68-69; B57 p.97-98; B74 p.106; B52 p.76; B87 p.19
- **Treat "five users find 85 percent" as true only for problems that hit about one user in three.** Five people give roughly an 85 percent chance of seeing a problem with a per-user rate near 0.31; measured rates run from 0.03 to 0.46, websites averaged 0.04, and on one site five users saw only about 35 percent of the problems. In a bad draw five users can find as little as 55 percent. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.166-167, 179, 181-184; B30 p.103-108; B20 p.565-571; B46 p.828-829
- **Size a formative study from the discovery formula rather than a fixed number.** A problem shows up at least once with probability 1 - (1 - p)^n, which gives n = ln(1 - goal) / ln(1 - p); take p as the rarest problem you still care about. The cost-benefit optimum sits near 3 to 5 users only when problems are common. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.160-163; B20 p.565-569
- **Use these counts for a 90 percent discovery goal.** A problem rate of 0.25 needs 9 users (15 to see it twice), 0.10 needs 22, 0.05 needs 45, and 0.01 at 99 percent needs 459. Five users catch about 76, 41 and 23 percent of problems at rates 0.25, 0.10 and 0.05; nine users catch 92, 61 and 37 percent. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.162-165; B30 p.108
- **Plan 10 users to catch a 0.15 problem with 80 percent probability, and 18 users for an 85 percent chance at a one-in-ten problem.** Rare but critical problems, such as hint text mistaken for entered data (about 10 percent of users), need these larger samples, more tasks, or several independent observers. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.162; B30 p.106-108
- **Deflate problem rates from small samples before projecting them.** A raw rate from fewer than 20 users is inflated; use 0.9p minus 0.046, or average the deflation and Good-Turing estimates. The adjusted estimate settles within 0.05 of the target after 2 to 4 users, and the plain binomial model overstates discovery by up to about 10 percent near n of 5. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.167-171, 188, 193
- **Do not expect the severe problems to surface first.** In two of three studies, how early a problem appeared bore no relation to its severity, so later participants can still uncover serious issues. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.178-179
- **Stop adding participants when new sessions stop revealing new critical issues.** A fixed small count assumes every problem is equally findable; large systems, open tasks and varied evaluators break that. In practice most issues repeat after about 5 to 9 sessions. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.565-571; B03 p.294-295; B27 p.67-68; B96 p.125
- **Test 3 to 5 people per distinct user group and add more for later, critical or diverse tests.** About five per cultural group finds most of the largest problems; prototype tests need 4 to 8 sessions, more when there are several personas; ten users find about 95 percent and fifteen about 97 percent of common problems. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.828-830; B49 p.23; B85 p.91, 112, 277; B41 p.188, 205; B28 p.239; B35 p.287-288
- **Accept 2 or 3 users per round when rounds repeat on a fixed schedule.** Three users a week, three a month, or two every two weeks still beat no testing, as long as the rounds keep coming and the cumulative total grows. Confidence: high. Sources: B34 p.123-124; B77 p.125-130; B02 p.577; B57 p.98; B11 p.157
- **Use at least 15 to 20 participants per group for statistics, comparisons and benchmarks.** Experiments need at least 15 per condition; A/B and cross-group comparisons need about 20 per group; surveys and summative studies need calculated sample sizes with stated margins of error and confidence levels. Confidence: high. Sources: B02 p.599-600; B49 p.23; B06 p.211-212, 218; B87 p.19; B11 p.61, 66; B94 p.27, 122-123
- **For a design comparison, a 90 percent confidence level usually suffices to declare a difference.** State the level used alongside the result. Confidence: low. Sources: B06 p.211-212
- **Use small samples to show a design is bad, never to prove it good.** A few users can show a completion rate or time is clearly unacceptable; showing it is acceptable needs a large sample. Report a test as defects found, not as a pass certificate. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.73; B28 p.44, 64
- **Do not judge subtle tweaks with a five-person test.** Small microinteraction or conversion differences need instrumented analytics and hundreds to thousands of users; a 5 versus 4.75 percent gap is hard to detect even with thousands. Confidence: medium. Sources: B14 p.161, 163; B74 p.33-34
- **Prefer within-subjects comparisons with counterbalanced order.** Having the same people try both designs removes between-person variance; one comparison needed 15 users within-subjects against 52 between-subjects. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.80, 133-134; B85 p.181-182
- **Compare task time with the old design using one group of participants who try the versions in alternating order.** Because task time depends heavily on the details of the task, having the same people use both versions gives the fairest baseline. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.72
- **Expect accessibility samples to multiply by capability and severity.** Three capabilities at ten severity levels with five users each is already 150 people, so sample the borderline levels deliberately; 7 or 8 per level is a typical minimum. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.136-137

## Cadence and planning

- **Test early, at the sketch, paper or wireframe stage, while changes are cheap.** If you wait for polished clickable builds or for code, little room remains to alter core structure; paper already reveals whether people understand the information architecture. Confidence: high. Sources: B35 p.278-281; B38 p.30; B72 p.385; B87 p.19, 25; B06 p.212-213; B66 p.118
- **Put users in front of the team on a fixed rhythm: at least monthly, ideally weekly or every sprint.** A standing slot (for instance three users each week, done by midday on a set day) ends scheduling debates and means the team never waits more than five business days for feedback. Confidence: high. Sources: B34 p.123-124, 152; B74 p.59, 203; B77 p.125-130; B03 p.290; B11 p.157
- **Run a test round at every fidelity step.** Testing paper, wireframe, beta and release versions keeps fidelity and function moving in a direction users confirm instead of polishing a wrong idea. Confidence: medium. Sources: B87 p.19; B06 p.212-213; B72 p.385
- **Choose the test type to suit the stage of the work.** Exploratory or formative tests probe the concept with lots of talk and small samples; assessment tests, run mid-cycle, measure how people actually perform tasks; validation or summative tests compare against preset benchmarks or competitors, with minimal moderator contact and planned samples. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.65-75; B94 p.27, 122-123; B26 p.87-88
- **Base conclusions on what people do, not on what they say.** Ratings, praise and stated intent diverge from behavior: one site was rated well organized while task success stayed under 50 percent, and people used to difficulty call awkward products easy. Confidence: high. Sources: B58 p.99, 113-114; B23 p.150, 236; B69 p.135, 179; B74 p.22, 40; B85 p.269; B41 p.206-207
- **Use testing to evaluate designs, not to invent them.** Testing does well on labels, grouping, discoverability and first use but poorly on intermediate use; when the budget forces a choice, pay first for research that informs the design. Confidence: low. Sources: B26 p.87-88, 169-172
- **Fit the method to the question.** For categories and labels, run card sorts and tree tests; for appeal, concept tests; to choose between options, A/B tests; for visibility, heat maps; and for ease of use, task-based usability tests. Confidence: medium. Sources: B35 p.285; B30 p.119-124; B27 p.70-72
- **Set measurable task targets before the design is built.** State a share of users, a time and an error limit (for example 95 percent through registration in 2 minutes without error, or at least 85 percent success on top tasks) and move to build only when tests meet it; a dozen targets is common, fifty is too many. Confidence: high. Sources: B28 p.42, 74, 80; B66 p.134; B58 p.129, 132; B20 p.410; B51 p.162
- **Begin preparing a round three weeks or more before results are due, and test at most about five features per round.** Circulate the script to the team a week or more in advance. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.290-291, 312
- **Pilot the full setup with a colleague before the first real participant.** A dry run catches unclear tasks, broken questions and recording faults; allow about an hour for it, a week ahead when possible. Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.126-127; B06 p.227-228; B17 p.80; B23 p.166
- **Freeze the build before test day.** Allowing code changes the day before testing produced a build that crashed every few minutes and wasted sessions. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.253
- **Have the whole team, including the decision maker, watch sessions and synthesize the same day.** Observers who never saw users struggle dismiss findings; read notes aloud, cluster them and agree next steps within a day, or record video when people cannot attend. Confidence: high. Sources: B34 p.124, 127; B77 p.125-130; B81 p.222-224; B58 p.114, 119-120; B85 p.278; B69 p.134-135
- **Have someone not attached to the design run the test.** Designers defend their own work; a moderator outside the project, or an outside reviewer, reads results more honestly. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.87-88; B66 p.118; B35 p.124-125
- **Budget a basic test round at roughly 100 staff hours.** A typical eight-user round splits into materials, running sessions, analysis and reporting, plus travel; testing abroad adds about half again. Confidence: low. Sources: B08 p.259-260

## Recruiting

- **Recruit people who match the real users, ideally ones about to do the real job.** Select on the attributes and behaviors that define the target group, verify screener answers, and avoid random cafe recruits or low-quality panels when the product is specialized. Confidence: high. Sources: B96 p.114-116; B40 p.46-47; B87 p.19-20; B71 p.52; B81 p.126-128
- **Write screeners that hide the right answer and exclude industry insiders.** Ask neutral behavior questions under a generic posting, and screen out people who work in UX, design, marketing, advertising or market research unless they are the users. Confidence: high. Sources: B81 p.126-128; B03 p.31, 126; B96 p.115
- **Prefer screened strangers over friends and family.** Acquaintances soften feedback; a loose friends-and-family round is acceptable only as a quick check for problems that would hit almost anyone, judged on whether real users would hit them too. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.255-256; B96 p.114-116; B77 p.131-132; B03 p.31
- **Within the target group, skew small samples toward less skilled and borderline users.** Five tech-savvy people solve almost anything, and extreme users fail early in ways that mask everything else; borderline users who should cope reveal the most. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.103-104, 108; B32 p.133, 137
- **Over-recruit to cover no-shows.** No-shows average about 11 percent; recruit 6 to 10 to be sure of 5, schedule 7 or 8 to end with 5 or 6, and where no-shows are frequent book 2 to 3 people per slot. Keep a spare participant close by each day. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.828-829; B91 p.185; B03 p.294-295; B27 p.67-68; B96 p.127, 130; B08 p.228, 244; B23 p.175
- **Budget recruiting time generously.** It takes far more effort than expected, often an hour or two a day through the week before testing; cap each round's recruiting window at a couple of days to keep rounds fast. Confidence: medium. Sources: B23 p.162; B81 p.245; B27 p.69
- **Pay incentives after the session, scaled to time and audience.** Paying at the end deters people who are only after easy money; busy professionals need several times the consumer rate, and early access can be a strong draw. Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.116; B77 p.132; B81 p.126; B20 p.549
- **Use mostly fresh participants each round, reusing about one in three to five.** Repeat participants confirm whether fixes worked; fresh ones avoid learned behavior. Developers and frequent testers adapt and stop noticing problems. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.551; B94 p.175-180; B36 p.260
- **Test first-time and returning users, and novices and experts, as separate profiles.** First-time users need onboarding returning users skip, so map and test both flows. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.56, 144; B93 p.370-371
- **Pre-screen with a short mini-task.** Pick people who match the profile and also follow instructions and voice opinions clearly. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.139
- **Keep signed consent forms for at least three years and state how long recordings are kept.** Get written consent for recordings and photos before the session. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.557, 574; B69 p.182; B85 p.274-275

## Tasks and scenarios

- **Phrase every task as a goal, never as steps, and never use the words on the interface.** Participants mirror the wording you give them, so a task that names the button or menu label tests reading, not finding; tasks must be unambiguous and end in an observable action. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.405-408; B28 p.121-123, 222; B85 p.270; B63 p.255; B81 p.213-214; B17 p.65-66
- **Provide no more context than an actual user would have.** Skip any guided tour before the task; offer a realistic scenario and then let people explore. With voice products, do not mention that speaking is possible, since whether they notice it is itself a finding. Confidence: high. Sources: B27 p.46, 64; B41 p.183, 186-187; B48 p.174; B03 p.32-33
- **Write explicit success criteria for every task before the study.** Define the end point, which wrong turns are acceptable, and whether a task with errors still counts as success; when the team disagrees on criteria, that reveals confusion about the design itself. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.118, 123; B30 p.168-170; B20 p.405-408
- **Choose tasks by frequency first, then criticality, then suspected weakness.** Cover the tasks that make up 75 to 80 percent of daily use, then those whose failure loses data or drives support calls; skip features fewer than 5 percent of users touch. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.123-125
- **Open with an easy task, then run the most important task early.** An easy start settles nerves; the main task comes right after setup, before participants learn what you want and stop behaving naturally. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.31; B17 p.67-68
- **Rotate the order of tasks and designs across participants.** Performance improves on later items as people learn along the way; a Latin square covers five tasks in five orders rather than 120. Confidence: high. Sources: B27 p.61, 77, 157; B17 p.69; B12 p.396; B41 p.187; B85 p.181-182; B94 p.72, 80
- **Build scenarios from the participant's own life and stakes.** Reuse context from the warm-up, let people use their own data, and where possible attach real consequences or real money so mild concerns become real ones. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.48, 145; B17 p.65-66, 73-74; B74 p.27-28
- **Pick the task type for what you know.** Scavenger hunts when one right answer exists, reverse hunts when wording would leak clues, self-generated tasks when goals are unknown, recreated faults for troubleshooting; mix easy and impossible, known-item and exhaustive tasks. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.143-146; B93 p.370-371
- **Plan 5 to 7 tasks and no more than about 45 minutes of task time per session.** Allow about 3 minutes per simple task, up to 10 to 15 for complex ones; a 60-minute session holds about 40 minutes of tasks, a 90-minute session 50 to 70. Confidence: medium. Sources: B06 p.214; B77 p.146; B03 p.300-301; B93 p.370; B20 p.410
- **Put novice task time at 3 to 10 times the expert time.** As a last resort, set 1.5 times expert time as the unacceptable limit. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.301; B94 p.72
- **Prepare more tasks than the slot holds.** Keep 20 to 30 minutes of detail tasks ready for a 10 to 20 minute slot so the session can adapt. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.70
- **Show a few second-level items under each label when testing categories.** Sample subcategories raise information scent and change which label people pick. Confidence: low. Sources: B93 p.387

## Moderation

- **Stay neutral and never help, explain or defend the design.** Answer requests for help with a question back ("what would you do if I were not here?"), keep a neutral face, avoid confirming words, and treat the same failure across users as a design fault. Confidence: high. Sources: B96 p.127-128; B03 p.32-33, 307; B77 p.133, 137-139; B30 p.147-151; B57 p.118-119; B85 p.111, 276-277; B48 p.174
- **Read a short orientation script word for word to everyone.** Say the product is being tested, not the person, keep it to a few paragraphs (never past two pages, about 5 minutes), and do not repeat the reassurance so often it sounds nervous. Confidence: high. Sources: B28 p.194-196, 265; B81 p.206-214; B57 p.114-118; B06 p.227-228; B30 p.147
- **Let participants struggle before stepping in, and log every hint.** When someone stalls, wait silently for 20 to 30 seconds, then escalate in steps: first invite another attempt, next offer a broad pointer, and only then give specific instructions, noting the level needed; let people give up on a task after a couple of minutes instead of showing them where to click. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.93-94, 244, 247-251, 342-344; B27 p.65; B03 p.33
- **Ask what will happen before an action and whether it matched after.** Once performed, an action overwrites the original expectation, so ask first. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.319; B28 p.244-246; B81 p.213-214; B85 p.268-269
- **Ask open questions and probe vague value words immediately.** Avoid yes-or-no and multiple-choice questions; when someone calls something cool, confusing or cluttered, echo the word back and ask what made it feel that way; use trailing questions and silence rather than leading prompts. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.217-219; B27 p.64-65; B85 p.268-269; B17 p.115-118
- **Never ask participants to design, predict their own use, or price the product.** Questions like "would you use this" or "how much would you pay" produce noise; watch what they do. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.269; B27 p.64-65
- **Establish relevance with a closed question before an open one, and avoid "why".** "Why" invites rationalized answers; short questions lead less. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.115-117
- **Let the participant talk about 85 percent of the time.** During in-depth interviews the moderator should speak only around 15 percent of the time. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.113, 119-120
- **Save the question of what they would change for the end.** Asked about a minute in, it draws cosmetic remarks; asked at the close, it brings out failed tasks and missing features. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.120
- **Separate the moderator from the note-taker.** Staff one facilitator and one note-taker; in paper tests add an operator who plays the computer. A moderator working alone records the session and reviews it later. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.83; B85 p.274-275; B74 p.107-108; B06 p.220; B91 p.188
- **Keep at most two or three observers in the room and the rest remote.** In a shared room, observers sit 10 to 15 feet from the station and the moderator stays in peripheral vision at about 45 degrees. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.832; B85 p.274; B32 p.155; B06 p.220; B28 p.139, 143, 260
- **Gather observer questions and pass them to the moderator in a brief slot at the close.** Five to ten minutes per session keeps the moderator free of interruptions; take stock after 3 or 4 sessions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.96-98; B77 p.137-139
- **Do not analyze or fix during the testing run.** Mid-test conclusions bias later sessions; note where people get stuck and save fixes for after. Confidence: low. Sources: B06 p.226; B74 p.204
- **Record critical incidents as they happen, with full context.** Note the task, the intent, the expectation, what happened, the emotional state and the recovery; details fade within minutes, and retrospective comments stay valid only up to about a day. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.471-475, 580-582; B46 p.811
- **Watch nonverbal cues and workarounds.** Frowning and fidgeting signal that people are blaming themselves; tape over switches, passwords shared among staff and objects put to new uses point to problems users already solved and then forgot. Ask how they came about without judgment. Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.127; B03 p.251-252; B32 p.77, 156; B69 p.179
- **Capture first impressions on a fresh load and use memory drawings.** Start from a blank page, ask what draws the eye and what the site is about after a minute or two; asking people to draw the screen with the monitor off shows what they actually noticed. Confidence: low. Sources: B03 p.303, 308
- **If every result validates the design, suspect your assumptions.** Read results honestly and avoid picking only the confirming moments. Confidence: low. Sources: B96 p.129

## Think-aloud

- **Choose the think-aloud protocol deliberately.** Concurrent think-aloud fits formative tests but adds cognitive load and slows people down; silent completion is realistic but reveals little of the reasoning; retrospective review falls in between; critique mode suits experts. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.75-76; B28 p.92, 118, 243; B26 p.87-88
- **Do not combine concurrent think-aloud with timed tasks or eye tracking.** Narrating changes attention and task time; drop it when timing matters or tasks are very short. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.92, 118, 243; B06 p.224
- **Warm up think-aloud and prompt quickly when it stops.** Practice narration before the first task and nudge a silent participant within a few seconds during think-aloud tasks; a stalled participant who has stopped trying is a different case (see the wait rule above). Confidence: medium. Sources: B06 p.222, 225; B85 p.274-275; B77 p.137-139; B57 p.118-119; B03 p.32-33
- **Swap think-aloud for post-task interviews or paired co-discovery when narration is unreliable.** Use them when speaking aloud is too taxing, or where participants avoid criticizing the facilitator; pairs of equal standing talking to each other surface reasoning naturally. Confidence: medium. Sources: B32 p.155-156; B08 p.141, 230-231; B49 p.281-282; B79 p.228-229
- **Run sessions one participant at a time, never as a focus group.** Groups amplify the loudest voice; a deliberate pair for co-discovery is the only exception. Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.126; B81 p.255-256; B77 p.124

## Fidelity of the test artifact

- **Expect low-fidelity prototypes to find about as many problems as high-fidelity ones.** In a measured comparison fidelity did not change the problems found, though paper sessions ran about 30 percent longer and 92 percent of participants preferred the computer version; paper still finds many severe problems. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.806; B20 p.431, 441-442
- **Know what each artifact is able to test.** Sketches check concept value; hierarchy, navigation and copy are checked with wireframes; brand, visual hierarchy, grouping and call-to-action clarity with static mockups; and workflow with clickable prototypes. Confidence: low. Sources: B34 p.129-132
- **Prefer a clickable prototype to static sketches when you want to watch behavior.** Static screens invite opinions such as "I would put a button there" and polite agreement; a clickable flow lets you observe the task. Confidence: low. Sources: B74 p.22, 40
- **Run a quick hallway test with 3 to 5 people on paper sketches and wireframes before moving to digital.** Confidence: low. Sources: B87 p.25; B78 p.105

## Session structure and timing

- **Keep a session to about 60 minutes, 90 at most for standard participants.** Value drops quickly after an hour; participants stay effective about 90 minutes, and some degrade after 30. Plan for 2 hours or less. Confidence: high. Sources: B23 p.171; B85 p.275; B81 p.165, 203; B34 p.123-124; B58 p.285; B20 p.554; B03 p.296; B06 p.214
- **Follow a fixed session shape with timed blocks.** Welcome and orientation about 5 minutes, background questions 2 to 15, first impressions or tour 3 to 10, tasks 30 to 40, probing 5, debrief and wrap-up 5 to 10. Confidence: high. Sources: B77 p.136-137; B03 p.304-311; B28 p.116; B17 p.64; B81 p.206-214; B96 p.122
- **Use the discussion guide as a set of questions to answer rather than a fixed script.** Keep a condensed guide of about two pages and improvise inside its structure; facilitator lines that must stay identical across sessions (orientation, task wording) are read verbatim. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.64, 85
- **Leave 15 to 30 minutes between sessions and cap a day at 4 to 6 one-hour sessions.** Use 3 to 4 sessions for 90-minute formats; a longer gap after the first session absorbs setup problems. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.295; B28 p.242; B17 p.58-59; B30 p.123; B96 p.125; B81 p.165, 203
- **Debrief observers for 10 to 20 minutes after each session and end each session with issues and actions.** Turn observations into concrete next steps while they are fresh. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.323; B74 p.108-109; B81 p.206-214
- **Probe mismatches between ratings and behavior in the debrief.** If a participant struggled yet rates the product highly, ask why; a gentle devil's advocate challenge shows whether the praise holds up under pushback. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.270, 276-278; B58 p.113-114
- **Run a 10-minute technical check call before each remote session.** Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.92

## Metrics, questionnaires and benchmarks

- **Measure objective performance and subjective ratings together.** Completion, errors, time and problems sit beside task and test satisfaction; a user may fail a task yet not mind if recovery is painless, so tally behaviors rather than vivid impressions. Confidence: high. Sources: B41 p.206-207; B94 p.27; B58 p.99, 113-114; B28 p.270
- **Break usability into measurable parts.** Effectiveness, efficiency, learnability, memorability, errors, satisfaction and accessibility can each get a target; preference alone is not usability. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.42-43; B06 p.210-211; B40 p.29
- **Give SUS once the study ends, and SEQ or SMEQ once each task ends.** SUS proved the most sensitive post-study questionnaire and accounts for about 43 percent of post-test use; SEQ and SMEQ outperformed other single-item post-task measures. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.203-204, 215, 228-229, 237; B35 p.325
- **Score SUS exactly.** Subtract 1 from each odd item, subtract each even item from 5, sum and multiply by 2.5; the Usable subscale multiplies by 3.125 and Learnable by 12.5. Reliability is about 0.91 to 0.92 overall, 0.70 for Learnable. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.215-217
- **Benchmark SUS against a mean of 68 (SD 12.5), not against 50.** A 68 is the 50th percentile; 50 is the 13th, 60 the 29th, 74 the 70th, 80 the 88th, 85 the 97th and 90 the 99.8th. Only 2 of 241 studies averaged above 90. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.219-220
- **Grade SUS on the curved scale.** A+ at 84.1 and above; 80.8 to 84 earns A; 74.1 to 77.1 earns B; 65 to 71 earns C; 51.7 to 62.6 earns D; under 51.7 earns F. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.221
- **Compare SUS to the norm for the product type.** Average score by type: 67.6 for B2B, 74 for B2C, 67 for web, 64.7 for mobile phones, 71.3 for hardware, 76.7 for internal software, 79.9 for IVR. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.222
- **Expect SUS to give stable comparisons from about 12 users.** It reached 75 percent correct conclusions at 8 users and 100 percent at 12, while other questionnaires stayed in the low to mid 70s at 14. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.228-229
- **Know the shorter alternatives and their norms.** UMUX has 4 items and SUPR-Q 13, both correlating about 0.96 with SUS; PSSUQ has 16 items with an overall norm of 2.82 where lower is better; SUMI and WAMMI scores have mean 50 and SD 10. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.206-208, 214, 239-244
- **Read post-task ratings as a proxy for time and errors.** SEQ correlates about minus 0.9 with time and 0.84 with errors; perceived difficulty grows with the square root of time, so 100 times the time feels about 10 times harder. Post-task and post-study ratings correlate about 0.64. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.230-231, 236
- **For single-item ratings, prefer 7-point scales.** Reliability climbs steeply up to about 7 points and levels off past 11; reverse-score negatively worded items, and keep every statement below about 20 words. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.204-205, 231; B41 p.190-193
- **Gather expected and experienced difficulty for each task to spot the fix-it-fast tasks.** Tasks people expect to be easy but then rate as hard come first; user predictions accounted for only about half of actual ease and overstated difficulty on 7 of 8 tasks. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.232-234
- **Expect post-task scores to be noisy with very small samples.** With 23 or more users, post-task methods correlate above 0.95 with the full data, but at 3 users only 0.72 to 0.83. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.237
- **Report task time with a geometric mean and split errors into slips and mistakes.** Confidence: low. Sources: B14 p.161, 163
- **Do not infer funnel drop-off from moderated sessions.** Being watched makes participants keep going; rely on analytics for abandonment. Confidence: low. Sources: B74 p.33-34; B14 p.161
- **Test comprehension, not only behavior.** Use cloze tests, readability checks and quiz-style questions about charts; wrong answers point to the chart type, complexity or labels. Confidence: medium. Sources: B55 p.103-104; B24 p.213
- **Give novel designs repeated exposure before rejecting them.** Unfamiliar forms test badly at first sight and gain liking with exposure. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.214, 327-328
- **Require a large proven gain before replacing a convention.** A radical departure should beat the convention by about 30 percent or 2 times; benchmark before and after. Confidence: low. Sources: B07 p.266-267
- **Keep in-app surveys to five questions plus an open "anything else".** Confidence: low. Sources: B41 p.230

## Analysis, severity and reporting

- **Prioritize each issue by severity times frequency.** Score impact on a defined scale (blocks the task, major delay, minor, suggestion; or 1 to 5) and multiply or combine with the share of users affected; persistence across screens and effort to fix refine the order. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.30; B17 p.159; B30 p.210-212; B58 p.109, 302; B06 p.226
- **Use concrete frequency bands when scoring.** For example: 90 percent of users or more scores level 4, 51 to 89 scores 3, 11 to 50 scores 2, and 10 or less scores 1; impact weights of 10, 5, 3 and 1 also work. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.30
- **Act on a pattern once three or more of five participants show it.** Even two or three of five sharing a strong reaction deserves attention; three affected out of five implies roughly 23 to 88 percent of all users. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.203, 226-227; B94 p.43
- **Fix the worst problems first, frequently by taking things away.** Each observer lists the top three problems from each session, the team ranks a top ten, and resists adding explanations; problems users recover from on their own and raw feature requests rank low. Confidence: medium. Sources: B77 p.134, 149-150; B30 p.215-216
- **Maintain a low-hanging-fruit list.** Any fix that takes one person less than an hour and needs no sign-off ships immediately. Confidence: low. Sources: B77 p.150
- **Cluster observations, write insights, then the smallest testable fix.** Group about 100 observations into 10 to 15 clusters, state an insight headline per cluster, list competing explanations, and give each the simplest change that tests it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.214-218; B34 p.127
- **Log issues per screen in a shared grid.** Use one column per participant and one row per section, or a spreadsheet holding screenshot, URL, issue, count of users affected and suggested fix; either feeds prioritization with no transcription step. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.101-102; B81 p.222-224
- **Control researcher bias with two independent coders.** Fix pass and fail criteria before the study, record verbatim, put tasks on cards, and have at least two people code errors and compare. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.168-170; B06 p.231
- **Throw out bad data before analysis.** Remove participants recruited in error, answers given to leading questions and observations from prototypes that broke; spend replay time on the confusing sessions, watch at least four session videos, and discard clusters holding only one or two items. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.134-135; B03 p.330, 333
- **Report in a page or two, within days.** A short summary emailed within 2 to 4 business days gets acted on; a long formal report does not. Confidence: medium. Sources: B77 p.128-130; B03 p.84-85
- **Expect most observed problems to be about knowing how to act.** Knowing which action to take on which object accounts for three quarters or more of observed UX problems. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.713

## Expert inspection and heuristic evaluation

- **Use three to five independent evaluators, never just one.** One evaluator finds about 29 to 42 percent of problems (35 percent on average; for major problems 42 percent, for minor ones 32 percent); a lone reviewer finds about 60 percent of what three find, and four or five reach about 75 to 80 percent of what inspection can uncover. Returns flatten near ten. Confidence: high. Sources: B89 p.40, 51, 57-58, 69, 81; B46 p.809-813; B20 p.499-500, 506-509; B02 p.612-613; B30 p.153-156; B58 p.295; B94 p.178; B11 p.67
- **Have evaluators inspect alone, then merge and rate severity.** No conferring until every individual pass is done; merge the lists, average severities, sort, then debrief. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.51, 65; B11 p.67; B58 p.295-297, 302-310
- **Make two passes: flow first, then each element.** The first pass learns the task flow and scope, the second judges each element in context; budget 1 to 2 hours per evaluator and split larger interfaces. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.53-54, 86; B02 p.612-616; B58 p.295-297
- **Expect inspection to produce both false alarms and misses.** About one third of heuristic findings turn out real, about 43 percent are false alarms, and roughly 21 percent of real problems go unnoticed; findings from non-experts were impossible more than half the time. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.814; B02 p.617; B89 p.211, 353
- **Never let inspection replace user testing; inspect first, then test.** Even the best expert evaluations caught fewer than half of the problems seen in the lab and fewer than a third of the most serious ones; inspection clears obvious issues cheaply, while testing uncovers mental-model, workflow and domain problems. Confidence: high. Sources: B89 p.44, 57, 82, 199, 210-211; B46 p.812-814, 821-822; B20 p.506-514; B02 p.612-613; B40 p.45; B58 p.309-310
- **Expect every method and every evaluator to find different problems.** Any two methods overlapped by just 10 to 15 percent and any two evaluators by about 9 percent; in one comparison, 90 percent of problems appeared in the report of a single lab out of four; user testing found several times as many problem types as walkthroughs did. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.499-500, 532; B89 p.232-235
- **Favor evaluators who combine usability expertise with domain knowledge.** Novices found about 22 percent of problems, usability specialists 41, and specialists who also knew the domain 60. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.84; B02 p.617
- **Stress the heuristics inspectors under-report, especially consistency and feedback.** Consistency caused about a quarter of lab problems but appeared in about 2 percent of inspectors' findings; feedback caused 27 percent. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.206, 213-214
- **Give evaluators realistic scenarios, realistic data volumes and time to explore.** Scenarios from real task analysis let non-specialists judge specialist software; sparse test data hides real problems; free exploration covers areas scenarios skip. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.33-34, 56, 135, 250-251, 356; B30 p.153-156
- **Turn generic heuristics into concrete platform checks, and score each with a rationale.** Rate every feature on a defined scale (pass or fail, 1 to 6, or 0 to 100) and record the reason. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.153-156; B35 p.124-125; B44 p.330-331
- **Search harder for absent elements when reviewing paper or static mockups.** On paper, an evaluator who hits a gap just flips to the next page instead of getting stuck, so problems of something being absent stay hidden. Confidence: low. Sources: B89 p.82
- **Use cognitive walkthroughs for learnability, knowing their hit rate.** About a quarter of predicted problems proved real in one study, while a streamlined walkthrough matched about 80 percent of user-study findings and cut time by 2 to 4 times; groups of 4 to 5 work well. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.816-817; B02 p.626; B89 p.154, 160
- **Walk through the product as a persona doing a real task.** Note what the persona notices, whether the target can be seen, and how long it takes to get from entry to the next action. Confidence: low. Sources: B35 p.130-131
- **Hunt for dead ends and overload.** Look for broken links, controls with no effect, paths along which a task can never be completed, and screens demanding too much at one time. Confidence: low. Sources: B35 p.27-28
- **Apply the trunk test to deep pages chosen at random.** With a blurry glance, a user should be able to tell the site, the page, the major sections, the local navigation, their current location and how to search. Confidence: low. Sources: B77 p.93-94

## Remote, unmoderated and guerrilla testing

- **Use unmoderated tests to answer whether newcomers can complete a task, not why.** Recorded remote sessions return within an hour or a day and suit large-sample benchmarks, but say nothing about liking, real use or how to fix; rough prototypes need a moderator, near-final ones can go unmoderated. Confidence: medium. Sources: B27 p.70-72; B77 p.151; B30 p.119-124; B87 p.19
- **Choose the location to fit the goal.** Remote moderated tests find issues cheaply, a lab is best for exposing the team to users, and remote sessions in people's homes reveal real hardware, clutter and interruptions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.119-124; B44 p.337-338
- **Run guerrilla tests of 5 to 20 minutes for a small reward.** Short intercept sessions with 4 to 6 people per task validate a flow quickly. Confidence: medium. Sources: B23 p.74; B27 p.61-62; B41 p.206
- **Run five-second landing page tests with 10 to 15 people.** One exposure is too thin to show a pattern with fewer. Confidence: low. Sources: B27 p.56, 68
- **Test the whole journey in the real context, and new features inside the full product.** A feature that passed in isolation became hard to find among dozens of others once shipped; bots trained on terse requests met friendly openers in real use. Confidence: medium. Sources: B29 p.66-67; B58 p.124-125; B66 p.131
- **Before launch, have two people walk a checklist across browsers and devices, then two more click through the live site.** Check every link and load speed on phones, tablets and desktops. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.130; B05 p.97

## Inclusive and accessibility testing

- **Test with people who use assistive technology, not only with automated checkers.** Tools confirm that alt text exists, not that it is useful; combine automated checks with expert review and sessions with disabled participants, and confirm beforehand that their own technology works with the product. Confidence: medium. Sources: B18 p.73, 205-206, 222-223; B32 p.148-151
- **Expect older and politeness-bound participants to praise the product and blame themselves.** Watch behavior rather than trusting what they say about satisfaction, and take care of their confidence. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.135, 178-179; B08 p.141
- **Use impairment simulators to supplement users, never to replace them.** Graded blur glasses were the most effective and cheapest part of one toolkit, and gloves or weights add repeatable insight, but no simulator conveys cognition, coping strategies or wants. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.211, 221, 224-228; B32 p.116-118
- **Place participant wellbeing above the protocol.** Offer breaks, other input methods and the option to skip; never ask for a task the person has doubts about; check prototypes for hidden hazards; keep sessions below 2 hours, with less than an hour of equipment use. Confidence: medium. Sources: B32 p.155-156, 165, 168-169; B69 p.182
- **Send accessible pre-session materials and plan venue access.** Consent forms, directions and protocol go out in each person's format; escort blind participants. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.148-151
- **Use coping strategies as design input.** Workarounds disabled users invent reveal unmet needs and new product uses. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.77, 156

## Cross-cultural testing

- **Hold sessions in the participant's own language, led by a trained local facilitator.** Working through an interpreter kills spontaneity; if an interpreter is present, brief them to render tone and passing remarks too. Confidence: low. Sources: B08 p.231-236
- **Adjust questionnaires, card sorts and think-aloud for each culture.** Each of these methods carries answer biases specific to a culture, so comparing raw results across countries can mislead; rely on objective performance measures. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.281-282; B08 p.141, 230-231
- **Control the setting where interruptions are common.** Colleagues helping and personal calls distort task times; book a closed room or use indirect methods. Confidence: low. Sources: B49 p.282, 287
- **Keep sessions short where operators are busy.** In some settings 30 minutes with at most four short tests is the practical maximum. Confidence: low. Sources: B08 p.316

## Specialized media

- **Test voice designs with a hidden human before the system exists.** A Wizard of Oz operator plays the prompts so you see what people actually say; in one study it raised command recognition from about 7 to about 76 percent. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.198-200, 208; B20 p.434
- **Test AI features against real model output, in fast iterative rounds.** Combine a rough prototype with a spike running a working model so people react to genuine responses, and run RITE-style rounds (1 to 4 participants each, about 9 to 12 overall, fixing between rounds) rather than a single late test. Confidence: medium. Sources: B12 p.360-361, 388-398; B20 p.520
- **Measure comfort and sickness alongside speed in immersive tests.** A much faster technique that makes a share of users sick is not better; use a standard sickness questionnaire, rest breaks, and sessions of about 30 minutes, since many users feel symptoms after 30 to 60 minutes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B83 p.658, 682, 714, 721; B36 p.285
- **Stage comfort testing for VR.** Yourself first, then 5 to 10 fresh people of varied height, age and susceptibility, then 50 to 100 before beta. Confidence: low. Sources: B36 p.260, 286
- **Use NASA TLX for workload and consider dropping its pairwise weighting.** Six subscales on a 100-point range; the weighting step may reduce validity. Confidence: low. Sources: B83 p.117-118
- **Role-play forms to find bad microcopy.** One person speaks only the labels while another tries to complete the form; the dialogue exposes vague labels and needless fields. Confidence: low. Sources: B54 p.154-156
- **Expect misuse and test for it.** If a part can go in upside down, someone will do it; decide which misuse the design must absorb. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.151
