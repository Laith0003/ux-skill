# critique-collaboration

Input rows: 254. Merged rows: 80.

## Idea generation and convergence in groups

- **Generate ideas alone before the group discusses them.** Parallel individual sketching or note writing yields more and better ideas than open shout-out brainstorming, and sticky notes let quieter people contribute on equal terms. Confidence: high. Sources: B81 p.113, 129; B34 p.76-81; B71 p.70-72; B74 p.96-97
- **Vote silently before anyone argues.** Post the options, let each person place dots without talking to build a heat map, run a short speed critique with the author silent until the end, then take a straw poll. Typical numbers: 20 to 30 small dots each for the heat map, about 3 minutes of critique per sketch, 2 votes per person for How Might We notes, 5 stickers per person in content workshops. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.84-87, 136-148; B96 p.89-93; B64 p.198-200; B23 p.293-294
- **Strip authorship from ideas before judging them.** Use the same paper and pens for everyone, give each sketch a title instead of a name, and use thick markers so drawing skill does not decide the winner; a sketch that needs its author to pitch it will also confuse users. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.121-122, 138; B96 p.89-90; B74 p.96-97
- **Give one named decider the final call, openly.** Get whoever holds the veto into the room, give deciders extra votes that everyone can see, and refuse requests to weigh in afterwards; replacing the decider with a team vote invites the decision to be reversed. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.138-148; B96 p.43-45, 92-93, 99; B78 p.95-96; B39 p.39-40
- **Converge by choosing, not by blurring ideas together.** Do not reach agreement by making concepts vaguer or splitting the difference; pick one bet, hold the runner-up back, or test both when there is room. Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.102-103; B34 p.80-81
- **Turn every problem raised into a How Might We question.** Write one per sticky note at a moderate scope, group the notes by theme, then dot vote; expect 30 to 100 notes in a sprint and spend about 10 minutes organizing them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.79-87; B30 p.25
- **Run Design Studio in timed rounds for groups of five to eight.** Frame the problem, have each person sketch about six ideas solo, give each a 3-minute presentation and critique, iterate in pairs, and finish with team convergence; larger groups get split. Reference timings: problem definition 15 to 45 minutes, solo sketching 10 minutes, pair refinement 10 minutes, convergence 45 minutes; short variants use rounds of about 8 minutes repeated about 3 times, or 6 to 8 sketches in 5 minutes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.76-81, 93; B78 p.101-102; B74 p.96, 195
- **Score options against criteria agreed before scoring.** Agree the criteria with all stakeholders, rate each idea per criterion (1 to 5 or yes/no), and use a scorecard of agreed design values so a disliked element is isolated instead of sinking the whole design. Confidence: low. Sources: B71 p.52, 72; B38 p.83-85, 103-104
- **Record decisions visibly and stop reopening them.** Keep a visible list of settled items and only reopen one at an agreed milestone or when genuinely new information appears; a short two-item debate survey after the first brainstorm and after concept selection shows whether the team is stuck. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.165, 167-168; B38 p.33, 59
- **Act out group sketches with one person as the interface.** One participant plays the device and another the user, both narrating, which exposes flow gaps fast; time-box simple paper prototypes to 10 to 15 minutes. Confidence: low. Sources: B74 p.96-97
- **Feed a broad batch of generated directions into a group rating.** When producing assets like logos, having many directions generated and letting the team vote on favorites pushes the choices past the habits of any single designer. Confidence: low. Sources: B75 p.458

## Critique: what a useful comment contains

- **Tie every critique point to a goal, a specific element and a reason.** A useful comment names the design choice, the objective or principle it serves, and how it helps or hurts; gut reactions and change instructions are not critique. The same four questions (objective, related elements, effective or not, why) apply to wireframes, visuals, personas and principles. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.23-28, 42, 51-52, 110; B89 p.56; B34 p.79; B96 p.100-101; B58 p.455-456; B78 p.95-96
- **State the problem in user terms, separately from any fix.** Describe what the user is trying to do, what the screen shows them and where they are likely to go wrong, before proposing a solution; a bare solution blocks shared understanding and alternative fixes. In live critique, park proposed fixes and ask which problem they address. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.174, 176, 309; B09 p.43, 131-132; B78 p.95-96; B20 p.634, 641-644
- **In written reviews, attach a reasoned recommendation to each problem.** Teams act on concrete "change this element in this place" advice with its rationale, while undocumented or fix-free reports get ignored or read as approval. Log each fault as its own item so a partial fix still removes some. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.56, 109, 111; B20 p.634, 641-644; B58 p.455-456, 463; B28 p.307-309
- **Point out what works with the same precision as what fails.** Explicit strengths can be reused elsewhere and stops a strong element from being changed in the next round because nobody defended it; reports should record positive findings too. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.47-48, 196; B28 p.307-309, 313-317; B58 p.455-456; B89 p.109, 111
- **Drop the praise sandwich.** Token praise wrapped around criticism signals bad news and is forgotten once the negative lands; discuss real strengths and real weaknesses as separate items. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.171-172
- **Flag whose viewpoint a comment represents, and settle preference fights by watching users.** Reviewers are not the target user; mark a point as a user view or an expert view, treat instant personal like or dislike as self-referencing, and discount color remarks from test sessions unless roughly 3 of 4 participants react strongly against the palette. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.48; B77 p.116-120, 180; B72 p.77, 80; B67 p.71-73
- **Ask "why" until a vague or preference comment becomes an objective.** "Less blue" can turn into "stand out from competitors"; "I hate them all" becomes a question about layout, color or type plus examples the client likes; "look traditional" becomes a communication goal. Paraphrase back and ask what would happen if the design stayed as is. Phrase "why" as an invitation ("tell me more"), not an attack. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.44-45, 146-147, 166-167; B95 p.151-152; B39 p.33, 37; B96 p.100-101
- **Build a critique vocabulary so you can say exactly why something fails.** Reading design writing gives you the terms to name a flaw and propose a fix instead of complaining; misapplied rules (for example short-term memory limits used to cap visual menus) and jokey tone undermine reviews. Confidence: low. Sources: B57 p.76; B58 p.455-456
- **Before critiquing, ask where the work stands and what help is wanted.** Knowing the intended fidelity avoids nitpicking early concepts or reopening settled decisions; presenters should open by stating the stage and the question they want answered. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.49-50, 144-146; B85 p.50, 101
- **Offer critique only when asked, and ask for it only when you will act on it.** Uninvited critique triggers defensiveness; requests for feedback that are really requests for praise teach people to stop contributing. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.42, 46-47, 55-57
- **Explain your work rather than defend it, and invite people to break it.** Getting defensive shifts the discussion from the work to the person; naming downsides yourself, listening silently while the group attacks an idea (ritual dissent, about a 5-minute pitch then silence), and pushing with concrete "what if" alternatives all improve the work. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.60, 194, 196-197; B38 p.66; B96 p.109; B25 p.23; B95 p.117
- **Critique while the idea can be explained but can still change.** The useful window runs from roughly 20 percent to 80 percent complete; seek feedback early, including from non-designers, and test cheap suggestions before rejecting them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.102-105; B05 p.137, 351-352; B95 p.67-68
- **Respond to a counter-mockup by comparing objectives.** When a stakeholder sends their own design, study it privately, then discuss which problems it solves and what it keeps, against the shared goals. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.180-183

## Critique: running the session

- **Set ground rules that neutralize rank.** Equal standing regardless of seniority, everyone contributes, one conversation at a time, no devices, and "yes, and" instead of "yes, but"; committees fail without an objective, a leader and organized feedback. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.129-134; B96 p.56-58; B71 p.70-72; B78 p.95-96
- **Send focused material ahead instead of a big reveal.** First-time viewers can only react, so circulate just the relevant flow about 2 days ahead with a short note on what to look at, share rough findings early, and ask attendees to read the report before a debrief. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.136-137; B17 p.22; B30 p.276-281
- **Present briefly, from the user's side.** Do not narrate what people can already see; walk through the experience and the interactions they cannot see, let rationale come out through questions, and mention constraints only when asked so they do not sound like excuses. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.139-142
- **Give everyone the same lens at the same time.** Six Thinking Hats, one persona at a time, or assigned roles such as anthropologist or experimenter keep the group analyzing one aspect together and turn reflexive objection into evidence-based argument. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.150-151; B57 p.40-41
- **Keep one public set of notes and turn it into ranked actions.** A single visible note taker lets people correct the record; afterwards fix quick hits, turn observations into exploration tasks, rank by relevance to goals, timing and agreement, and write down the reason for every item you decide to leave. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.151-152, 155-156; B28 p.263, 279-281
- **Keep critique groups to about six people.** Beyond about six a critique conversation splinters, and huge review calls cannot hold a coherent discussion; allow about 3 to 5 minutes of group critique per person. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.123-124, 135; B96 p.91-93
- **Hold short, regular critiques and cut back when they stall progress.** A workable cadence is a 30-minute weekly session with one or two presenters, one leader per team of five or fewer, costing under 5 percent of team time; if discussions circle or critique becomes validation-seeking, reduce frequency. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.99-100, 108-109; B85 p.53
- **Keep critique separate from approval reviews.** Reviews seek sign-off, produce change lists and attract many attendees with their own agendas; run critique beforehand so the review holds no surprises. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.122-125, 197
- **Prefer real-time critique to comment threads.** Asynchronous comments force reviewers to guess intent; when async is unavoidable, state the objective, the exact focus and every assumption. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.61-62, 153-154
- **Have someone other than the product owner facilitate.** The owner cares most about the result; a neutral facilitator runs the clock and captures contributions in the speakers' own words. Confidence: low. Sources: B96 p.42-43
- **Bring in an outside designer when a discussion stalls for 15 minutes.** A fresh view breaks loops that the core team cannot. Confidence: low. Sources: B26 p.181

## Group inspection and observation

- **Use group review for breadth, but only with knowledgeable members.** Teams found noticeably more problems than individuals (about 19 versus 13); discussion added about 16 percent more problems for experts and 15 percent for non-experts but nothing for developers, and over half of non-expert reports described errors that could not occur. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.211, 214-215, 217, 248
- **Have one trained person review the whole finding set together.** Isolated reports contradict each other (one report calls scroll bars on a short list wasted space while another warns that hiding them conceals scrolling), so someone must reconcile trade-offs and generalize fixes. Confidence: low. Sources: B89 p.305, 313, 315
- **In group walkthroughs, let users speak before designers and developers.** Everyone writes their intended action first, users comment first, and developers are briefed not to explain problems away. Confidence: low. Sources: B89 p.90-93
- **Do not redesign mid-study.** Observers should see all sessions before generalizing, should resist changing anything on the strength of a single complaint, and should note what participants never notice; defer redesign talk to the end. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.322-323; B28 p.263, 279-281
- **Turn observers into co-analysts who keep a running issue list.** Between sessions take about 10 minutes to agree how each issue is worded and mark which participants ran into it; each observer writes their 3 most important observations; vote priorities at the end. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.263, 279-281; B30 p.246; B50 p.54-55
- **Validate and analyse research with the people who meet users.** Group analysis with category cards and workshops where practitioners must recognize proposed user groups both verify the data and build ownership, even if an analyst alone would be faster. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.45-46, 54-55, 57
- **Staff formal inspections deliberately.** Reference points: test-flight reviews about 1 hour, consistency inspections half a day to a full day with unanimous agreement, formal inspection teams of 4 to 8 with a one-day class first. Confidence: low. Sources: B89 p.114-115, 168, 176, 184
- **Interview in pairs: one asks, one takes notes.** Splitting the roles keeps the interviewer focused on the participant. Confidence: low. Sources: B67 p.45

## Stakeholders and buy-in

- **Make stakeholders watch real users.** Live observation persuades where argument fails; effective teams give every member at least 2 hours of user exposure every 6 weeks, getting stakeholders to the first 2 interviews tends to keep them coming, and a research dashboard can track days since the last test and share of the team that observed. Limit in-room observers to one. Confidence: high. Sources: B30 p.23, 240-241; B03 p.321, 578; B85 p.49, 54, 58; B17 p.21; B77 p.116-120
- **Bring a concrete artifact to every meeting.** A prototype or journey map aligns mental models, shortens meetings and replaces debates built on assumptions; keep one always-current link, date shared files, and open every status meeting with the newest version. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.49, 54, 58; B87 p.14-16; B67 p.71-73; B74 p.199-201
- **Match prototype fidelity to the audience and the question.** Keep collaborative artifacts rough so non-designers contribute and feedback stays on flow; show stakeholders mid fidelity, clients their own brand, and developers high fidelity with motion and specs. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.72-73; B74 p.106, 128; B85 p.51-52, 102
- **In stakeholder interviews, move from their solution to the problem.** Ask which problems the redesign must solve, what evidence shows those problems are real and how they are tracked, and what changed since earlier attempts failed; when asked to build something, first ask why. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.82-87; B29 p.288
- **Win support with numbers and a small pilot.** Quantify the pain (support calls times cost, errors, stale or ownerless pages), begin with a quiet pilot, for example error messages or an added question in an existing test, and change the processes you control first. Confidence: medium. Sources: B55 p.182-185; B64 p.105-107; B29 p.69, 312-313, 317
- **Sort stakeholders by their power over the work, not by org chart.** Find who decides, who funds, champions, potential showstoppers and interested others, and include front-line staff, not only managers. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.60-61
- **Hold the kickoff once early findings exist.** Frame the problem with real data, explain who plays which part, and make clear how each person takes part and how decisions get made. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.62-63
- **Channel strong stakeholder opinions into bounded choices and roles.** Present a short, pre-screened set of options, hand controlling stakeholders the low-stakes calls, turn vocal users into beta testers, accept harmless client input so they own the result, and when you disagree deliver the requested version beside your recommended one with its rationale. Confidence: low. Sources: B57 p.39-40; B95 p.86-87, 213-214
- **Weigh stakeholder input for domain knowledge over taste.** Expect color and font comments without getting defensive, but lean most on their knowledge of the business and its aims. Confidence: low. Sources: B74 p.201
- **Execute a client-mandated choice with full craft.** If a weak choice is insisted on, make it as good as it can be rather than sabotaging it. Confidence: low. Sources: B95 p.81-82
- **Involve developers early and treat the prototype as shared language.** Naming a developer contact for brainstorms and reviews avoids features quietly dropped and arguments reopened at handoff, and a prototype shows states and conditions the way engineers reason. Confidence: low. Sources: B38 p.33, 59; B87 p.14-15
- **Agree revision limits and fixed elements up front.** Document the number of revision cycles per deliverable, and if different people own wireframes and visuals, flag the elements that are fixed and leave the rest open. Confidence: low. Sources: B38 p.31, 39
- **Align on visual direction early with a mood board.** Show color, typography, illustration and icon style, navigation treatment, spacing and contrast so reviewers react to something concrete instead of a blank canvas. Confidence: low. Sources: B78 p.97-100

## Workshops

- **Keep ideation and sprint workshops under about eight people.** Sprint teams work best at 4 to 5 with the decider included, Design Studio at 5 to 8, and general workshops at 8 or fewer; split larger groups into pods of 4 to 6. Confidence: medium. Sources: B23 p.281, 300; B35 p.91; B34 p.76-81; B96 p.43, 45; B78 p.101
- **Run alignment and mapping workshops with 6 to 12 people in breakout groups.** Invite people with knowledge rather than the whole company, prefer several smaller sessions, hold a prep meeting about a week before, and plan 1 to 3 days for full agendas. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.61, 212-215; B67 p.51-52
- **When users join, never let stakeholders outnumber them.** Power dynamics silence users in a room full of the organization. Confidence: low. Sources: B67 p.52
- **Budget enough time for each workshop type.** Ideation and Design Studio need at least about 3 hours; a first journey mapping session about 2 hours and never more than 3; a fill-in-the-gaps follow-up about 1 hour; a research-to-ideas workshop about 3 hours (45 presenting, 15 questions, 90 generating, 30 sharing) or 1 hour for a usability fix session. Confidence: medium. Sources: B23 p.281; B34 p.76-81; B67 p.56, 65; B03 p.568, 572
- **Timebox stakeholder workshop segments.** Reference timings: about 30 minutes for a brand or analytics briefing, 15 to 30 for a technical one, competitor likes 15 per participant capped at 90, elevator pitch at least 1 hour, template or content prioritization 30 to 60 per screen, collaborative wireframing 30 to 60 per screen, introductions 30 to 60 seconds each, daily retrospective 10 to 15 minutes and 30 at the end. Confidence: low. Sources: B23 p.137-145, 289, 293-296; B96 p.54-55, 76
- **Count the mapping session itself as an outcome, and start it with the research.** Participation breaks silos; present findings, personas and empathy maps first (even as a small exhibit), have people write on the diagram and grade each phase, and use diverging scores between groups to start discussion. Confidence: medium. Sources: B67 p.50-51, 56-58; B64 p.198-200
- **Prepare the map template and fill it in a fixed order.** Put persona and scope at the top and rows on the left, leave phase names blank, complete the phase row before working through columns from left to right, demonstrate with the first column, hold a parking lot for stray ideas, and leave opportunities until the whole map is visible. Confidence: low. Sources: B67 p.52-55, 58-62, 64-65
- **At each phase ask what blocks access, skill, time or money.** Many steps suggest a skill barrier and heavy drop-off suggests a time barrier. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.203

## Reporting findings

- **Lead with the conclusion, then the evidence.** Put the most important finding first in one sentence, group themes into eight or fewer, give each finding a scannable headline with its recommendation, fix global structural issues before field-level ones, and limit any presentation to fewer than 7 key points and each deliverable to 2 or 3 key messages. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.553, 560-561; B17 p.148-150; B28 p.307-309, 313-317; B58 p.39, 651; B62 p.128, 144-145; B29 p.223-228
- **Report counts, not vague quantifiers.** Write "four of six participants", never "most users". Confidence: low. Sources: B03 p.553
- **Keep reports short and glanceable.** If readers must turn the page it is too long; nobody reads a 35-page report or sits through an hour; prefer a one-page summary, a summary email or 3-slide deck, one-page radiators such as dashboards and journey maps, and a 60-second story. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.25; B06 p.245-255; B17 p.22, 152-155; B28 p.311-312
- **Use short video evidence.** Executives get highlight reels under 3 to 5 minutes with clips under 1 to 3 minutes each, covering 3 to 5 topics; expect about an hour of editing per finished minute. Confidence: medium. Sources: B77 p.197; B17 p.166; B28 p.322-324; B03 p.561-564; B06 p.245-255
- **Run debriefs as discussions, not slide shows.** Co-chair with the product owner, talk for about a quarter of the time (15 of 60 minutes), focus on the five most severe problems, agree problems before solutions, and keep a third of any presentation for questions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.276-281; B03 p.563-564; B17 p.166
- **Present usability results as screenshot sequences with notes.** Pin each task screen in order and attach color-coded notes for quotes, findings, questions and agreed actions; a hallway poster holds about 5 items. Confidence: low. Sources: B30 p.246-248
- **Test the report with a reader before finalizing.** Hand over a draft, wait a day or two, then hold a 10 to 15 minute conversation about what they understood. Confidence: low. Sources: B03 p.561-564
- **Share finished maps in many formats.** A short subtitled video (about 5 minutes), a wall poster and links from related tickets keep the map in use. Confidence: low. Sources: B67 p.69-70

## Team structure and feedback culture

- **Keep core design teams small.** Core teams of 2 to 6 full-time members work; no more than about 10 core people per product team; a core team of 25 cannot get work done. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.182; B06 p.322; B34 p.35, 166; B55 p.61
- **Give shared systems owners and ambassadors, not enforcers.** A lead, a team or per-team ambassadors keep a system alive through office hours, on-call help and education; content councils often run 12 to 18 months with a chair allocated about 25 percent of their time. Confidence: low. Sources: B22 p.279-284; B55 p.159-160
- **Mix disciplines in provisional teams that expect failure.** Cross-discipline teams formed before a brief exists can rehearse future practice and treat mistakes as learning. Confidence: low. Sources: B45 p.80-82
- **Agree rules for AI-generated work before using it.** Decide who reviews generated output, how it is judged, who implements it, and how platform terms affect ownership (some make outputs visible to all users). Confidence: low. Sources: B75 p.319, 527-529
- **Make feedback specific, prompt and routine, with care and directness together.** Say why something worked close to the event; do not withhold hard feedback to stay liked; use "what went well, even better if" for quick notes and situation, result, impact for difficult ones. Confidence: low. Sources: B13 p.118-119, 189-193
- **Rehearse talks aloud for about three times their length.** Speaking practice exposes confusing content; review a recording silent for body language and audio-only for clarity, and replace hedges with finished, active sentences. Confidence: low. Sources: B13 p.126-130
- **Compare all attempts side by side before reaching for theory.** Learn by producing effects, showing every attempt and critiquing as a group, and lay out each study so the intended effect cannot be misread. Confidence: low. Sources: B90 p.27-28, 92, 95-96
- **Design shared and spectator modes for headset experiences.** Headsets isolate wearers, so add multi-user, spectator or asymmetric roles, and make the social layer add value beyond more users. Confidence: low. Sources: B48 p.153-154, 175, 238
