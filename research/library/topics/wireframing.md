# wireframing

Input rows: 24. Merged rows: 11.

- **Keep wireframes grayscale and plain.** White background, gray outlines and a single typeface; reserve blue for links and buttons and red for errors, mark images with crossed boxes, and set supplemental text at about 50 percent gray; leave out gradients and rounded corners so reviewers judge placement and task support rather than styling. Confidence: medium. Sources: B62 p.231-237; B38 p.28, 46; B06 p.191-192
- **Make hierarchy work in grayscale before adding color.** Spacing, contrast and size must carry the hierarchy on their own so color becomes an enhancement, not a crutch. Confidence: medium. Sources: B47 p.12-13; B38 p.28, 46; B62 p.231-237
- **Put real copy in wireframes, never lorem ipsum.** Include every label, button, call to action, navigation item and dropdown option, since the length of content sets the layout; symbolic formats (a currency mask or date pattern, for instance) and labeled placeholders belong only in specs, never in a usability test. Confidence: medium. Sources: B27 p.152-153; B62 p.238-240
- **Sketch the happy path first, then every state before the wireframe is tested or handed off.** Early sketches can leave edge cases out, but a working wireframe notes what each interactive element does, including errors, misuse, popups and defaults; vague boxes cannot resolve behavior. Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.94; B27 p.105-106; B78 p.103-105
- **Sketch before you code, and sketch in the room.** Throwing away a five-minute sketch costs far less than throwing away five hours of code; sketching live in meetings surfaces misunderstandings early, and users can take the pen as well. Limit each wireframe to roughly 30 minutes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B57 p.68-70; B96 p.94; B38 p.71
- **Diverge quickly with timed sketching.** Fold one sheet into eight frames and put a different idea in each, with about 30 seconds of sketching and 10 of rest per frame, five minutes in total, using thick markers; present each in a minute or less (30 seconds when more than six people take part). Confidence: low. Sources: B96 p.85-87; B25 p.50
- **Storyboard people, not just screens.** A three-frame storyboard with short captions and human moments, made in about 20 minutes, explains an idea better than screenshots; leave real names off. Confidence: low. Sources: B96 p.88-89
- **Give every screen one primary task and arrange screens into flows.** Name the main task each page serves and make the ways into it obvious before adding secondary content; sketch steps on small cards so flows can be reordered, and work from the outside in: device, screens, elements, then interactions and state changes. Confidence: low. Sources: B38 p.29; B74 p.78, 81, 167
- **Size paper prototypes to the device.** Use letter or A4 for desktop, half-letter or A5 for tablets (A4 for the biggest tablets), and for phones A5 sheets, index cards or sticky notes; a slit of 1 to 2 mm lets a paper strip simulate scrolling. Confidence: low. Sources: B74 p.78-80, 83
- **Link wireframes and test them on the real device.** Linked wireframes deliver roughly 80 percent of a fully interactive prototype's benefit; a black and white medium-fidelity screen on the target device, tested informally with 3 to 5 people, isolates layout and flow from styling. Confidence: low. Sources: B27 p.154; B06 p.191-192; B78 p.103-105
- **Keep wireframes maintainable.** Mark components with short codes on a separate, hideable layer and flag new library candidates; refresh sitemaps and wireframes after about every five changes. Confidence: low. Sources: B62 p.139-140, 214; B35 p.217
