## Labels and buttons

- **Label buttons with a verb and an object that state what happens.** One-word labels such as OK, Add, Done or Submit leave people guessing; Create project, Place order or Add record let people decide by reading the button alone, and the label may even carry the amount or the commitment (start a paid plan). Precise action wording is among the most violated and most important guidelines. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.754-755, 757, 779; B54 p.153; B33 p.64-67, 107-108; B07 p.211; B26 p.245, 480; B14 p.59-61; B58 p.305-308
- **Use one term per concept everywhere, and different terms for different concepts.** Alternating password and PIN, alert and warning, or Calendar and Organizer confuses users and multiplies translation cost; keep a shared glossary across UI, help and documentation, and make instructions name controls exactly as labeled. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.760-766; B52 p.166; B58 p.305-308; B49 p.74, 262, 349; B08 p.35, 40, 59; B39 p.139-141; B22 p.60-61; B14 p.59-61; B78 p.66; B93 p.169-170
- **Name controls in the user's task language, not the system's.** Lighter and darker beats faster and slower on a toaster, most recent first beats ascending; two people pick the same name for a function less than one time in five, so test labels against users' own words. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.702, 776-777, 781; B26 p.245, 322-323; B89 p.137, 142, 146, 369; B46 p.215, 359; B86 p.34
- **Prefer plain, obvious names over clever, marketing or internal labels.** Plain labels are understood instantly; coined or brand terms force a pause to decide whether a click is worth it. Confidence: high. Sources: B77 p.25; B93 p.158-161; B33 p.107-108; B11 p.97
- **Write toggle and checkbox labels as actions, and name the hidden default.** A checkbox that reads like a status confuses before it is checked; Snooze 15 minutes or Stay signed in on this device states the rule the control applies. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.755-756; B14 p.59, 89-90; B07 p.87-88
- **Pair icons with a text label or at least an accessible tooltip.** An icon alone cannot carry meaning for first-time and infrequent users; a short hint helps them without slowing habitual users. Confidence: high. Sources: B33 p.72; B78 p.66; B14 p.95-96
- **Make the destination title repeat the words of the link or button that led there.** When the arrival heading differs from the departure label, people doubt they are in the right place. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.819-821; B46 p.616-619
- **Keep one syntax and one level of detail within a label set.** One menu should not combine verbs with nouns and questions, keep list items grammatically parallel, and avoid gaps among siblings. Confidence: medium. Sources: B93 p.177-178; B63 p.112
- **Default destructive confirmation dialogs to the safe choice.** For irreversible actions, Enter or the default button should cancel. Confidence: low. Sources: B07 p.211
- **Do not promise with a generic Get started before explaining what people will get.** The label must match the actual outcome. Confidence: low. Sources: B33 p.42
- **Label filter resets as See all rather than Remove filter.** Taking a filter away to get more results is a reversal people struggle to reason through. Confidence: low. Sources: B04 p.239-240

## Links

- **Write link text that predicts its destination and makes sense out of context.** Screen reader users often list links alone, so click here, read more and learn more fail; put the keyword first, make the headline the link, combine a picture teaser and its text into a single link, and state file type and size for documents. Confidence: high. Sources: B18 p.157-158; B46 p.616-619; B23 p.470; B93 p.163-165; B02 p.535; B39 p.104
- **Keep link labels to one line, around 9 to 10 words at most and under 60 characters for titles.** Short descriptive links performed best. Confidence: low. Sources: B46 p.616-617

## Errors, status and system messages

- **State the problem and the remedy, with the control that applies the remedy next to the message.** Error messages are the lowest-rated item in standard usability questionnaire norms, so they repay investment; avoid empty words such as error, warning, invalid, illegal or abort. Confidence: high. Sources: B14 p.120; B94 p.213; B46 p.594-599; B20 p.817, 828-831; B48 p.173
- **Let the system take the blame, without cute or first-person voice.** Drop accusatory you, avoid I and jokes in failure copy, and never use oops for real failures; state the condition plainly. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.817, 828-831; B46 p.594-599; B14 p.120; B33 p.107-108
- **Treat ordinary events as ordinary, never as failures.** An empty paper tray is a workflow step; give a plain message and a continue action, with technical detail behind a separate link. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.817-819
- **Turn raw system or model output into a plain request with a reply action.** Never show a raw score, threshold or internal code to the user. Confidence: medium. Sources: B66 p.120; B48 p.173
- **Lead confirmations with the information people need.** Put the concrete fact first (the arrival window, the amount), and avoid canned cheer that grates on repeat visits. Confidence: medium. Sources: B33 p.100-102
- **Tell people how long an uncertain wait usually lasts.** A line such as processing usually takes about 15 seconds beats silence, and the copy must stay tolerable if the wait stretches to minutes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B82 p.120; B63 p.45
- **Inform rather than command.** You have new messages leaves people in charge; you need to answer your messages takes control away. Confidence: low. Sources: B20 p.793
- **Use relative times unless the exact time matters.** Three hours ago reads faster than a raw timestamp. Confidence: low. Sources: B14 p.97

## Length and plainness

- **Cut words hard: half, then half again.** Remove welcome copy and happy talk that says nothing; survey instructions can often lose two thirds of their words with no loss. Confidence: high. Sources: B77 p.60-61, 63; B71 p.66; B14 p.95-96
- **Write at a plain reading level.** Keep sentences to about 20 words or fewer, messages to two or three lines, and aim at roughly a sixth to eighth grade level; simpler versions worked best for every reader. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.594-599, 603, 615; B40 p.30; B03 p.278-279
- **Use high-frequency words, not jargon, idioms, slang or acronyms.** Idioms exclude and can even read as threats in other cultures; define any necessary term in context. Confidence: high. Sources: B63 p.109-110; B60 p.151-154; B52 p.166; B08 p.35, 40
- **Do not refer to controls only by their color or location.** Name the control by its text; add color or location only as an extra cue (WCAG 1.3.3). Confidence: high. Sources: B18 p.113, 173
- **Choose precision over brevity when they conflict.** Ready to purchase tells people what they consent to; Ready does not. Clarifying questions from users signal over-trimmed copy. Confidence: medium. Sources: B63 p.107-108; B33 p.65-66
- **Never put instructional copy where a clear label would do.** Remove filler lines that ask people to log in or tap next; a sign explaining a control is evidence the control's design failed. Confidence: medium. Sources: B14 p.95-96; B06 p.71-72
- **Assume instructions will go unread and do not hang critical information on them.** People overlook unexpected or infrequent text. Confidence: medium. Sources: B44 p.107-108; B94 p.58-59
- **Give an example rather than a format description.** A sample like July 22 is quicker to follow than an explanation of which part is the month and which the day. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.43; B63 p.98
- **Write instructions that assume no prior knowledge.** Confidence: low. Sources: B69 p.173

## Voice and tone

- **Keep one voice and vary tone with the person's emotional state.** Define voice with paired traits (warm but not childish), soften tone in stressful moments, and keep legal and system copy in the same voice. Confidence: medium. Sources: B55 p.130-132; B39 p.28, 31, 129
- **Use humor only if the instruction remains fully clear and the moment suits it.** Whether a joke fits depends on what the person is doing, not the sector: someone reporting fraud wants empathy. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.72-73; B33 p.100-102
- **Choose framing, anchors and word order on purpose.** Presenting an outcome as a loss or a gain, the first number shown and question order all change choices; use these effects honestly. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.147-152
- **Say your for what belongs to the person and our for what belongs to the company.** Labels starting with My sound presumptuous and jar with the system speaking. Confidence: low. Sources: B33 p.109-110
- **Avoid patronizing or stigmatizing framing for accessibility features.** Name the benefit rather than calling things easy or medical. Confidence: low. Sources: B32 p.68, 80
- **Retire offensive technical metaphors in UI and docs.** Use neutral terms such as primary and replica. Confidence: low. Sources: B65 p.87

## AI and conversational copy

- **State what the assistant can do instead of inviting anything.** Open prompts set false expectations; list the scope and give two example requests before an open question. Confidence: medium. Sources: B51 p.127, 196-197; B63 p.98
- **State limits and hedge uncertain output.** Say where the system works best and where it struggles, and use calibrated wording for predictions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B51 p.128-129, 185; B16 p.371-372
- **Describe AI as a tool, not a mind.** Avoid verbs like knows or understands and inflated names that invite overreliance. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.76, 113-114
- **Keep each spoken prompt to about three sentences and end with the cue.** Ten words take about four seconds to speak; make each turn as short as still understandable and expand only when the user shows trouble. Confidence: medium. Sources: B63 p.93, 96, 106; B60 p.56, 58
- **Never ask a question whose likely answer you cannot handle, and never mix question types.** An either-or question invites yes; a yes or no opener ending in a list invites ambiguity; reserve yes or no for confirmations. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.41-42, 60; B63 p.99-102
- **Rotate and shorten repeated prompts, and orient people with markers.** Reused lines sound robotic; first, last question and got it tell people where they are. Confidence: medium. Sources: B60 p.77-78; B41 p.61-62
- **Order words in the order of action, with the actionable part last.** Confidence: low. Sources: B63 p.111-112

## Process

- **Keep microcopy requirements in their own document and test them.** Record errors, inline help and labels per state (including validation rules as if then statements) for writers and QA, and fix formatting rules such as case and date style. Confidence: high. Sources: B55 p.140-141; B62 p.72-74; B22 p.61-63, 66
- **Put actual wording into sketches and prototypes instead of dummy text.** Words carry most of the idea and often decide whether a concept works. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.122, 193
- **Proofread display text separately and in a different font.** Headline typos slip through, and switching typeface breaks autopilot reading. Confidence: low. Sources: B19 p.35, 66
