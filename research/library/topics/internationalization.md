## Scope of localization

- **Localize structure, flows and behavior, not only the words.** Even fully fluent readers failed tasks when a site's features, language selector and structure followed another country's conventions; language, formats, colors and icons are only the visible surface, while navigation, information structure, imagery, FAQ placement and the phrasing of system messages must fit the culture too. Confidence: high. Sources: B08 p.16-17; B49 p.23, 71-72; B58 p.407-409; B02 p.43, 494-495
- **Assume most of your users are not native English speakers.** Only about 8 to 10 percent of the world has English as a first language and roughly a third of web users do, and even within one country millions speak the main language less than well. Confidence: high. Sources: B08 p.20, 29; B46 p.120, 655; B18 p.50
- **Adapt flows, payment, units and services per market.** Launches have failed on imperial units, a map service that did not cover the country, a checkout accepting only cards in a market that mostly pays cash at the door and culture-bound security questions; check local payment methods and dominant platforms early. Confidence: high. Sources: B58 p.407-409; B23 p.603, 605-607; B11 p.166-167
- **Test with monolingual local users and a native, same-culture moderator.** Bilingual reviewers and long-expatriate translators miss odd phrasing, reserved participants hide criticism from the product's own designers, and inspectors predict from their own culture (a behavior tuned for one country was wrong for another). Confidence: high. Sources: B58 p.410, 637-640; B89 p.289; B06 p.267, 272; B50 p.131-132
- **QA every localized build end to end.** After localizing, verify navigation and every link, translated images and icons, pages generated from data, and display in several browsers. Confidence: low. Sources: B49 p.254

## Architecture for locales

- **Keep all locale data and text out of code and layouts.** Build a culture-free core with swappable locale resources for strings, dates, currency, addresses and names, so a new market is a resource change, and componentize even supposedly unique country pages. Confidence: high. Sources: B08 p.31, 40; B49 p.22, 72-73; B62 p.309
- **Never build sentences by concatenating strings.** Store whole messages with named placeholders that translators can reorder, avoid grammatical endings attached to variables, keep spaces around inserted values, and use platform formatters for plurals. Confidence: high. Sources: B08 p.155, 166; B49 p.264, 345-346; B52 p.85-91
- **Keep text out of images and icons.** Put captions outside the graphic and rework keyboard mnemonics per language. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.345-346; B46 p.657-662
- **Pseudo-localize from the first build.** Ship with two languages from day one and run a pseudo-locale that lengthens strings by about 40 percent and swaps in characters from the target scripts, which reveals untranslated literals, clipped labels and undersized fields before translators start. Confidence: medium. Sources: B52 p.85-91; B49 p.275
- **Budget localization effort by writing system, not by language count.** Right-to-left scripts with contextual shaping and multibyte Asian scripts with input methods cost several times more to support (roughly five times) than Western European scripts. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.266-268

## Text length

- **Size every text container for translation growth, scaled by string length.** Short strings grow most: up to 10 characters can grow 100 to 200 percent, 11 to 20 about 80 to 100, 21 to 30 about 60 to 80, 31 to 50 about 40 to 60, 51 to 70 about 31 to 40, longer text about 30 percent; German typically runs 30 to 50 percent longer than English and Arabic also needs extra room, so give labels at least 50 percent slack, let widgets grow or wrap instead of abbreviating, and leave slack in non-wrapping navigation. Some scripts contract (Chinese runs about half the length of English), so layouts must also survive shorter text. Confidence: high. Sources: B46 p.657-662; B23 p.603-604; B11 p.166; B06 p.264-267; B49 p.74, 274; B52 p.87-88; B92 p.146; B88 p.182

## Formats

- **Format numbers, currency, dates and units through locale data.** Separators differ (1,234.56, 1.234,56, 1 234,56, 1'234.56), digit grouping differs (India groups as 12,34,56,789), and both the placement of the currency sign and the number of decimals vary, negatives appear four ways (leading or trailing minus, parentheses, color), and date order is month-day-year in the US, day-month-year in Europe and year-month-day across much of Asia; use ISO 4217 codes for international audiences and convert units by function. Confidence: high. Sources: B08 p.59-69; B49 p.122, 269-270; B52 p.91-93; B58 p.424-425, 433-436; B22 p.64-67; B23 p.603, 606-607; B02 p.43
- **Store and transmit dates as ISO 8601 and display them locally.** A short numeric date such as 8/9/16 means different days in different regions, and a device's region setting may not match its interface language; where one display format must serve everyone, spell out the month and use a four-digit year, and do not assume three-letter month abbreviations are distinct in every language. Confidence: high. Sources: B52 p.92-93; B08 p.59-62; B58 p.424-425
- **Make week start, weekend and calendar configurable.** US calendars begin the week on Sunday, much of Europe on Monday and Egypt on Saturday; weekends fall on Friday and Saturday or Thursday and Friday in some Muslim-majority countries, and some users follow Hijri, Hebrew, Persian or Japanese calendars; a wrong week start makes people book the wrong day. Confidence: high. Sources: B61 p.51, 312; B08 p.63; B58 p.424-425
- **Do not assume Western name, address and phone structures.** Many people have no middle name, name order varies, and phone numbers group differently with different country codes; size and label fields accordingly. Confidence: high. Sources: B58 p.407-409; B23 p.606-607; B08 p.31, 40
- **State the time zone for every scheduled time and follow the local clock.** Most countries use a 24-hour clock while US users expect a 12-hour clock with a.m. and p.m., and one relative-time style should be used consistently. Confidence: medium. Sources: B22 p.64-66; B52 p.94
- **Parse typed numbers and dates by the user's locale.** Truncating a typed price at the separator turned 23.566,00 into 23 in one product; validate against the locale's separators. Confidence: low. Sources: B58 p.433-436

## Language choice

- **Never use flags for languages; list each language in its own name.** A flag represents a nation, not a language: one language spans many nations and one nation may use several, so choosing languages by flag is inaccurate and may offend; make the switcher easy to find and do not assume one language per country. Confidence: high. Sources: B08 p.21; B11 p.166-167; B46 p.657-662

## Right to left and bidirectional text

- **Design for bidirectional layout from the start.** Arabic and Hebrew text flows from right to left, yet digits and any Latin words inside it still flow from left to right, so layouts, alignment, reading order and image sequences must mirror, Arabic sets flush right while Latin in the same layout stays flush left, and the menu side flips. Confidence: high. Sources: B88 p.119, 176-177; B08 p.48, 52-53, 208; B06 p.264-267; B11 p.166; B23 p.603; B10 p.184
- **Mirror every control, not just the page.** In right-to-left locales labels align right, checkboxes sit to the right of their label, dropdown arrows move left, list and table columns reverse order, scrollbars move to the left edge, and progress bars grow from the right. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.261
- **Check direction-based mappings and metaphors for the culture.** Readers of right-to-left scripts tend to place the future on the left, so timelines, sequences, next and back arrows and scroll metaphors are cultural; when switching a metaphor, switch it everywhere, since mixed systems confuse most. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.125-129, 156; B08 p.52-53
- **Use the emphasis tools each script really has.** Arabic has no capitals or italics, so emphasize with a different style, weight, color or controlled letter elongation; line up each script by its own guide line when mixing them (the Arabic central line matched to the Latin baseline) rather than forcing a shared baseline. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.36-37, 109, 176-178, 189, 191
- **Give CJK text its own setting rules.** Chinese and Japanese have no word spaces, Chinese often needs more leading and larger sizes, and body lines of about 20 to 30 characters are typical; when pairing Latin with Korean or Chinese, adjust size and vertical position. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.182, 189, 191; B58 p.407-409
- **Follow each language's punctuation conventions.** Quotation marks differ (double, single, outward or inward guillemets, baseline openers); confirm with a native speaker. Confidence: low. Sources: B88 p.78-79
- **Check that letterforms are legible to the target readers.** Some handwritten or script forms read poorly to readers from other cultures. Confidence: low. Sources: B31 p.320

## Culture, meaning and voice

- **Check color meanings per target market and document them.** White is a mourning color across parts of East Asia and India yet stands for purity in other places, red signals luck and celebration in China, green is sacred in Islam, and some hues carry historic stigma for particular communities; treat semantic color as a documented decision, not a universal law. Confidence: high. Sources: B68 p.192-196; B53 p.126, 202, 299, 319; B76 p.66, 164-167; B37 p.45, 139, 209-210
- **Transcreate voice and conversational content rather than translating it.** Recreate the emotional effect for each culture; a bot that speaks a dialect must also understand it, so localize prompts and training phrases together, and adjust formality, directness, forms of address and shared references. Confidence: high. Sources: B33 p.97; B63 p.232-235; B73 p.27-28
- **Treat cultural density preferences as tendencies to test, not rules.** High-context cultures, Arab countries among them, tend to accept denser, more varied and more animated pages while low-context cultures expect compact, consistent pages, but similarities across countries often outweigh differences; validate with local users. Confidence: high. Sources: B11 p.164, 166; B49 p.198-199; B50 p.128-133
- **Avoid culture-bound icons, gestures and metaphors.** Hand gestures, pointing fingers, feet, sports metaphors, mailboxes and flags do not travel; localize scenarios as well. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.657-662; B06 p.267, 272
- **Test AI features separately in every supported language.** Models trained on large corpora perform worse in lower-resource languages, moderation fails most outside English, and one global policy fits no region. Confidence: medium. Sources: B65 p.112; B43 p.172-174
- **Review color names per locale instead of translating them.** Languages divide the spectrum differently (separate light and dark blue, separate yellow-green), so swatch and picker labels need local review. Confidence: low. Sources: B37 p.39-40; B53 p.365-366, 437-438
- **Offer both gendered forms when a translation is ambiguous.** Do not silently pick a gender for a gender-neutral source sentence. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.432
- **Plan the cultural approach to personas and studies.** Include a persona who does not speak the main language, choose between neutral, per-market or stereotype-challenging global personas, cover the two to four key regions with identical recruiting criteria, and have local partners verify the analysis. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.46, 59, 128-136
