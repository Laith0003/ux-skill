# ai-ux
Input rows: 299. Output rows: 80.

Confidence note: AI is technology-dependent, so every 2018+ source gets +1. Rule 5 of sources.md lifts any lesson that three or more independent books support to high.

## Trust and calibration

- **Calibrate trust to what the system can really do.** Trust above capability produces harmful reliance and trust below it produces abandonment, so keep calibrating at every stage of use rather than only on day one, and treat it as an explicit goal in health, safety and rights domains. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.121-122, 131-132; B45 p.38-40; B66 p.23-26; B43 p.52-53
- **Design against automation bias by prompting verification.** People follow a wrong automated prompt far more often than a paper procedure (about 75 versus 25 percent in one aviation study), so never present model output as the final word on anything consequential. Confidence: high. Sources: B43 p.52-53; B82 p.131-132, 143; B79 p.214-215
- **Present model output as a likely answer, not a settled fact.** Outputs are probabilistic (never 100 percent), arrive fluent even when wrong, and an authoritative voice makes fabrication believable; carry the uncertainty in the interface and invite the user's judgment. Confidence: high. Sources: B16 p.48, 61, 370; B43 p.96, 127; B65 p.212-213; B51 p.38, 56; B82 p.131-132; B75 p.157, 465-469
- **Describe capability and limits plainly, never in hype.** Products judged against overclaims fail even when they work, and calling a system smarter than experts invites people to hand it decisions it cannot carry; say what the feature does and what it cannot do. Confidence: high. Sources: B65 p.16-17, 221-223; B66 p.36-43, 64-66; B43 p.108, 216; B75 p.140
- **Avoid humanlike avatars and personality for task tools.** Anthropomorphic helpers interrupted, distracted and felt deceptive, and even simple chat programs make people attribute understanding that is not there; keep framing close to what the system actually does. Confidence: high. Sources: B21 p.132, 181; B43 p.151, 207; B75 p.140, 178
- **Build trust through small, low-risk first exposures.** Many users skip onboarding; a contained, reversible action tied to one feature builds the mental model, and familiarity with assistive features raises comfort with more automation later. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.157-159, 183; B66 p.124-125; B45 p.38-39
- **Use the published human-AI interaction guidelines as a checklist.** The set of 18 covers: state what the system can do and how reliably, offer help at the right time, make it simple to call up, wave off or fix, show why it acted, carry the last few exchanges forward, change its behavior gradually, and tell people when it changes. Confidence: high. Sources: B66 p.135-136; B21 p.83; B11 p.125
- **Hold AI to a higher bar than a human doing the same job.** People forgive their own slips but call a device broken at the same error rate, a first bad experience suppresses the whole category, and an assistant stuck near one-in-three accuracy is dropped within three to five uses. Confidence: medium. Sources: B66 p.7-8, 18, 24-26, 42-45, 124; B12 p.120
- **Label who made each piece of content.** Tag each item as written by a person, by the model or by both, retain an edit record, and tell users whether a person or a machine is answering them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.195-196, 224; B65 p.74, 77

## Control and autonomy

- **Give every automated feature visible state and a manual override.** Automation handles easy cases and fails silently on hard ones; any automation that can act must be disclosed, show its mode conspicuously, and offer activate, operate and stop controls. Confidence: high. Sources: B21 p.59-60, 75; B79 p.214-215; B02 p.114, 125, 288; B51 p.94-95
- **Keep a preview, edit and undo path, and a non-AI way to finish.** Prefill, autocomplete and suggestions should speed people up when they hit and stay out of the way of typing it yourself when they miss; when the system is unsure or fails, fall back to the ordinary experience or a conventional tool. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.94-95; B12 p.52, 258-259; B16 p.129-132; B75 p.247-248
- **Treat control and automation as two axes, not one slider.** Automate the reliable technical parts and leave goals, judgment and the decisive moment to people; autonomy comes in levels (a common scale runs 1 to 10, with 2 to 5 augmenting and 6 to 10 automating), and every level short of full autonomy keeps a person in the loop. Confidence: high. Sources: B21 p.61, 72-74, 97; B51 p.103-104; B66 p.66-69
- **Automate dull work and augment work people own.** Leave people in charge of work they like doing, work with serious consequences, work someone must answer for, or work too fuzzy to specify; position AI as a teammate to experts, never as their replacement. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.91-94; B66 p.84-86; B12 p.34-35, 43-44; B21 p.97
- **Make suggestions easy to ignore, and let ignoring mean no.** Ghost text, squiggles and alternates that need an explicit key to accept leave the decision and timing with the user; never commit a suggestion on a neutral key or auto-send a one-tap reply. Confidence: high. Sources: B21 p.87-88; B16 p.406-408; B51 p.163-164
- **Let users correct predictions inside the flow.** Choosing another route, rating a pick, editing a suggested label or removing a term that drove a classification all improve satisfaction and feed the model. Confidence: high. Sources: B61 p.277; B21 p.179-180; B51 p.173-176; B60 p.111-112
- **Offer a few ranked options with their consequences.** Two to four choices with estimates (as route planners do), and several variations per generation, keep the user deciding; a single-answer layout signals more certainty than the content has, so call out each option's downsides. Confidence: high. Sources: B21 p.182; B51 p.154; B16 p.374-380; B75 p.243-244, 319
- **Have AI propose edits, never overwrite.** Embedded AI should change shared work through the same tracked-change, comment and diff tools as people, and ask before altering text the user wrote. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.188-189, 195; B82 p.154-155
- **Steer recommendations with a few sliders that update results live.** Preference sliders produced more engagement, better understanding and a strong stated wish for control, even though users took longer. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.59, 185-188
- **Expect demand for control to rise with the stakes.** People accept defaults for entertainment picks but want to steer medical, financial and career decisions, so scale the controls to the consequence. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.58, 187
- **Keep familiar controls where habit expects them.** Search, settings, back, close and sign out never shift position; model-driven content lives only in reserved areas of the layout. Confidence: low. Sources: B51 p.287-288

## Confidence and uncertainty

- **Tie the display cutoff to what an error would cost.** A casual label can show at 60 to 80 percent, medical or lending decisions need about 98 to 99 percent, suggestions the user will vet can show from about 50 percent, and below the bar the product should say so and offer rephrasing or escalation. Confidence: high. Sources: B61 p.126-127, 427; B16 p.268; B21 p.58; B12 p.53
- **Show confidence as words or buckets, and only after testing it helps.** Raw percentages mislead or invite blind trust (84.3 versus 85 means nothing), an LLM percentage is a token likelihood rather than an accuracy rate, and model scores must be converted to probabilities before any display. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.150-155; B82 p.142; B61 p.251-252, 259-260
- **Give the model an out-of-scope and a maybe answer.** A classifier answers only inside its trained world, so add an explicit not-recognized path and a neutral class; adding a neutral class lifted accuracy from 0.56 to 0.79 in one case. Confidence: medium. Sources: B61 p.29, 146-149, 409; B12 p.53
- **Bound model output with deterministic rules and fallbacks.** Hard limits on spend, price and edge cases, caching, rate limits and graceful handling of an unavailable model keep a probabilistic part from producing an unacceptable result. Confidence: medium. Sources: B12 p.53; B75 p.498-499
- **Put a price on every kind of hit and miss before picking a model.** Give each correct and incorrect yes and no a value; accuracy alone can hide a model that misses more than half of real problems, so use precision when false alarms cost more and recall when misses do. Confidence: medium. Sources: B12 p.103-110, 118-123
- **Show the estimate in natural units and let users judge.** People define late, risky or expensive differently, so a predicted delay in minutes beats a fixed verdict label. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.379
- **Size alert and suggestion volume to what people can process.** Tune the model to the number a person or team can act on per day (a salesperson handles roughly 10 prospects); too many and too few are both useless. Confidence: low. Sources: B12 p.127

## Explanation

- **Explain the benefit and the reason, at the moment of effect.** Say what the user gains from the feature, add short cues such as because you did X or why am I seeing this right after the triggering action, and keep model internals in progressive disclosure. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.134-135, 146-150; B16 p.401-405; B12 p.53-54; B56 p.153
- **Make explanations actionable, local and contestable.** Tell people which inputs they could alter to reach another outcome and how to dispute it; never explain with factors they cannot change, and use the nearest contrasting case to show the decision boundary. Confidence: high. Sources: B21 p.178, 185; B61 p.391, 394-395, 410-411; B56 p.176; B45 p.36
- **Say whether output is personal or aggregate, and from which data.** Distinguish made for you from what others did, name the source (calendar, purchases, location), and reveal the conclusions the system has inferred about the person, not only the raw inputs. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.141-143; B56 p.127; B82 p.145-147
- **Prefer exploration before submission to explanation after rejection.** Let people adjust the inputs and see the result update live (a loan panel with sliders, a score simulator) rather than refusing them and explaining later. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.182-184; B56 p.176
- **Do not trust the model to explain itself.** Reasoning traces can omit real influences in up to a third of cases, and model claims about its own design are unreliable, so product copy and explanations must come from the system, not the model's self-report. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.401; B43 p.95
- **Label ranking as curation and make it inspectable.** In one survey 62 percent of users were unaware that their feed was being filtered, and ranking shifted mood and voting preference, so disclose it and let people see why an item appears. Confidence: medium. Sources: B56 p.153-154
- **State the baseline an explanation is measured against.** An attribution value means nothing without its comparison point, so phrase it as compared with a typical case. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.396-400
- **Use example-based explanations and evidence overlays.** Showing similar training examples is often clearer than numeric attributions, and expert users gain confidence from region or pixel overlays on images. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.406-413
- **Match explanation depth to the audience.** Affected users get short plain explanations; regulators and internal teams can take detail. Confidence: low. Sources: B51 p.124-126

## Grounding and output

- **Cite only what was actually retrieved, at claim level when people will act on it.** Models invent sources and cases; legal, medical and financial outputs need claim-level citations, side-by-side comparison or human review, and showing disagreement between sources can raise trust. Confidence: high. Sources: B16 p.391-395; B82 p.143; B43 p.96, 128-135; B75 p.465-469
- **Allow editing one piece of a result without regenerating all of it.** Selection-based edits (inpainting, shorten or expand a passage) regenerate only that part, and a version store with undo lets people explore without losing a good result. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.129-137; B82 p.152-154
- **Declare the lens behind an answer.** Expose model and version, agent or tool, locale, mode and assumed context behind a why-this affordance, and keep a badge on the active model when routing is automatic. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.69, 145-147
- **Show what context the model is reading.** Label the implicit input next to the prompt (selected text, current document) or dim what is out of scope so users can predict and correct it. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.83
- **Structure generated text for scanning.** Request headings, brief paragraphs, emphasized next steps and a closing list of sources, and for an explicit not-available rather than invented data. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.137-139
- **Put forward actions around the output.** Offer save, export, send, book and cite next to the result, mark which actions are final versus draft, and flag links that leave the product. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.148-150
- **Show progressive previews during long generations.** Watching an image form lets people judge the large features before the final render. Confidence: low. Sources: B75 p.328

## Input and prompting

- **Pair one-click presets with free-text input.** People tend to type brief imperative requests; preset buttons show what can be asked, though a crowd of them swamps the user and a handful suggests nothing else is possible, and implicit context plus direct manipulation should spare typing. Confidence: medium. Sources: B82 p.79-80, 91-93
- **Allow formatting in the prompt field.** Lists, quotes and code blocks separate context from instruction; formatted input raised one model's legal reasoning accuracy by nearly 20 points. Confidence: medium. Sources: B82 p.94-96
- **Nudge on overlong prompts instead of hard caps.** Models lose details buried mid-prompt despite large context windows; ambient complexity cues beat character limits that break flow. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.90-91
- **Name model choices by outcome and offer an escape from heavy modes.** Describe options by what they are good for, and give a one-click quick-answer override when deep reasoning or a tool turns on automatically. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.68-71
- **Disclose cost and quota before spending.** Show per-request cost and remaining allowance up front, warn when quality drops, and keep in-progress work when credits run out. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.73
- **Give generative image tools explicit control fields.** Provide a separate negative-prompt field (models ignore not and without), seed lock for one-word variations, and strength sliders for reference image versus text; short concrete prompts of about three to seven words with the key subject first work best. Confidence: low. Sources: B75 p.245-247, 302-305, 330, 336, 545-547
- **Plan discovery around who starts the interaction.** Features the user must invoke need clear names and visible handles; features the system offers unprompted need good timing, enough certainty and an easy way to decline. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.58

## Agents and delegation

- **Design agent use as management: mission, plan, monitor, interrupt, review.** Delegation runs asynchronously, so every stage needs its own surface, and accept or reject on findings steers the next round. Confidence: high. Sources: B16 p.244-245; B12 p.330-337; B82 p.176
- **Stop at once on request; pause keeps context, rollback never destroys.** Provide stop, pause and redirect, show done, in progress and remaining on pause, and restore checkpoints as a new branch with provisional outputs labeled. Confidence: high. Sources: B16 p.257-258; B82 p.190-191, 195; B12 p.330-337; B21 p.59-60
- **Confirm consequential actions specifically, and once.** Before money moves, messages go out, items are removed or private data is touched, spell out what will happen, to what, and the result, with proceed and cancel; confirm at the decisive commit rather than every step to avoid blind approval, and act at once only when a wrong guess is cheap. Confidence: high. Sources: B16 p.276-278; B12 p.182-183; B66 p.22-23; B60 p.194-195
- **Hand off to a person with the full situation.** Escalate sensitive cases such as fraud, tell the user what happened, the next step and how to take it, and check before transferring so a session is not lost by accident. Confidence: high. Sources: B51 p.165-166, 273; B11 p.91-92, 173; B60 p.194-195
- **Let AI helpers appear on intent, act briefly and leave.** Trigger on a mention, command or schedule, wait for natural pauses, batch updates, offer snooze or mute, never interrupt critical entry, and give coaching after the effort and on request. Confidence: high. Sources: B16 p.188-195, 331-332; B66 p.137-139; B21 p.116
- **Show the plan before acting, and require review only when stakes are high.** Present the interpreted plan as an editable contract for costly or irreversible work; a task under a minute needs about three steps, around 15 steps should fold into phases, and two or three focused clarifying questions precede heavy tasks. Confidence: medium. Sources: B82 p.176, 184-185, 192-193; B16 p.252-253
- **Tier agent permissions by risk with one consistent request pattern.** Read-only actions run quietly, edits and commands need approval with a scoped remember choice (session, workspace, always), and all approval requests share one location, symbol and phrasing. Confidence: medium. Sources: B82 p.174, 190-191; B16 p.276-278
- **Report agent progress in layers and keep the log.** Give a summary with an expandable activity log, opt-in notifications and a full record after completion; when steps are unknown, show the attempt number or elapsed time and escalate with what was tried. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.254-259; B82 p.100-103, 181-182, 187
- **Keep an audit trail of actions and versions.** Record each action with the model, code and data versions behind it, so people can review past runs and failures can be traced. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.90, 164-165; B16 p.256, 259
- **Give a focused agent a small toolset.** About three to six tools is the sweet spot; reliability drops past a dozen. Confidence: low. Sources: B16 p.275
- **Prefer intelligence inline where the work happens over a bolted-on chat panel.** A side chat adds a mode, breaks flow and takes space; embed help in context when possible. Confidence: low. Sources: B16 p.321-325
- **Have the model send structured instructions to the interface.** Return intent, component and data for the client to render instead of prose aimed at the user. Confidence: low. Sources: B16 p.94-96, 201-203

## Memory, data and change

- **Announce model changes and let users opt in.** Retraining can lift average accuracy while breaking cases people relied on; keep the old behavior available, and tell users the tool changes often. Confidence: high. Sources: B61 p.372-373, 378; B75 p.71-72; B66 p.135-136
- **Show what is remembered and let people edit, delete or reset it.** State what is retained and the retention period, provide an off-the-record mode, allow reset of a stale profile and opt-out with a manual path, and make opting back in easy. Confidence: medium. Sources: B16 p.397-398; B51 p.169-172, 251
- **For consequential generated reports, build only from items a person selected.** Automatic selection suits low-stakes summaries; legally important output should be built from items a person chose. Confidence: low. Sources: B12 p.163-168

## Strategy and evaluation

- **Evaluate AI features in real use, not on benchmarks.** Track adoption, frequency, abandonment, satisfaction, groundedness and trust; benchmark scores and vendor claims (one measured 63 percent against a claimed 76 to 83) do not predict usefulness. Confidence: high. Sources: B11 p.75-76; B43 p.108, 205-207, 216; B66 p.20-21
- **Test classifiers on diverse people before release.** Run representative test sets and deliberately probe a generator for bias (about 100 outputs per prompt) before users see it. Confidence: high. Sources: B56 p.129; B45 p.19, 21; B75 p.262
- **Start from the problem, not from AI.** Teams that start with the technology solve small or imaginary problems or answer the question their data allows; skip AI where habits, predictability, full transparency, sparse data or high error cost dominate, or users say they do not want it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B51 p.82-85; B12 p.37-38
- **Route ambiguous moderation to people and allow fast appeal.** Context-blind classifiers flag harmless content; offer more than one contestation channel. Confidence: medium. Sources: B43 p.167-168; B45 p.36
- **Do not ship emotion detection from faces.** Evidence does not support reading inner states from facial movement, least of all in hiring, education or policing. Confidence: medium. Sources: B65 p.161, 183-187
- **Design for context of use and priority of safety.** Account for the setting, the previous turn and the identity of the speaker; in vehicles safety outranks information, which outranks entertainment. Confidence: low. Sources: B66 p.45, 71-77
- **Plan a holdout before acting on predictions.** An intervention (a discount to a predicted abandoner) hides whether the prediction was right. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.277
- **Publish scoring criteria to prevent gaming.** Hidden criteria push people toward tricks; visible ones steer effort to real quality. Confidence: low. Sources: B43 p.49-50
- **Leave room for people to diverge from the forecast.** Predictive features should not lock a person's identity or future into what the model expects. Confidence: low. Sources: B45 p.33-34
- **Depict AI features by what they do, not glowing-brain imagery.** Mystifying visuals hide what the system relies on. Confidence: low. Sources: B65 p.57, 221-222
- **Get a qualified review before shipping machine-written code.** Such code tended to be needlessly complex and to skip stated constraints; reusing a proven library snippet is often quicker. Confidence: low. Sources: B75 p.487, 495-498

## Conversational surfaces

- **Display your interpretation so the user can repair it.** Restate the understood request; confirm a paraphrase when intent confidence sits between about 0.2 and 0.8 and treat lower scores as not understood. Confidence: medium. Sources: B60 p.43, 111-112, 143-144; B12 p.182-183
- **Fit the assistant's mood to how the person feels.** An upbeat voice speaking to unhappy drivers roughly doubled accidents; in sensitive domains neutral beats mismatched. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.100, 126
- **Define one consistent, broad persona.** Users assign a personality anyway; write the traits first and keep them broad enough to be trusted in serious domains later. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.93-97, 124; B73 p.72
- **Show recognized speech for search, hide it in conversation.** Exact words matter for queries; in chat, visible misrecognitions erode trust even when the reply is right. Confidence: medium. Sources: B41 p.166-168
