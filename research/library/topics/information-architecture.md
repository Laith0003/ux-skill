## Organizing principles

- **Organize by the user's tasks and mental model, never by the org chart or the database.** Visitors do not know which department owns their question; structures that mirror internal units or tables force people to bounce between screens, and one task-based redesign cut processing time to about a third. Derive top-level categories from grouped user goals in their own vocabulary. Confidence: high. Sources: B93 p.124; B20 p.158-160, 649, 850; B64 p.323-326; B35 p.199-200, 358-359; B40 p.23
- **Favor broad and shallow hierarchies, grouped on the page.** People give up after two or three levels; breadth is limited by visual scanning, not by short-term memory, so the 7 plus or minus 2 rule is not a valid menu limit; put many links in labeled clusters (a well-tested main page held about 85 links in about 10 groups) and reserve 3 to 4 levels for very large settings sets. Do not inflate the top level just to shorten click paths. Confidence: high. Sources: B93 p.141-143; B86 p.111; B87 p.49-50
- **Design every page for visitors who land deep.** At least half of visitors enter through a page other than the home page, so each page needs identity, local context, a short introduction to the product and a next step. Confidence: high. Sources: B93 p.108, 202; B07 p.103, 138; B33 p.52
- **Offer exact schemes for known-item seekers and ambiguous schemes for explorers.** Alphabetical, chronological and geographic orders serve people who know the name; topic, task and audience groupings serve browsing; provide both where possible, choosing among the LATCH methods (location, alphabet, time, category, hierarchy) deliberately, and let people re-sort without changing the result set. Confidence: high. Sources: B93 p.127-131, 152; B86 p.52-53; B11 p.81-82
- **Cross-list items that genuinely fit several categories, but sparingly.** Different audiences look for the same content in different sections, so key items need entry points in each place they are sought; when cross-listing becomes common the categories lose their meaning. Confidence: high. Sources: B93 p.140, 324; B74 p.186; B23 p.387, 389
- **Place functions by frequency of use and by risk.** Keep tools used many times a day in immediate reach, once-a-day tools a click or two away, occasional ones two or three clicks deep, and push irreversible or view-changing functions deeper; simplify by prioritizing, removing, relocating and hiding, in that order. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.270-273; B06 p.70-71
- **Keep labels and categories identical across channels, and change them slowly.** Layout can differ between desktop, app and phone support, but the semantic structure must not, so people carry one mental model; visual design can be refreshed freely while structure changes disorient returning users. Give each kind of content the same treatment wherever it appears. Confidence: medium. Sources: B93 p.40, 81, 90; B92 p.116
- **Model content as independent facets.** Describing items by type, region, price, audience and similar facets supports filtering, sorting and several navigation designs from one foundation. Confidence: medium. Sources: B93 p.325-329; B04 p.154-155
- **Make every filter value set cover the whole inventory.** Items missing an attribute vanish once any filter is applied (in one store 7 of 23 items disappeared); add an Other value that catches empty attributes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B04 p.154-155
- **Keep hybrid schemes shallow and show each scheme in its own block.** Mixing topic, task and audience inside one deep menu prevents a coherent mental model; mixing is tolerable only at the top level. Confidence: low. Sources: B93 p.136-137
- **Use a metaphor only when it is familiar and sets correct expectations.** An unknown metaphor, or one that implies behavior the product lacks, creates confusion. Confidence: low. Sources: B93 p.135-136, 390-391
- **Put a list's highest-value items first or last.** Because of primacy and recency, items in the middle are recalled least. Confidence: low. Sources: B44 p.124; B11 p.81-82

## Vocabulary and metadata

- **Route every synonym to a single preferred term linked to its parent, child and sibling terms.** Collect the words users actually use, let a controlled vocabulary route their terms to yours, and pair user tags (which capture emerging terms but are messy) with an expert taxonomy. Confidence: high. Sources: B11 p.35, 85-89; B35 p.180-181; B93 p.308
- **Name sections with the terms peers and users already use.** Count labels across about 17 peer sites; a feature or label on half or more of them is the baseline, and users prefer plain friendly words over corporate ones. Confidence: medium. Sources: B58 p.347, 353, 361, 619
- **Plan the metadata track in parallel with the visible interface.** A simple-looking search field rests on schema, indexing, vocabulary and ranking work, which has to begin at the same time as the interface. Confidence: low. Sources: B11 p.51-52

## Card sorting and validation

- **Sequence the research: open card sort, then closed sort, then tree test.** Open sorting surfaces the groupings and labels people produce themselves, closed sorting tests a candidate structure, and tree tests or a clickable low-fidelity structure validate paths with find-it tasks; do this before wireframing navigation, since IA is hard to change later. Confidence: high. Sources: B11 p.64, 67-68; B85 p.63-65, 148-149; B06 p.119-120; B35 p.180-181
- **Size card sorts to avoid fatigue.** Use about 30 to 50 cards (about 52 at most, never 100 or more, where sorters start dumping cards into piles), sessions of 30 to 60 minutes, at least six participants run one at a time (up to about 15), shuffled decks and a no-home pile. Confidence: high. Sources: B35 p.290-292; B03 p.219-220; B58 p.173, 183-184, 571-573; B06 p.119-120; B23 p.298, 385, 387
- **Read sort results with judgment.** Discard haphazard sorts that put everything in one or two piles, split clusters that are too large (25 items is too many for one group), treat long branches as weak agreement, label each group with the name the majority of sorters used, and treat a vague catch-all group as a sign the main categories are wrong. Confidence: high. Sources: B58 p.176-182; B23 p.387, 389; B03 p.224
- **Write cards at one consistent level of detail and without shared words.** Never mix items with their parent category; reword labels that share a word because sorters group by the word, avoid brand names, keep real domain terms with a short description, and merge variants into one card. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.220, 224; B58 p.175, 573
- **Run sorting studies in each target culture.** Groups formed by function in one culture and by theme in another, and each made the fewest errors with its own scheme. Confidence: medium. Sources: B08 p.89-90
- **Test labels and calls to action before visual design.** Brand-led or witty labels often break the user's model; validate terminology with tasks in a clickable structure, because a site map cannot be user-tested and stakeholders struggle to read one. Confidence: low. Sources: B85 p.55, 65, 87-88; B74 p.28-29, 41
- **Have users vote on their top tasks.** Present a shuffled list of tasks in plain words, have each participant choose five and tally the votes to see the few tasks that matter most. Confidence: low. Sources: B78 p.28-29
- **Cluster large feature sets with affinity diagramming.** Put each feature on a note and group them physically to find the categories for menus with hundreds of items. Confidence: low. Sources: B57 p.85-86

## Flows and structure artifacts

- **Diagram every flow longer than one or two steps, including its failure branches, before drawing screens.** Even a login splits into wrong password, unknown account, forgotten password and no account; the diagram exposes every state that needs a design and shows how much there is to build, and a one-page flowchart replaced a prose spec with about 80 percent less text. Confidence: high. Sources: B27 p.144-147; B38 p.24, 27-28; B05 p.89, 95
- **Draw wireflows with real screens and honest path styling.** Start arrows at the triggering control, draw pages outside the scope as empty boxes, and mark a permitted but unwanted path, for example skipping onboarding, with a dashed line. Confidence: low. Sources: B62 p.133-134; B38 p.26
- **Map the current flow before redesigning it.** Walk the existing product step by step and pin questions, concerns and ideas under each step. Confidence: low. Sources: B38 p.94-96
- **Back every site map with page tables and stable IDs.** Give each content item an outline ID (1.0, 1.1, 1.1.1) that links audit rows to recommendations, and record each page's objective, key messages, sources, owner and upkeep. Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.75-78, 142-145
- **Keep the template inventory small.** Each extra template raises design, build and upkeep cost and forces people to learn another layout; plan levels from home (level 0) to article or product (level 4 or 5). Confidence: low. Sources: B35 p.203-204, 209-210

## Evolving a live structure

- **Safeguard the small set of pages that draw nearly all visits.** On a site of over 5,000 pages, about 5 percent were viewed regularly and about 20 pages took most hits, in line with an 80/20 pattern; redesign in phases, keep those pages easy to reach and redirect old URLs. Confidence: medium. Sources: B58 p.99, 557; B11 p.103
- **Use one search box that covers everything people want.** Several search boxes that each query different data, combined with audience-split navigation, confused every one of 45 test users. Confidence: medium. Sources: B06 p.170-172
- **Let real usage paths shape cross-links.** When analytics show people reading a sequence of pages, add next and previous links; when an item can live in only one category, put it where traffic shows people look. Confidence: low. Sources: B58 p.359-364
- **Answer the core questions on the main page.** It should say where people are, how to search and browse, what the site provides, what has changed recently, how to contact a person and how to reach their account. Confidence: low. Sources: B93 p.105

## Culture, layering and new media

- **Tune information density and flow order to the culture.** High-context, relationship-oriented groups tolerate and prefer more information units and parallel tasks, holistic readers scan non-linearly while analytic readers need simple layouts with explicit labels, and booking flows differed by market (browse first, quick search first, or domestic versus abroad first); make density configurable and design market flows early. Confidence: medium. Sources: B49 p.191, 198-199, 215; B08 p.351, 361-367
- **Make spatial environments readable through clear routes, boundaries, zones, junctions and landmarks.** Make those landmarks stand out at decision points, and in AR spread tools around the user with shallow nesting instead of deep menus. Confidence: medium. Sources: B83 p.542-543; B48 p.171-172
- **Layer content for a glance, a second look and a deep read.** Split dense material into distinct digestible parts and never let a novel format obscure the information. Confidence: low. Sources: B84 p.127-130, 149, 159-161
- **Do not ship chat as the whole information architecture of an AI product.** People still need starting points, alerts, settings, account and history; a session list acting as a hub lets them leave and resume multi-stage work. Confidence: low. Sources: B12 p.141-148, 241-243
- **Format related-item groups distinctly from results.** Give each group a simple heading, a link to view everything and a sideways strip of about 8 to 10 items that looks different from the vertical result list. Confidence: low. Sources: B04 p.215-223
