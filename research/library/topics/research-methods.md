## Behavior over self-report

- **Watch what people do in their real setting instead of trusting what they say they do.** Self-reports drift from actual behavior, so interview where the work happens, watch for workarounds, and check stated habits against logs or observation. Confidence: high. Sources: B26 p.73, 81-85; B20 p.125-128, 137-138; B79 p.223-224, 229-230; B02 p.310-311; B30 p.19-21; B03 p.148-149; B58 p.243, 245-247; B66 p.99-100, 141; B72 p.48, 228; B27 p.38-40; B67 p.45-46; B40 p.36
- **Recreate the real environment when you test.** Light, noise, gloves, interruptions, seat position and time pressure decide whether a design works; in realistic distracting settings people were on task only about 18 seconds of every minute, and roughly one in five usability problems is specific to the location. Confidence: high. Sources: B20 p.410-411, 560-561; B03 p.231-232, 249-250; B06 p.55, 93-94; B28 p.134, 136; B57 p.102-103; B29 p.63-66; B49 p.153; B17 p.30
- **Pair a quantitative "what" with a qualitative "why", and use at least three angles: see, say and do.** Analytics, logs and A/B tests show where people drop out; observation and interviews explain why; no single method covers its own blind spots. Confidence: high. Sources: B30 p.21-22; B02 p.310-311; B06 p.79, 87; B27 p.82, 88, 204; B11 p.61, 66; B50 p.40-43, 171; B17 p.31-32; B67 p.38-41; B29 p.56-57, 63-66; B71 p.26, 46
- **Ask about the last concrete time, never about the future.** Prompts for the most recent real episode, a walk through yesterday or a live demonstration predict behavior; questions about intent, likelihood or hypothetical usefulness produce guesses. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.47, 138-141, 181-185; B03 p.148-149, 352; B64 p.140-144; B23 p.310-311; B27 p.41, 44-45; B44 p.276-279
- **Treat users as reporters of problems, not designers of solutions.** Do not ask participants to invent features or pitch your idea to them; when they propose a fix, ask what problem it would solve, and use the request as a pointer to an underlying need. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.73, 81-85; B20 p.148; B89 p.84-85; B11 p.45-46, 48; B40 p.36; B27 p.41, 44-45; B57 p.35-37
- **Rank evidence by strength before acting on it.** Behavioral sources (observation in the field, task-based testing, analytics, controlled experiments) weigh most; diaries, card sorts and expert reviews sit in the middle; group discussion, stated preference and senior opinion weigh least. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.198-202; B28 p.55-56; B03 p.160-161
- **Capture hard-to-recall behavior in the moment.** For events people misremember weeks later, use contextual observation, diaries, experience sampling or analytics rather than retrospective interviews or surveys. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.32-33; B02 p.340; B03 p.271-272; B72 p.210
- **Use preference questions only to weigh trade-offs, not to explain choices.** Asking which of two costs matters more reveals priorities experts cannot guess, but people readily make up explanations for choices that were driven by things they never noticed, so never treat stated reasons as causes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.283-284, 291-294; B30 p.19-21
- **Make at least one on-site visit even when experts are available.** A single field session can expose a critical cue that subject-matter experts never mention; a handful of users observed over two days can be enough context for a small internal tool. Confidence: medium. Sources: B12 p.38-40; B79 p.223-224; B58 p.43
- **Do not replace research participants with synthetic users or AI personas.** Simulated users yield products tuned for simulations; AI can help summarize transcripts and open answers but misreads sarcasm and context, and automated AI audits have shown high error rates. Confidence: medium. Sources: B12 p.377-386

## Framing and planning

- **Start from the decision the research must inform, then write precise, observable questions.** A vague goal such as checking general usability biases a study toward finding nothing; a concrete question, such as whether people can tell which elements respond to a click, drives tasks, measures and the report. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.280-281, 290; B28 p.107-109; B30 p.24, 29, 38; B17 p.11
- **Keep the question set small and ranked.** Rate every candidate question for importance and for severity on a five-point scale each, take the product, and start with the top scores; hold a study to roughly six major and twelve minor questions, with the main one written as one real question. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.68-69; B30 p.24, 29
- **Never run research to justify a position.** Only ask questions whose answers can change something still open; most experimenter effects lean toward the researcher's own hypothesis. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.71; B30 p.167
- **Write the business objective and the learning objective separately.** A conversion target is not a research goal; also surface the unstated objectives stakeholders will judge the work against. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.11
- **Write team assumptions down as hypotheses and split facts from guesses.** Collect everyone's hunches at kickoff on separate colors for facts and assumptions, let anyone challenge a fact back into an assumption, and refine or reject them session by session (3 to 10 minutes of solo writing, 20 to 30 minutes in total). Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.63-64; B17 p.11-15; B50 p.37-38; B01 p.16
- **Sequence broad research before narrow research.** Use surveys or field visits to learn whom to study and which problems matter, then usability tests to evaluate solutions; field visits test whether a problem exists, usability tests test whether you solved it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.73; B30 p.35-37; B17 p.28; B57 p.101-102
- **Run research as a continuous loop, with a fixed set of baseline questions.** Evidence should keep building through discovery, design and delivery rather than stopping after one study; repeating the same few baseline questions in every small session reveals long-term shifts. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.128; B29 p.54; B72 p.25
- **Write a research plan before or while you build.** Include background, the assumption and goal, hypotheses, target audience, methods, tasks, timeline, budget and expected outcomes, and revise it every week or two; build only the prototype screens the tasks need. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.109, 266-267, 270-271; B67 p.36-38; B03 p.80; B29 p.55
- **Plan only as much data as you can analyze.** Twenty interviews with one day of analysis, or twenty hours of video with two weeks to report, is a broken plan; set coding and synthesis time before fieldwork. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.206; B50 p.38-39, 61-62
- **Budget analysis effort from known ratios.** Transcribing takes 2 to 4 hours per recorded hour and yields about 30 pages; automatic transcription is about 80 to 90 percent accurate; two people need about two weeks for 8 to 10 interviews; plan about 3 hours to run and 2 to analyze each usability participant and 2 to 4 weeks for a comprehensive analysis. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.81-82, 442, 464-466; B11 p.77; B64 p.148; B02 p.308; B17 p.128-129, 136; B28 p.283
- **Expect typical method durations when scheduling.** Field visits and focus groups take about 2 to 4 weeks end to end, usability tests 1 to 3, surveys 2 to 6, diary studies 4 to 6, and a persona effort 2 to 5 days spread over two weeks; a quick contextual study with 5 to 8 users fits in 1 to 2 weeks. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.76-77, 158; B20 p.661, 671; B79 p.237
- **Pilot every instrument before the real study.** Do a cheap read-through with a few people, then a pilot session; pilot questionnaires and screeners on 3 or 4 people and leave extra time after the first real session to fix the guide. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.74-75; B28 p.111, 167-169; B69 p.180; B06 p.101-103; B17 p.51-52, 58-59
- **Scale the research to the value of the decision.** Small wording changes warrant a quick check, while a bet that shapes the company warrants deep fieldwork; link each study to a business measure. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.30-31
- **Decide up front whether you need insight, evidence or ideas.** Evidence persuades skeptics, insight steers the team, and co-design or group methods produce ideas that another method must check. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.28-29, 36-38
- **Use one-to-one sessions and analytics as the core kit, but do not stop at interviews.** Add natural-use observation, scripted tests, card sorts and participatory methods when interviews cannot reach the answer; methods either ask, observe or invite participation. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.38; B29 p.59-60; B69 p.154
- **Stop when findings repeat and the hypotheses are settled.** Signals of enough: themes recur, gaps are filled, every archetype is covered, or the next round would cost more than it returns. Confidence: low. Sources: B29 p.67-68; B17 p.31-32
- **Break fuzzy constructs into measurable parts before designing a study.** For example, treat usability as three measurable parts per ISO 9241-11 (effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction), and do the same for any vague quality goal. Confidence: low. Sources: B30 p.72-73
- **Question the brief to find the right problem first.** Treat the request as a suggestion, ask why repeatedly (Five Whys), and diverge then converge on the problem before the solution; check whether the need is procedural, political or educational before writing code. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.170, 218-221; B44 p.279-280; B57 p.29-30
- **Blame the situation, not the user.** When people keep making the same error, observe the workflow and fix the system rather than retraining them. Confidence: low. Sources: B57 p.42-43
- **Answer the basic questions before any layout.** Settle the audience, their key tasks, the business aim, the technical limits and the reason to choose this over alternatives; a concept redesign that skips this discovery is decoration. Confidence: low. Sources: B38 p.20; B95 p.144-146, 224; B29 p.180

## Existing data and stakeholders

- **Mine what already exists before new research.** Sample tickets, call records, app reviews, social mentions, earlier test reports and lessons from similar past projects (a month of feedback usually takes under a day to read), and log each item as evidence, interpretation and implication; remember that proxies such as support staff over-represent struggling users. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.130-133; B67 p.35-36; B50 p.37-38, 44-45; B71 p.26; B17 p.14
- **Interview stakeholders and frontline staff first, and treat what you hear as assumptions.** Talk to about half a dozen people, one or two per function, for 15 to 60 minutes each; ask where the current picture is wrong, listen more than you talk, let pauses run, pose at least one deliberately basic question, and confirm findings in writing. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.134-135, 151; B81 p.74-78, 238; B67 p.34-35; B55 p.90-91; B23 p.128, 131; B96 p.42; B49 p.244
- **Treat complaints and bug reports as pointers, not specifications.** Feedback skews negative and comes from a vocal minority; call the reporter to learn what they were trying to do, because the real need is usually bigger than the bug; review the top complaints monthly. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.487-488; B57 p.28-29; B34 p.134; B67 p.35-36
- **Hear the customer directly.** Second-hand feedback passed along through sales gets distorted, and an occasional satisfaction number never explains causes; spend about an hour a week listening to support calls and put decision makers in sessions to listen without defending. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.58; B72 p.260, 267
- **Mine search logs for demand and vocabulary.** The most frequent searches, searches that return nothing and questions sent to support reveal demand and the terms people actually use; a few dozen top queries can cover a fifth of all searches, and an hour a month on the top ten queries pays off. Confidence: medium. Sources: B93 p.192-193, 358-360; B04 p.48, 50
- **Set the success target before reading analytics.** Traffic counts say little about design quality, a heavily visited page may be a pass-through to one link, and numbers from before a redesign are history, not a baseline. Confidence: low. Sources: B44 p.261-262; B67 p.38-41
- **Study why churned users left.** Look at what went wrong for them (checkout trouble, late delivery, bad reviews) instead of profiling who stays. Confidence: low. Sources: B56 p.84-85
- **Sample large content inventories with intent.** Audit everything under about 5,000 items; above that, weight the sample by objectives, audiences, traffic, owners and depth (for example 5,000 of 10,000 items, about 10,000 to 16,000 for a million). Confidence: low. Sources: B55 p.78-80
- **Sketch the customer value chain before choosing what to study.** List the actors, put the primary actor and the provider in the middle and show how value flows between them; it takes minutes and prevents mapping the wrong relationship. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.108-109
- **Draft the experience with stakeholders as a hypothesis.** A rough draft on the wall gets agreement on the model and yields the questions research must answer; park solution ideas and stay on the model. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.137
- **Inventory touchpoints by walking the flow as a customer.** Note every point of contact, whether on screen, on paper or with staff, then try the less common paths too, such as a trial account versus a paid team account. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.138
- **Map the current journey and its pain points before prototyping.** Use an as-is map and an empathy map (think, feel, say, do), then rank pain points to decide what to prototype. Confidence: low. Sources: B85 p.46-48
- **List the drivers that move the audience and the barriers that block success.** Obstacles may come from regulation, technical standards or the market, and neglecting a powerful motivator can turn into an obstacle of its own. Confidence: low. Sources: B71 p.34
- **Bound competitive reviews.** Cover at most 5 direct and 5 to 10 indirect competitors, spend about one day (never more than two) on a review of 4 or 5 products, rely on published sources if under a week, and watch 4 or 5 people use rival products before building your own. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.93-94, 98; B23 p.197-198, 202; B27 p.53-55, 132

## Sampling

- **Choose representative participants over large numbers.** A small sample drawn from the right population beats a huge sample drawn from the wrong one; stratify when subgroups differ, and a well-run study with five users beats a poor one with a thousand. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.27-29, 90; B30 p.62; B27 p.37, 39-40
- **Size qualitative studies per user type, not per study.** Plan about 4 to 6 participants per role or segment (enterprise) and 8 to 12 per type (consumer), 8 when types are unknown, around 20 in a first discovery round; review after about 10 depth interviews. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.75, 79-80; B30 p.138; B64 p.142-143; B03 p.237-238; B27 p.40-41, 73-74; B69 p.178; B29 p.68
- **Derive quantitative sample sizes from variance, precision, confidence and power.** Start with n = s^2 x (z_alpha + z_beta)^2 / d^2 and iterate using t; without prior data, estimate the standard deviation as range / 6. Under identical criteria an estimate needed about 13 users, a benchmark 20, a within-subjects comparison 27 and a between-subjects comparison 102. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.124-127, 131, 135-136, 154-157
- **Shrink variance to shrink the sample.** Clear instructions, practice trials, simpler tasks, log transforms of times and within-subjects designs all cut the required n; one comparison needed 15 users within-subjects versus 52 between. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.133-134, 137-138
- **Put at least 4 people in every comparison cell.** A 2 x 2 design therefore needs 16; covering six binary factors with one person per cell already needs 64, so limit the factors. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.114-115, 163; B30 p.101
- **Include the least competent plausible user in every sample.** When the least skilled realistic participant gets through, nearly everyone will; samples supplied by managers or agencies skew toward star performers and make the product look better than it is. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.184-186; B69 p.172
- **For discovery, seek extreme, boundary and lead users; for evaluation, mix boundary and mainstream users.** People with the biggest unmet problem, or most unlike the obvious user, reveal the edges of the experience; routine satisfied customers ask for the same thing cheaper. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.172, 213; B72 p.62, 302, 318; B30 p.53, 103
- **Prefer a diverse mix to a big count, and vary the channels.** Role and experience diversity matters more than n, and every recruitment channel biases the sample (email misses offline older users, so add paper or in person). Confidence: medium. Sources: B57 p.97; B69 p.176; B83 p.665, 681-682
- **Raise problem discovery with varied tasks and several observers.** Use multiple skilled observers, mix simple and complex tasks, include less-skilled users, and prefer more evaluators with less time each over a few with a lot. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.174
- **Counterbalance order in within-subjects designs.** If every participant tries all tasks or variants, vary the sequence across participants so practice effects do not reward whichever item appears later. Confidence: medium. Sources: B28 p.113-115; B58 p.149
- **Test against at least one alternative.** Pit the design against what it replaces, another concept or a rival; a single-design test can refine it but cannot show whether a different idea would win. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.292; B27 p.53-55; B58 p.149
- **Change one variable at a time in experiments.** Changing several settings at once hides which one mattered. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.230
- **Do not read eye-tracking heat maps from small samples.** Quantitative heat maps need at least about 20 participants; qualitative gaze review with think-aloud can work with 10. Confidence: low. Sources: B04 p.137

## Recruiting and screening

- **Recruit on behavior first, attitudes second, demographics last.** The people who do the activity matter; buyers and approvers are often not users, so test users and survey buyers separately. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.113-114; B30 p.94-96, 177; B17 p.50; B28 p.167-169
- **Write screeners that disqualify early and never reveal the target answer.** Put exclusion questions first, ask for exact counts within a fixed recent window such as the last month, hide the target among options, do not name the client, ask sensitive data in bands, add one open question to judge articulateness, and include an invented brand to catch professional participants. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.123-124, 129; B17 p.50-51; B28 p.167-169; B30 p.94-96
- **Keep screeners short for the channel.** Aim for 10 to 15 questions and no more than about 20 for interview or test recruits, under 5 if already prescreened, about 5 for guerrilla sessions; phone screeners take 5 to 10 minutes and online self-completion 2 to 3. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.123; B17 p.51-52
- **Limit recruitment to two primary variables.** A pair of key criteria can be crossed into a quota grid with loose cells of 1 to 3 people; anything else becomes one of at most seven secondary criteria or a preference. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.42-47
- **Screen out anyone who joined a study or test within the past six months.** Frequent participants learn to perform for researchers. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.122, 128; B17 p.48; B30 p.177
- **Over-recruit and confirm repeatedly.** Expect about a quarter of invitees not to show: book 20 to 25 percent extra or a paid floater (paid 2 to 4 times a normal slot), collect three availability slots, confirm the evening before, and keep a contact list about ten times the size of the next round. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.121, 136-137; B28 p.179, 186-187; B30 p.98; B17 p.58-59
- **Give recruiting enough lead time.** Allow at least two weeks for an agency (more for niche audiences, plus one to two weeks for participants with disabilities) and book sessions at least a week ahead, but not much more than two. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.131, 140, 292; B17 p.52; B28 p.187, 332
- **Always pay a fair incentive, and never tie it to performance.** Unpaid studies recruit slowly, skew to fans or angry customers and suffer more no-shows; pay everyone the same for attending and making an effort, pay on arrival, and keep payment separate from consent. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.56-58; B28 p.188-189, 197-198; B30 p.127-131
- **Use friends and family only as a dry run.** Avoid colleagues and people who know each other, draw from several unrelated sources, and use communities, intercepts and specialist recruiters to reach real users. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.271-272; B69 p.176; B27 p.37, 39-40; B29 p.181-182
- **Choose which user groups to study first by what they can teach and how easily you can reach them.** Place candidate groups on a two-by-two of learning value against reachability; begin with high-value easy groups, queue high-value hard ones, and drop the others. Confidence: low. Sources: B30 p.90-92

## Consent and ethics

- **Get informed consent item by item.** Walk through recording, who sees the data, how to withdraw and how long data is kept, on a form shorter than one page; record verbal consent item by item for remote or low-literacy participants; minors need guardian consent plus their own assent; keep consent separate from NDAs. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.60-61; B30 p.127-131; B03 p.144
- **Refer to participants by code, never by name.** Label people as P1, P2 and so on in findings and file names, and limit any use of recordings to what participants agreed to. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.189, 320-321
- **Make it clear the product is under test, not the person.** Keep small talk brief, match the participant's register, and stay on the agreed time. Confidence: low. Sources: B40 p.39-40

## Interviews and field visits

- **Ask neutral, open, single-topic questions.** Open with prompts that invite a story or a description, split anything joined by and/or, avoid yes/no and judgmental wording, ask whether someone uses a category before naming a product, and offer examples only after an unprompted answer. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.149-151; B40 p.39; B44 p.276-279; B96 p.73-74; B26 p.81-85; B64 p.140-144
- **Use the participant's own words and answer questions with questions.** Keep their terms even if technically wrong, and turn their questions back to them (asking what they expected) to expose their mental model, and mine transcripts for their nouns and verbs to name things. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.152; B33 p.89-90
- **Take the apprentice role.** Build rapport for about five minutes, then let the participant teach you their work while you watch, rephrasing the same question until the answer is usable. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.138-141; B64 p.140-144; B57 p.37-38
- **Use critical incidents and episodes when you cannot observe.** Ask for a time it went badly, what happened, how it felt and what ideal would have been; let the participant pick the most relevant episode, and when answers stay vague, ask about yesterday. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.144; B30 p.181-185
- **Run sessions with two people: one asks, one notes.** A moderator who also takes notes derails the conversation; never bring more than two researchers into a field session, and record when working alone. Confidence: medium. Sources: B40 p.38; B64 p.140-143; B30 p.138-139; B67 p.42-45; B58 p.270-271; B26 p.75, 80
- **Time-box interviews and leave buffers.** Keep desk interviews to about an hour with the participant talking most, in-context visits to 1.5 to 2 hours at most, the script readable aloud in under 10 minutes, about 30 minutes between sessions for notes, and no more than 2 or 3 field visits or 6 interviews a day. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.69, 75, 80; B40 p.39; B64 p.140, 142-143; B02 p.313, 452; B20 p.136; B58 p.242, 262
- **Let silence work.** After an answer, pause about five seconds, and give people five to ten seconds to think before you prompt; most natural conversational pauses are under a second, so longer silences invite elaboration. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.174, 177, 187; B55 p.90-91; B44 p.276-279; B60 p.214
- **Show the prototype last.** Confirm the person is in the audience and has the problem, and collect what they want before they see a design; requirements named after seeing a prototype are biased by it. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.121-122; B58 p.194, 575
- **Ask for typical moments when things go well and when they go wrong.** Questions about lived experience, not product features, draw out context-rich stories rather than lists of feature gripes. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.261-262
- **Infer goals from behavior instead of asking for them.** People cannot state their goals accurately, so reconstruct them from actions, indirect answers, non-verbal cues and the environment, and ask about goals before tasks. Confidence: low. Sources: B26 p.81-85; B01 p.11
- **Log the setting on every site visit.** Record noise, light, interruptions and the artifacts people use, and keep a paper backup for any recording device. Confidence: medium. Sources: B06 p.93-94; B03 p.231-232, 249-250
- **When shadowing, hold questions until the task ends.** Interrupting mid-task destroys the behavior you came to see. Confidence: low. Sources: B06 p.95-96
- **Separate observation from interpretation in your notes.** Mark interpretations with brackets, a symbol or a separate column, and keep each note to one concept in 1 to 3 sentences, so analysis does not quietly replay your assumptions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.253, 441; B20 p.174-175
- **Size field visits by audience factor.** Plan roughly four visits for each audience trait that matters (up to eight when time permits) and at least two or three per site; devote two thirds or more of each visit to watching, and send follow-ups within two days. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.237-238, 246-247; B06 p.93-94; B20 p.135
- **Keep guerrilla sessions to 5 to 15 minutes.** If nobody stops for 10 to 15 minutes, relocate. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.35, 54
- **Study real conversation recordings, failures included, before designing a conversational product.** Interviews reveal topics but not how people actually talk; transcript review sessions take 10 to 15 minutes per excerpt. Confidence: medium. Sources: B60 p.207-210, 214

## Focus groups and workshops

- **Use focus groups for perceptions, never for behavior.** Groups show stated desires and self-image, cannot show what people do and never generalize statistically; people who stress a value in a group often act against it in practice. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.160-161; B28 p.55-56; B58 p.243, 245-247; B30 p.198-202
- **Run focus groups at 6 to 8 people, never as a single group.** Use about 4 for depth, rarely run more than four groups, cover 3 to 5 topics of about 10 minutes, break after 90 minutes, allow at most two silent observers, and recruit people who match the target market. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.158, 164, 169, 178; B69 p.155, 177; B55 p.90; B46 p.134; B06 p.100; B38 p.90-91
- **Have people write or rate alone before the group talks.** Silent individual notes, ratings or sketches cut groupthink and the pull of dominant voices, especially when prioritizing features; debrief each session with silent sticky notes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.188-189; B96 p.73-74
- **Keep working sessions small.** About 5 to 7 people keeps strategy meetings candid; day-long design workshops of 8 or more proved inefficient; brand card sorts work with at most 6 stakeholders and 1 facilitator. Confidence: medium. Sources: B93 p.341, 385; B39 p.40; B44 p.309; B03 p.500
- **Set communication goals with a stakeholder adjective sort.** Have stakeholders spend 45 to 60 minutes placing roughly 150 descriptive words into current identity, aspired identity and rejected identity, then cluster and rank the aspired pile into 3 to 6 themes; a faster version overlaps brand offers with audience needs. Confidence: low. Sources: B39 p.38-46
- **Discover components by cutting up printed pages in teams.** Teams of 3 to 6 sort 15 to 25 printed pages each for 1 to 2 hours, half cutting and half classifying, with at least 15 minutes of discussion. Confidence: low. Sources: B62 p.180-183
- **Use performance methods for users far from the team.** Informance, forum theatre and live role play help when designing new categories or for people culturally or demographically distant, and surface stakes absent from data. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.140, 166; B45 p.82

## Surveys and questionnaires

- **Use surveys to size and validate, not to discover.** Preset answers bias what people say and long text boxes go unanswered; form hypotheses qualitatively, then survey a larger screened group; stated intentions in polls predict behavior poorly. Confidence: medium. Sources: B27 p.72-75; B67 p.41-42; B30 p.47; B38 p.91
- **Write neutral, answerable, single-idea items.** No leading wording, jargon, double negatives, two-part questions or questions that need calculation; keep options balanced, add an "other" option and a neutral midpoint, start easy, and use radio buttons for single choice. Confidence: medium. Sources: B23 p.239, 248-249; B69 p.180; B02 p.321-325; B03 p.149-151
- **Never let answer ranges overlap or leave gaps.** Use 15-19 and 20-24, not 15-20 and 20-25. Confidence: low. Sources: B02 p.321-322
- **Use 5 or 7 points for agreement and up to 11 for fine judgments.** Reliability levels off at about 7 steps with little gain past 11, and 7-point, 11-point and 0 to 100 scales showed no reliability difference; use 3 points only for yes / no / don't know. Confidence: high. Sources: B94 p.205; B02 p.324; B32 p.110-111
- **Keep item tone consistently positive.** Mixing positive and negative statements causes misreading and miscoding (about 11 percent of mixed-tone datasets) without reducing response bias. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.223-227
- **Measure frequency, not usefulness.** "How often do you use X" measures behavior; "X is useful" invites agreement; score top-box to counter acquiescence and define frequency bins explicitly. Confidence: low. Sources: B57 p.99-101; B06 p.138
- **Keep surveys short.** Target roughly ten items and five to ten minutes; on phones most respondents quit before the ten-minute mark, so break longer instruments into parts; never exceed about 45 minutes for a remote study and add an incentive past 20 to 25 minutes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.138; B66 p.79-80; B58 p.227, 506, 589, 665-666
- **Use open-ended questions sparingly.** They are costly to code and often skipped, so reserve them for genuine unknowns. Confidence: medium. Sources: B06 p.101-103; B27 p.72-75; B23 p.254
- **Screen out bots and inattentive respondents.** Bots can complete up to a quarter of some online surveys; add checks and inspect open answers. Confidence: medium. Sources: B66 p.79, 101
- **Plan response volume.** A 40 percent return rate is generally acceptable; aim for about 100 responses as a practical base and 50 to 100 per design when comparing variants; a cover letter signed by an authority figure lifts returns sharply. Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.325-326, 328; B58 p.587-589
- **Check instrument reliability.** Look for a reliability coefficient near 0.9 when stakes are high and 0.7 to 0.8 for exploratory work; a correlation with a criterion as low as 0.3 to 0.4 can still make a measure worth using. Confidence: medium. Sources: B94 p.204
- **Split long in-session questionnaires by phase.** For sessions over an hour give short questionnaires after each phase, and cap fill-in answers at about three to force priorities. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.231, 236
- **Measure core needs before and after launch.** Rate the same agreement statements tied to each core need at both points. Confidence: low. Sources: B25 p.91
- **Use reaction-word cards to probe desirability.** A deck of about 118 words, 60 percent positive, where people pick all that apply and narrow to five. Confidence: low. Sources: B06 p.99

## Diaries and longitudinal studies

- **Design diaries around a clear trigger and enough entries.** Make tasks relevant, low effort and indirect; get at least about 6 entries per person (a week for daily events, about two months for weekly ones); a typical study is 10 people x 7 days x 3 entries; recruit 2 extra, expect drop-off, and pay 60 to 100 percent of lab rates. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.263-265, 271-272, 285; B67 p.47-48; B17 p.37; B32 p.110-111
- **Prompt experience sampling at natural moments.** Asking at a routine action such as unlocking a phone raised response rate and accuracy; short expiry (about 5 minutes) and a tight compliance window keep reports fresh. Confidence: medium. Sources: B02 p.340, 597-598

## Card sorting and structure research

- **Use open card sorts to discover and closed sorts to validate.** Open sorts show how people group things; closed sorts test proposed categories and labels; with enough participants a shared grouping emerges. Confidence: medium. Sources: B93 p.365-368; B38 p.90
- **Size card sorts deliberately.** About 15 participants reach a .90 correlation with the full sample (.95 at 30, .98 at 60); 20 to 25 cards often suffice and about 100 is the ceiling; never present a five-person sort as quantitative, though 3 to 5 people free-listing still help. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.136-137; B93 p.190-191, 365, 368
- **Build mental model diagrams from many short stories.** Formal projects use 20 to 30 participants; a rapid version gathers 1 to 2 page stories a week ahead and summarizes about 100 of them in a few hours. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.319, 324
- **Prioritize by importance and unmet satisfaction, not by feature list.** Score outcomes as importance + (importance - satisfaction), and rank observed tasks rather than planned features; put effort into important tasks people often fail and leave tasks that already succeed alone. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.62-63; B58 p.127-128, 562-563
- **Frame needs as jobs within a circumstance.** Every job has functional, emotional and social sides, and the situation is the unit of analysis; a job map moves through define, locate, prepare and confirm, then execute, monitor, modify and conclude. Confidence: low. Sources: B64 p.58-59, 302-303

## Analysis and synthesis

- **Debrief right after every session and synthesize as you go.** Spend 10 minutes to an hour per session (or 2 minutes of silent notes), alternate collecting and sense-making so methods can adapt, and write insights as problems before ideating. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.441, 453; B96 p.73-74; B85 p.278-279; B72 p.25
- **Give every participant equal weight before clustering.** Summarize each person (about five bullets or three notes) so vivid sessions do not dominate; rate recordings to prioritize replay but listen to part of every one. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.135, 139-140; B30 p.142
- **Guard conclusions against confirmation bias and against the pull of memorable sessions.** Use a mixed team and more than one observer, invite challenge, and check each claim against the rest of the data; experimenter effects mostly favor the researcher's hypothesis. Confidence: high. Sources: B17 p.144-145; B06 p.95-96; B30 p.167; B03 p.253
- **Run affinity sorts with small teams and clear limits.** Fewer than 10 people, about 2 people per 100 to 150 notes over 1 to 1.5 days, one idea per note, groups of a few notes rising to 12 to 15, about three label levels, under 50 codes for most projects, and no more than 2 or 3 re-sorts. Confidence: medium. Sources: B20 p.180-188; B03 p.453, 457; B06 p.106-107; B17 p.138, 144; B50 p.49
- **Normalize statements before clustering.** Start each note with a verb, write in the first person, keep one idea per note and describe the person's intent, not the tool. Confidence: medium. Sources: B64 p.319, 323; B20 p.174-175
- **Cluster participants on behavioral variables and keep only causal patterns.** Plot each person by relative position on each behavioral axis; a group that clusters on about 6 to 8 of the typical 15 to 30 variables is a real pattern only if the shared behaviors connect logically, not by coincidence. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.113-114; B50 p.50-53; B01 p.16-17
- **Model workflows, artifacts and spaces alongside people.** Capture organizational process flows, the forms people use and the physical layout, and never turn a paper form directly into a screen. Confidence: low. Sources: B01 p.21-22
- **Park outliers instead of ignoring or chasing them.** Look for patterns, keep contradictory data in a parking lot and cross-check in other channels such as support email. Confidence: low. Sources: B34 p.127
- **Step away between absorbing data and finding patterns.** Sleep on it or take at least a short walk before clustering. Confidence: low. Sources: B17 p.145
- **Show how the evidence was gathered.** State who was heard, how many, how the analysis worked and what it cannot answer; transparency is what persuades skeptics when results contradict their beliefs. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.56, 96
- **Treat single studies and informal online polls as hypotheses.** Many published findings fail to replicate, so confirm striking results in controlled tests before building on them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B43 p.217-218; B19 p.134

## Inclusive and cross-cultural research

- **Research with the people who struggle most, screened by ability.** Designing around those with most difficulty tends to help everyone; screen by what people can do rather than by diagnosis, read a segment broadly, and build accessibility into the plan from day one, because tacking on a handful of older or disabled participants late changes nothing. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.172, 175; B32 p.74, 104-105; B91 p.122-124
- **Recruit disabled participants who reflect your real market, using specialist channels.** Work with recruiters who know disability and with local disability organizations; when comparing by characteristic, plan 12 or more people for each characteristic, and budget roughly 5 days of effort spread across 4 weeks to recruit a diverse group of 25. Confidence: medium. Sources: B91 p.122-124; B69 p.175-177
- **Localize screeners, tasks and data, not just the language.** Labels such as expert or frequent user are culturally relative; names, addresses and scenarios must be locally realistic so failures reflect the design; talk to someone with recent time in the region first. Confidence: low. Sources: B08 p.225-226, 229-230; B03 p.410
- **Sample very different markets and hold the observer constant.** Pick the most contrasting locations the budget allows, run them in sequence with the same key observer, and do the rest of the research domestically; culturally matched materials gave the lowest error rates. Confidence: medium. Sources: B08 p.17-18, 89-90, 217-218, 236-237
- **Treat national culture scores as rough group tendencies and favor behavioral data.** Culture indices are disputed and never apply to individuals; behavioral traces such as logs, recorded events and gaze need less interpretation, so they travel across cultures better than interviews, but they say nothing about satisfaction. Confidence: low. Sources: B49 p.41, 222-223, 283-284

## Research for AI and data systems

- **Audit training data for coverage, provenance and bias.** Data defines what a system can recognize and inherits the skews of its source corpora; specify core and edge cases and demographic spread up front, label with several coders and reconcile disagreements, and keep sensitive attributes available when you need to measure unequal outcomes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B65 p.105-107, 111-112; B66 p.140-141; B61 p.129, 149; B56 p.180
- **Validate against real behavior, not staged data.** Datasets of posed or acted examples do not represent natural behavior. Confidence: low. Sources: B65 p.169-170, 177-182
- **Keep evaluation data honest.** Separate and version training, regression and new data, rerun the same frozen regression set whenever anything changes, split correlated records by their grouping key, and route a defined random slice of users (for example 10 percent) to a new version before rollout. Confidence: medium. Sources: B63 p.144-146; B61 p.319-327, 376
- **Balance intent examples in conversational datasets.** Start with a few dozen phrasings per intent (five typed interviews can seed the set) and avoid hundreds-versus-ten imbalances that make small intents unreachable. Confidence: low. Sources: B63 p.138, 144, 262
- **Test AI systems with known cases, comparisons between versions, metamorphic relations and adversarial red teams.** Example of a metamorphic check: lowering a maximum price must return a subset of the earlier results. Confidence: medium. Sources: B21 p.171-172
- **Audit opaque models through their outputs and the people they score.** Feed varied profiles through the system, compare treatment, and ask those being scored whether the evaluation makes sense. Confidence: low. Sources: B56 p.172-174
- **Include people affected by an algorithm who never touch its interface.** Study their everyday practices and the folk theories they hold about how the system works, and gather real usage evidence before setting AI policy. Confidence: low. Sources: B45 p.15-17; B43 p.258
