# design-sprints

Input rows: 40. Merged rows: 15.

## When to sprint

- **Point a sprint at the biggest, riskiest open problem, scoped to one explorable area.** Sprints earn the team's full attention when stakes are high, a deadline is tight or a project is stuck. Skip them when the design is already validated, the work is only days long, more than one or two days of research is needed first, the scope is sweeping ("rethink marketing") or tiny ("fix the login page"), or the business case is unclear. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.31; B96 p.31-32; B96 p.42
- **Treat a sprint as an alignment and ideation tool, not a substitute for research.** A sprint compresses discovery into days, so it trims deep research; questions that need longer study belong in a research plan run before or after. Confidence: low. Sources: B29 p.71-73; B96 p.31-32
- **Settle the framing before sketching starts.** When a problem is contested or not yet framed, each person ends up drawing and evaluating toward a different problem; also avoid running a studio if leaders will not allow the design to stray far from a concept that already exists. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.120-121; B96 p.31-32

## Structure and timing

- **Run five days in a row by default, at full intensity.** Teams found shorter formats draining, while 10-day, month-long and six-week formats yielded nothing extra; stretching the sessions across weeks removes the time pressure that drives the sprint and drifts into a waterfall process. When time is short, a three-day compression is the minimum, with no less than one day for each phase. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.46; B96 p.13-14, 26, 35-36; B34 p.145-146
- **Cap the working day at about six focused hours with regular breaks.** Run roughly 10:00 to 17:00 with lunch at 13:00 and a 10-minute break every 60 to 90 minutes (never more than 120), devices out of the room; longer days or weekend work did not add output and increased attachment to ideas. Plan about 35 participant hours in total and keep backup agendas for exercises that go sideways. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.45-48, 96, 252; B96 p.41, 49, 117
- **Never drop the customer test.** Run understand, diverge, converge, prototype and test; a sprint without the test phase only produces opinions. Confidence: low. Sources: B96 p.24, 35; B81 p.151-153
- **Budget the understand phase explicitly.** A workable split is background 1.5 h, inspiration 1.5 h, problem 1 h and users 3 h; a journey map can take 15 minutes to 3 hours depending on depth. Use a visible timer in chunks from 3 minutes to an hour. Confidence: low. Sources: B96 p.53; B81 p.54

## People and roles

- **Keep the sprint team at seven or fewer, with mixed expertise.** Cover finance, marketing, customer, technology and design, include one constructive skeptic, and bring other experts in as short interviews on day one instead of adding seats; a 40-person first attempt nearly failed. Confidence: medium. Sources: B81 p.12, 38-41; B09 p.116
- **Appoint a Decider who remains present, plus a neutral Facilitator who is a different person.** A decision maker who skips the sprint may reverse a tested outcome later; when the Decider is unable to be there, have that authority handed over in writing. Time and process belong to the Facilitator, who stays impartial on every decision. Confidence: low. Sources: B81 p.35-37, 42
- **Focus on a single target customer and a single target moment.** On the map, the Decider picks the point carrying the most risk and the most opportunity, and that point should address one or more of the sprint questions. Confidence: low. Sources: B81 p.89-93

## Diverge and converge

- **Sketch alone before sharing, aim for quantity, then converge with critique before any vote.** Solo sketching avoids groupthink; split the journey into parts across ideation rounds; a fast divergent round is 6 to 8 ideas in 5 minutes. Turn critique off while generating, then compare, merge and eliminate against the stated objectives before dot voting. Confidence: medium. Sources: B96 p.80, 84-85; B09 p.112-114; B23 p.290
- **Structure a design studio as two or three rounds of sketching, presenting and critique, with groups of three to six.** Send out the problem, goals, personas and scenarios ahead of time. First round: 8 minutes sketching on 6-up paper, aiming for 5 or more ideas, then 3 minutes each to present and 4 to 5 minutes of critique. Second round: 8 minutes narrowing to one concept, with the same present and critique steps. Third round: 20 minutes building a team concept, 3 minutes to present it, and 7 minutes of critique from the other teams. Working alone, sketch 6 frames in 20 minutes, then spend 20 minutes on one bigger sketch; budget 30 minutes of setup and roughly 10 minutes per participant. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.115-119; B23 p.290, 292, 422
- **Limit the sketching tools to keep early ideas rough.** Thick black markers on small 6-up grids, with no pencils or erasers, prevent detail from arriving too early; reviewers highlight strong areas in green and weak ones in red. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.117-118
- **Put sketches on the wall while presenting.** A presenter holding a sketch in front of the body turns it into a shield between designer and critics; a wall lets the presenter stand with the team. Confidence: low. Sources: B09 p.118
- **Test conflicting ideas head to head instead of blending them.** When two strong directions compete, prototype both and test them under realistic made-up brand names rather than compromising into one weaker hybrid. Confidence: low. Sources: B81 p.151-153
