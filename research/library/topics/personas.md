## Foundation

- **Build personas from observed behavior in research, never from imagination.** A persona is a model of patterns seen in real interviews and observation; invented or assumption-only personas mislead decisions and lose the team's trust. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.99, 113-116; B03 p.498, 503; B02 p.453-454; B23 p.347-349, 357; B96 p.69-72; B72 p.46, 48, 56; B58 p.250-256, 262; B11 p.71-73; B78 p.22-24; B40 p.48; B57 p.61-62
- **Segment by behavior, needs and goals, not by demographics.** Group people by the outcome they are pursuing; keep only traits that predict product-relevant behavior (such as device access), since age and income rarely predict skill or needs; market segments can filter whom to interview but seldom map onto design personas. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.121; B03 p.498, 503; B34 p.61-62; B02 p.453-454; B35 p.62-66; B09 p.76-77; B85 p.45-46; B01 p.10-11
- **Derive personas by clustering people on behavioral variables.** Expect 15 to 30 variables per role; a group that clusters on 6 to 8 of them is a pattern, usually 2 or 3 per role (sometimes 1 for specialized roles); plot users on pairs of opposing variables and count how many fall in each group. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.113-114; B50 p.50-53; B17 p.164-165; B72 p.46, 48
- **Sample enough people per segment to see patterns.** Spend roughly 90 minutes with each participant in their own setting and cover each segment with 4 to 6 people (never fewer than 4 per type); a small project needs 15 or more interviews and a large one may need about 60, well above the handful used in a usability round. Confidence: medium. Sources: B58 p.239, 242, 262, 595, 599; B23 p.349; B26 p.75
- **Trace every persona detail back to data and keep a foundation document.** Nearly every statement should map to an observation or quote; link the description to the method, sample and analysis so readers can see it is not wishful thinking. Confidence: medium. Sources: B50 p.56, 83-84, 96, 171; B11 p.71-73; B01 p.7
- **Give every persona goals, or it is not a persona.** Infer goals from behavior and write each as one plain sentence; a typical persona has three to five outcome goals, at most one long-term aspiration and at most two goals about how the experience should feel; goals let you remove a task while still meeting the need. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.116, 121; B03 p.498, 503; B02 p.453-454; B50 p.64; B01 p.8, 11-13
- **Describe the persona only within the product's focus area.** Include the attitudes, context and habits that relate to the domain being designed and leave out unrelated preferences; that focus is what separates a persona from a market segment. Confidence: medium. Sources: B50 p.14, 16; B34 p.61-62; B02 p.453-454
- **Keep design personas apart from marketing personas.** Design personas describe how and where a product is used; marketing personas describe buying, brand and media behavior and rarely help product decisions. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.11, 125; B01 p.10-11
- **Create a persona set for each product, not one for the whole company.** A set seldom carries over to another product, even a closely related one, without fresh research; do not recycle a set from an earlier project. Confidence: medium. Sources: B69 p.162-164; B01 p.7-8
- **Validate and size personas with numbers.** After qualitative clustering, run a survey on a larger sample that asks about each behavioral variable two to four times in different wording, shuffled, and count how many people fall into each persona; managers trust personas more when sizes are shown. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.126; B50 p.50-53, 171; B72 p.49
- **Check persona traits against population data.** If the real customer base is 70 percent one group, the cast should roughly reflect that; verify any assumed trait against published statistics. Confidence: low. Sources: B23 p.349; B44 p.301

## Cast and priority

- **Keep the cast small: usually 3 to 6, rarely beyond 8.** Beyond about six, readers confuse personas with each other, and a cast above ten becomes impractical to design against; merge similar user types and drop personas that do not represent primary users. Confidence: medium. Sources: B50 p.19, 58, 170; B69 p.162, 164; B23 p.347, 349; B38 p.86-87; B58 p.239; B72 p.57, 66, 134; B52 p.65; B06 p.121-125
- **Give every interface a single primary persona.** The primary is the persona whose needs, once met, leave the others at least satisfied; if no single persona works, the product needs separate interfaces or its scope is too broad; more than 3 or 4 secondary personas signals the same problem. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.117-119; B03 p.516; B35 p.32, 70-71; B50 p.28, 58-60
- **Use the cast to decide priorities rather than to cover every user.** Shortlist 5 to 20 candidate groups, merge any whose design needs are identical, and agree 2 or 3 product-level primaries with the team; designing for everyone leaves no principled way to choose features. Confidence: medium. Sources: B17 p.164-165; B06 p.121-125; B35 p.32, 70-71; B38 p.86-87; B25 p.36
- **Make every persona differ by a meaningful behavior.** Personas separated only by age, gender or income should be combined; keep a detail only if it helps distinguish one persona from the rest, anchored on 3 to 5 attributes that separate them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B35 p.72-75; B64 p.111; B50 p.81-82; B01 p.18
- **Serve secondary personas with additions that do not hurt the primary.** A secondary persona gets a small number of additions layered onto the primary design; a supplemental persona needs nothing extra; people who influence adoption (managers, IT staff, form receivers) and people affected without using the product get shorter descriptions. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.28, 58-60, 80; B01 p.20-21
- **Optimize for the person who uses the product, not the person who buys it.** Buyers, reviewers and IT managers are usually not the users; note where user and payer differ (as in multi-sided or B2B2C products) and create a separate persona only when the buyer needs its own interface. Confidence: medium. Sources: B03 p.113-114; B96 p.69-72; B25 p.36; B01 p.10
- **Name who you are not designing for.** A negative or anti-persona (for example the tech-savvy early adopter in a consumer product) tells the team which edge cases and requests to drop, saving engineering and QA effort; outlier profiles also guard against false assumptions. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.46-47; B71 p.50-51; B01 p.21
- **Consider the most constrained user as the primary.** Designing for the person with the tightest limits often serves the bulk of the market, and a persona set that includes injury, arthritis or hearing loss surfaces concrete usability issues. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.117-119; B69 p.62, 163
- **Favor mainstream users over experts and early adopters.** Mainstream users want things to just work and abandon anything confusing or unreliable; what pleases them usually pleases experts too. Confidence: low. Sources: B06 p.69-70
- **Weight features by persona frequency.** Ask whether the primary persona would use an operation often, rarely or never, and rank features accordingly. Confidence: low. Sources: B11 p.71-73; B01 p.6
- **Inspect with the least sophisticated profile first.** That profile carries the most usability risk; when time is short, focus on frequent tasks and tasks with a high cost of error. Confidence: medium. Sources: B89 p.169; B28 p.184-186
- **Classify novices and experts by behavior, not self-rating.** Self-assessed skill is unreliable and time spent online is not proficiency; score the frequency of specific tasks (for example 0 to 4 per task) or run a short proficiency check, and set cut-offs from the scores. Confidence: low. Sources: B28 p.157-159, 217-219
- **Create separate personas for culturally different groups.** International products have no single homogeneous user; tag role and task models with the locale they describe. Confidence: low. Sources: B49 p.248-251
- **Model shifting shopping modes as roles, not fixed people.** One person moves between exploring, casual browsing, targeted buying and committed purchase within a session, so pages should support switching between modes. Confidence: low. Sources: B04 p.58-67

## Writing the description

- **Keep each persona to one page, two at most.** Short descriptions that state behaviors directly beat long biographies; roughly a paragraph covers one or two traits, and detail beyond the evidence suggests precision that is not there. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.120; B64 p.111; B50 p.82; B09 p.76; B23 p.347; B38 p.87-88; B01 p.19
- **Avoid stereotypes, averages and decorative trivia.** Describe distinct behavioral clusters, not an average user who matches nobody; names, cars and hobbies are narrative aids only; avoid gendered caricatures and negative category labels as names. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.121; B03 p.503, 509; B09 p.76-77; B50 p.66-68; B01 p.8-9
- **Cover the fields that drive design.** Environment, typical day or workflow, current tools, goals, needs, frustrations and obstacles, and technology profile, drawn as brief characteristics from observation. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.61-62; B35 p.72-75; B57 p.61-62; B38 p.87-88; B50 p.71-73; B01 p.17
- **Write a short third-person narrative grounded in the data.** Narrative is remembered better than bullets, a third-person narrator can note what the persona does not know, and first-person voices make personas blur together; keep it to a day-in-the-life sketch the research supports. Confidence: medium. Sources: B50 p.75, 110; B26 p.120; B01 p.19
- **Write rounded characters the reader can recognize.** Give at least two opposing traits and one peculiarity, plus appearance, background, temperament and relationship to the domain; abstract role keywords make readers fill gaps with stock characters, and more distance from the team's culture needs more context. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.66-75
- **Use one consistent template so personas are easy to tell apart.** Name, tagline, representative quote, keywords, distinct color and image on one page per persona. Confidence: medium. Sources: B50 p.81-82, 172; B35 p.72-75; B64 p.111-113; B38 p.87-88
- **Choose quotes and images with care; they steer how a persona is read.** A single comment or photo can make readers dismiss or caricature a persona; pick representative quotes, prefer realistic contextual photos over stock, beware ethnic cues that invite bias, and consider drawings that avoid cultural markers. Confidence: medium. Sources: B50 p.76-77, 83-84, 133, 155-160; B01 p.19
- **Keep personas as editable working sketches, not glossy posters.** A flip-chart sheet with name, sketch, facts, behaviors, needs and goals on movable notes stays a conversation starter that can change with research; hang it where the team works. Confidence: medium. Sources: B30 p.204-209; B64 p.111-113
- **Keep persona relationships out unless the research shows them.** Default to socially unrelated personas; link them as family or coworkers only when a shared workflow must be shown, or deliberately as a persona family in inclusive ideation workshops. Confidence: low. Sources: B69 p.62, 163; B01 p.17-18

## Using personas

- **Put personas to work in scenarios from day one.** A persona has no value until the team imagines it using the product; decide early how the cast will drive scenarios, stories and requirements, and tie each scenario to one goal. Confidence: medium. Sources: B50 p.18, 22, 86; B02 p.453-454; B78 p.25-28; B57 p.62-63
- **Stress-test designs by placing the persona in concrete situations.** Describe the setting, connection, time available, distractions and trigger, trace the path to the goal, and be honest about where the design fails (loops, dead ends). Confidence: medium. Sources: B57 p.62-63; B78 p.25-28
- **Keep product-specific usage out of the persona description.** Put current product use and task flows in scenarios so the same persona can enter many situations and outlive today's design. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.92, 172
- **Replace "the user" with a named persona in reviews.** A vague user can be bent to support any argument; naming a specific persona reveals reviewers who are imagining different people and grounds critique in agreed goals and scenarios. Confidence: medium. Sources: B09 p.74-76; B38 p.86; B71 p.50-51; B01 p.6
- **Do not design for yourself or for everyone.** Designers and engineers assume users think and know what they do; a persona keeps the audience separate from the team, and broadening to please every group adds load for all of them. Confidence: medium. Sources: B85 p.45-46; B06 p.73, 86; B01 p.4-6
- **Treat a persona as a model, never as a substitute for testing.** Recruit test participants from the same segments; if two sub-groups of one persona both struggle in different ways, split it. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.56-57; B23 p.357
- **Start with proto-personas only as labeled hypotheses, then validate them.** Sketch 3 or 4 assumed personas in hours (a four-quadrant sheet works), mark them as assumptions, and check in the field whether those people exist and have those needs; unvalidated proto-personas tend to linger and carry little authority. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.59-63; B64 p.111-113; B11 p.71-73; B50 p.80, 125-126; B58 p.262
- **Treat personas as living documents.** Revisit them after each study and run a full check at least every one to two years; research can overturn even basic traits such as the dominant gender or age of the audience. Confidence: medium. Sources: B34 p.61, 64; B50 p.22, 57-58, 166; B17 p.164-165; B30 p.204-209
- **Prepare for resistance when personas contradict beliefs.** Involve marketing and management early, show the data and the method, and argue with reasoning they already accept. Confidence: low. Sources: B50 p.56-58, 95-96
- **Plan persona work as a short, staged project.** Allow about one week each to recruit, run fieldwork and analyze, plus a week of contingency; run synthesis workshops with 5 to 8 people, summarizing each participant in a few notes of 3 to 4 bullets; expect around 15 tagged observations per interview. Confidence: medium. Sources: B58 p.238, 241, 250-255, 596; B03 p.500, 504; B17 p.139-140
- **Limit each journey map to one or two personas.** A generic all-purpose persona will not work; make several focused maps, one per persona and goal. Confidence: low. Sources: B67 p.16, 34
