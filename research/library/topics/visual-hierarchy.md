# visual-hierarchy

- **Make prominence follow importance.** The most important element should be the largest, highest, boldest or most isolated, and the real message must outrank decoration; related items should look related and child items should sit visibly inside their parent. Confidence: high. Sources: B77 p.44-47; B86 p.211-215; B10 p.174-175; B39 p.34-35; B91 p.69; B76 p.70
- **Build emphasis by turning other things down.** Rank elements as instant, secondary and exceptional, then dim or shrink the competitors rather than boosting the key item; when everything is loud nothing leads. Confidence: high. Sources: B26 p.443-444; B47 p.46-47; B92 p.70-71; B77 p.49-50; B46 p.191-194
- **Limit focal points and emphasis techniques.** Too many highlighted items, or too many emphasis methods at once, cancel each other and lower comprehension; keep one clear focus per view and a small number of secondary ones. Confidence: high. Sources: B86 p.211-215, 221; B46 p.191-194; B02 p.136; B44 p.217-218; B05 p.284, 287
- **Give the primary action the strongest contrast on the screen.** A call to action stands out by contrast with its surroundings (a warm fill on a cool page, a filled button on a dark band), not by a magic hue; one measured test of a higher-contrast button raised signups by about 31 percent. Shrink or quiet the options you do not want chosen. Confidence: high. Sources: B54 p.132-135; B86 p.396-399; B27 p.181, 188; B78 p.40-41; B44 p.217-218
- **Combine size, weight, color, space and position rather than leaning on size.** Each cue sets a basic order but combinations read most clearly; relying on size by itself pushes primary text too large and secondary text too small, so soften the color or add weight instead. Confidence: high. Sources: B92 p.70; B47 p.38-39; B57 p.83; B19 p.41; B52 p.206-207
- **Make every element identifiable without reading it.** Titles, captions, data, headings, controls and navigation should be recognizable by size, weight, position or style alone, the way a headline is found on a newspaper page. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.161; B77 p.32-34
- **Design pages for scanning.** People glance and hunt for words that match their task, so pages must work at a glance; on text-heavy pages lead paragraphs, subheads and bullets with information-carrying words. Confidence: medium. Sources: B77 p.32-34; B78 p.34-35
- **Check hierarchy with a squint or blur test.** Blur a screenshot by about 5 to 10px or squint at it; whatever still stands out is the real hierarchy, and it should be the key content and actions. Confidence: medium. Sources: B26 p.443-444; B78 p.44-45
- **Settle value structure in grayscale before adding color.** Light and dark contrast carries composition more than hue; a layout that fails in grayscale will fail in color. Group tones into about two to four main value planes. Confidence: medium. Sources: B68 p.23; B70 p.43-45; B53 p.88, 315
- **Keep saturated color to small accent areas over a muted field.** A small patch of strong color in a large quiet area looks more intense and draws the eye, while a hue unrelated to the scheme jumps forward and fights the focal point. Confidence: medium. Sources: B70 p.63; B68 p.95, 126, 150-153; B53 p.445; B37 p.238-240
- **Balance area against lightness.** Light, strong hues need less area than dark ones to hold equal weight (yellow to violet about 1 to 3, red to green about 1 to 1), and a large low-contrast element can be balanced by a small high-contrast one. Confidence: medium. Sources: B70 p.59-61; B68 p.104, 110-111; B10 p.122-124
- **Keep a heading closer to its own section than to the content above.** Space inside a heading block must be smaller than the space after it, and each heading level must differ clearly in size or space above. Confidence: medium. Sources: B77 p.51, 202; B88 p.141, 226
- **Introduce hierarchy variables one by one.** Begin with a single size and style, then bring in space, weight, scale and lastly color, so structure emerges without piling on typefaces. Confidence: medium. Sources: B88 p.160-161; B46 p.191-194
- **Avoid uniform sizing.** When everything is the same scale the page has no rhythm or reading order; use progressive scale steps, and plan bigger scale contrast for energy and smaller for calm. Confidence: medium. Sources: B92 p.70-71, 144; B10 p.139-143, 177-178
- **Remove anything that does not carry meaning.** Purposeless lines, dots, patterns, gradients and shadows lower the signal-to-noise ratio; treat each element as noise until it proves its value. Confidence: medium. Sources: B77 p.49-50; B71 p.64, 138; B64 p.172-173; B05 p.201-202
- **Give primary content center stage.** Make the main area no less than double the width of the side margins and let it fill most of the first screen; do not let decorative graphics push the tasks people came for below the fold. Confidence: medium. Sources: B86 p.232; B58 p.98, 111
- **Treat visual polish as part of usability.** People judge a page's look within about 50 ms, attractive interfaces are perceived as easier and produce fewer errors, and sloppy details erode trust; still fix utility and usability first and apply visual treatment on top. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.44-49; B11 p.102; B66 p.127-128, 134
- **Style consistently, because style signals meaning.** Things that look alike are read as the same kind of thing, so one differently styled item implies a different role or importance. Confidence: medium. Sources: B44 p.98; B62 p.86-87; B64 p.172-173
- **Place critical details beside the element users fixate on.** Shoppers focused on price miss the departure time beside it if it is far away; put vital facts next to the focal point. Confidence: low. Sources: B44 p.101
- **Show only the controls the current task needs.** Surface a tool when its task starts and hide it when the task ends, rather than drawing attention to every function at once. Confidence: medium. Sources: B06 p.69-71; B26 p.443-444
- **Keep critical information on screen, not in memory.** Working memory holds only about three to five items and is wiped by interruptions, so do not rely on transient messages for things users must act on. Confidence: medium. Sources: B79 p.103-104; B41 p.36, 53
- **Use soft edges for connection and hard edges for separation.** Soft boundaries read as near and related, hard ones as distant and separate; reserve emphasized edges for places that must split apart. Confidence: low. Sources: B90 p.16-17, 57-58
- **Separate with background, shadow or space before adding borders.** Too many borders clutter; a slight background change, a shadow or extra space usually groups better. Keep line styles to about three thicknesses and two styles per screen. Confidence: medium. Sources: B47 p.238-241; B46 p.187-189; B64 p.175-176
- **Use a fixed elevation scale of about five shadows.** Small for buttons, medium for menus, large for dialogs; a two-part shadow pairs a soft ambient blur with a tight contact shadow that fades at higher elevation. Confidence: low. Sources: B47 p.180-189
- **Lower the contrast of heavy icons next to text.** Solid icons outweigh adjacent text, so give them a softer color; thicken faint borders rather than darkening them. Confidence: low. Sources: B47 p.56-59
- **Merge labels into values where context allows.** Plain label and value pairs flatten displayed data; write the value with its context or demote the label. Confidence: low. Sources: B47 p.48-51
- **Choose a reading path for the page type.** Text-heavy pages follow an F-shaped scan, so put the hook in the first two paragraphs; simple action pages follow a Z, ending at the primary action. Confidence: low. Sources: B78 p.34-38
- **Lead the eye through a planned sequence.** Use scale, bold headlines, repetition and rhythm to fix the order in which elements are seen, and start any motion where the eye already is. Confidence: medium. Sources: B10 p.125; B42 p.110-115; B09 p.80
- **Reserve motion for the content that matters most.** Animating only the key element keeps attention there; exaggerated motion is justified only for important prompts such as unsaved work. Confidence: low. Sources: B42 p.107-110
- **Expect light shapes on dark grounds to look larger.** The same element appears bigger reversed out, so equal sizes in light and dark themes will not read as equal. Confidence: low. Sources: B70 p.18, 87
- **Use depth cues deliberately.** Larger, lower, overlapping, warmer and sharper elements read as nearer; lighter, lower-contrast, softer and cooler ones recede. Confidence: low. Sources: B10 p.164-165; B70 p.91
- **Break an established treatment to mark the exception.** Once a standard position exists, outdenting or rotating one element sets it apart. Confidence: low. Sources: B92 p.78
- **Prefer low complexity, high unity and a familiar form with one fresh detail.** Typical overall forms with a novel element are liked most, and visual complexity lowers perceived ease of use. Confidence: medium. Sources: B72 p.327-328; B71 p.64
- **Use simple distinguishing shapes for icon triggers.** Visual search narrows to a tiny field and finds a single distinct feature faster than a combination of features. Confidence: low. Sources: B14 p.50
- **Keep test prototypes near-monochrome.** Color only actions and alerts so styling does not confound tests of structure and labels. Confidence: low. Sources: B74 p.180, 185
