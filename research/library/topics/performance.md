# performance

- **Design to the three response-time limits.** About 0.1 s feels instant and is the ceiling for direct manipulation, about 1 s keeps the train of thought unbroken, and about 10 s is the limit of attention; write these into requirements instead of a vague speed goal. Confidence: high. Sources: B26 p.295-296; B86 p.409-410; B11 p.102-103; B46 p.623-624; B82 p.115
- **Pick loading feedback by the length of the wait.** Show nothing under about 200 ms so no indicator flickers; from 200 ms to about 2 s show an indeterminate indicator; past about 2 s add progress, expected length and a cancel; past about 5 s add a description and percent done; past about 10 s let the work continue in the background and announce completion. Confidence: high. Sources: B52 p.264; B86 p.409-410, 415; B26 p.452, 662; B46 p.627-629
- **Say what the system is doing, not just that it is busy.** Step messages such as connecting, or step 1 of 4, make the same wait feel shorter and stop complaints without any speed change. Confidence: high. Sources: B52 p.261, 326; B42 p.152-156; B82 p.97-98; B86 p.409-410; B24 p.312-314
- **Choose the wait indicator by what you can predict.** Use a skeleton when the structure of incoming content is known, a spinner for short waits of unknown shape, and a determinate bar only when the estimate is reliable, because a stalled bar is worse than none; generic spinners make an app feel slower than content-first placeholders. Confidence: high. Sources: B82 p.116-117; B24 p.312-314; B42 p.152-156; B52 p.276-277; B85 p.166
- **Show results as they arrive.** Render the top of the page first, stream partial results, explain long waits and let people keep working while the rest loads; incremental display raises tolerance for long loads a great deal. Confidence: high. Sources: B82 p.97-98, 104; B46 p.623-626; B58 p.412-414
- **In headsets, treat a dropped frame as a comfort bug.** Lock rendering to the display's native refresh, keep motion-to-photon latency near 20 ms and never past about 60 ms, and drop render resolution before dropping frames (details in xr-3d). Confidence: high. Sources: B36 p.98-99, 157-170; B48 p.61, 151-152, 162-163; B15 p.12-13, 79; B83 p.625-626
- **Treat load time as a retention problem.** More than half of mobile visitors leave a page that has not loaded in about 3 seconds, and people remember a site as slower than it was (about 15 percent slower while using it, 35 percent afterward); test on slow networks, not office wifi. Confidence: medium. Sources: B44 p.72-74; B84 p.60; B91 p.78
- **Aim for responses under about 400 ms in productive work.** Below that point (the Doherty threshold) people stay in flow, and creative tools gain from feedback in the 0.4 to 0.9 s range or faster. Confidence: medium. Sources: B11 p.102-103; B46 p.623-624
- **Keep response times consistent.** Variation annoys more than length: hold deviation under half the mean, ideally under 20 percent, and treat fluctuating frame rates as a defect in their own right. Confidence: medium. Sources: B46 p.623-624; B83 p.625-626
- **Follow strict rules for progress bars.** Pair the bar with text, fill it once, never move it backward, advance in small even steps, reach 100 percent only when finished, and measure completed work rather than guessed time. Confidence: medium. Sources: B52 p.262-263; B82 p.116-117
- **Reserve space for media before it loads.** Send image dimensions ahead of the file so content does not jump, and fill the reserved box with a dominant-color placeholder so the wait looks intentional. Confidence: medium. Sources: B52 p.222, 282
- **Animate only cheap properties.** Position, scale and rotation through transform, plus opacity, cost the browser least; measure the expense before animating any other property, and reach for plain CSS instead of a heavy library on small effects. Confidence: medium. Sources: B42 p.77-79
- **Assume the connection will drop.** Keep entered data across drops, cut page weight and image size, drop heavy third-party scripts, and retry with exponential backoff (wait times 2 to the power of the attempt) for up to three attempts before reporting a failure. Confidence: medium. Sources: B40 p.62; B52 p.313, 322; B06 p.300-301
- **Make waits feel shorter by design.** Slow cyclic indicators tire people less than fast ones because people count cycles, and progress effects that seem to accelerate make the same download feel about 10 percent quicker. Confidence: medium. Sources: B54 p.46-47
- **Use optimistic updates only for low-stakes actions.** Likes and comments can show success at once and retry quietly; payments and time-critical actions confirm with the server first. Confidence: low. Sources: B52 p.265-266
- **Never add an artificial delay to show a splash.** Fast loads with placeholders beat any static logo screen; even a 5 s startup is judged too long. Confidence: low. Sources: B52 p.255, 276-277
- **Shrink the loader once real content appears.** A large centered indicator suits an empty area, but once text or reasoning is streaming, switch to a quiet status line and collapse intermediate output. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.98-99
- **Match the latency strategy to the task.** Real-time control and transactions must be fast, creative and chat tasks can be engaging while they work, and search, payments and learning must above all be clear about state. Confidence: low. Sources: B82 p.116
- **Set speed budgets per connection type.** Measure with a lab tool and hold separate targets, for example speed index under 2.5 s on desktop broadband and under 5 s on a mobile 3G profile. Confidence: low. Sources: B59 p.15
- **Prefetch from observed behavior, not everywhere.** Eager-load the few results most people open (the top three), load about twice a screenful as the first batch, and prefetch further pages only for the share of users who actually continue. Confidence: low. Sources: B52 p.293, 297-298, 301
- **Do no work when nothing changes.** Repainting at 60 fps on an idle screen and polling every few seconds for empty results waste battery and data; push or back off instead. Confidence: low. Sources: B52 p.41, 325; B14 p.82
- **Precompute expensive results.** Batch heavy calculations such as recommendations and refresh them on a schedule so a visit only reads stored results. Confidence: low. Sources: B61 p.262, 270-271
- **Judge smoothness as people perceive it.** Users do not count frames; 60 fps is a test benchmark, and a choppy or late response matters more than the exact number. Confidence: low. Sources: B42 p.79, 145
- **Measure load time where users are, top of page first.** Server logs can look fine while users suffer; time real sessions on users' machines, correct for connection variance with a fixed reference page, and find which elements are slow. Confidence: low. Sources: B58 p.412-414, 642-644
- **Weigh visual richness against load cost before promising it.** Every image, animation and script adds wait; confirm feasibility with engineering first, and when a size budget forces heavy compression, redesign the asset rather than ship unreadable artifacts. Confidence: low. Sources: B85 p.178-180; B95 p.86
- **Perceived speed depends on layout, not only latency.** A calmer results page can feel faster than a busy one running the same engine. Confidence: low. Sources: B33 p.46-47
- **Choose hardware after the interaction is designed.** A processor picked before UI work may be unable to keep up with fast scrolling, forcing artificial friction later. Confidence: low. Sources: B72 p.380
- **Keep most tasks on one input device.** Moving a hand from keyboard to pointer costs about 1.5 to 2 seconds per switch, so design so that most tasks need only one device. Confidence: low. Sources: B46 p.465, 468
